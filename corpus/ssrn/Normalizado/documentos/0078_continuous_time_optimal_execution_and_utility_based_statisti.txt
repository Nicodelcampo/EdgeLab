Abstract
This paper examines a continuous-time execution problem for a risk-averse large trader in
the presence of temporary, permanent, and transient market impacts as well as an exogenous,
stochastic factor that aﬀects the ﬁnancial asset price. The stochastic factor, which we refer to
as the Markovian environment, is described by a generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process.
The problem is formulated as a continuous-time stochastic control problem over a ﬁnite horizon.
By analyzing the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, we derive an optimal
execution strategy characterized by an aﬃne function of the state variables with time-dependent
coeﬃcients. The time-dependent coeﬃcients are determined by a solution to a system of numerically tractable ordinary diﬀerential equations (ODEs) with terminal conditions, consisting
of (i) a matrix Riccati diﬀerential equation (MRDE), (ii) a vector-valued linear ODE, and (iii) a
one-dimensional ODE. Under mild conditions, we show that the MRDE admits a unique solution
and establish a veriﬁcation theorem. Simulation-based numerical experiments illustrate that the
Markovian environment signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the optimal execution strategy. Speciﬁcally, we
numerically observe the possibility of a utility-based statistical arbitrage via a round-trip trading.
Keywords: Optimal execution; Markovian environment; Transient market impact; HJB equation; Matrix Riccati diﬀerential equation; Utility-based statistical arbitrage

Introduction
Optimal execution problems, ﬁrst investigated in the seminal works of Bertsimas and Lo [7] and
Almgren and Chriss [3], have attracted widespread interest over the past two decades. In ﬁnancial
markets, an institutional trader or a large trader (e.g., pension funds, life insurance companies)
executes large orders, moving the ﬁnancial asset price in an unfavorable direction.
The eﬀect
caused by the large trader on the price is called market impact (or price impact). The large trader
is then required to manage two types of risk: the liquidity risk arising from the large trader’s order
submission and the risk of future price ﬂuctuation. The problems faced by large traders are referred
to as execution problems.
Readers may refer to, e.g., Cartea et al. [13], Gu´eant [38], Laruelle
1

and Lehalle [44], and Shimoshimizu [63] for a wide range of diﬀerent conﬁgurations of execution
problems.
As for the market impacts, the temporary, permanent, and transient market impacts are among
the most discussed types. A temporary market impact refers to the part of the market impact
that fades away immediately after the execution, while a permanent market impact is another part
of the market impact that remains in the rest of the trading window. In contrast, the transient
market impact, recognized as a striking aspect of market impacts, is a type of market impact that
decays over the trading window and is empirically conﬁrmed (Bacry et al. [5]; Bouchaud et al. [9];
Gatheral [33]). The transiency of the market impact indicates that a shortage of liquidity caused by
a large trader’s execution results in an imbalance between buy and sell orders in a ﬁnancial market.
There is considerable literature on execution problems in the presence of transient market impact
(e.g., Gatheral et al. [35]; Kuno and Ohnishi [42]; Kuno et al. [43]; Schied and Zhang [61]; Tsoukalas
et al. [67]; Luo and Schied [46]; Ohnishi and Shimoshimizu [53, 54, 55, 56]; Fukasawa et al. [30, 31];
Neuman and Voß [50, 51]; Cordoni and Lillo [20, 21]; Ackermann et al. [1], to mention only a few).
In addition to market impacts, other exogenous, stochastic factors often have a detrimental
eﬀect on the market price of a ﬁnancial asset. These factors capture environmental uncertainty. For
example, existing empirical and theoretical studies demonstrate that the order ﬂow of small traders
incurs a price impact (Potters and Bouchaud [58]; Cartea and Jaimungal [11, 12]) and inﬂuences
an (optimal) execution strategy (Cartea and Jaimungal [11, 12]; Fukasawa et al. [30, 31]; Ohnishi
and Shimoshimizu [54, 55, 56]).1 Another example of such an exogenous factor is the order book
imbalance (OBI), which refers to the diﬀerence between the volume at the best bid and that at
the best ask, divided by its sum, and has a mean-reversion property (Lehalle and Neuman [45]).
Existing studies show that OBI causes price ﬂuctuation (e.g., Cont et al. [18]; Stoikov [65]) and thus
inﬂuences an (optimal) execution strategy (Lehalle and Neuman [45]). These examples indicate that
an exogenous, stochastic factor can aﬀect the ﬁnancial asset price and consequently inﬂuence the
trade performance of a large trader.
Based on the background described so far, we examine an optimal execution problem for a riskaverse large trader in the presence of market impacts and an exogenous, stochastic factor, both of
which aﬀect the ﬁnancial asset price. The large trader is assumed to have a constant absolute riskaversion (CARA) von Neumann–Morgenstern (vN-M) utility function. We consider a generalized
market impact model by assuming that temporary, permanent, and transient market impacts exist
and an exogenous, stochastic factor modeled by a generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process.
The main objective of this paper is to analyze how a risk-averse large trader, taking into account the
exogenous, stochastic factor, optimally executes her orders. In this paper, we refer to the exogenous,
stochastic factor as the Markovian environment.
By formulating the execution problem as a stochastic control problem and examining the corresponding Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, we explicitly obtain an optimal execution
strategy as an aﬃne function of the state variables, including the Markovian environment. The
veriﬁcation theorem is also provided by restricting the class of admissible strategies. Our numerical experiments also reveal that the Markovian environment signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the optimal
execution strategy. For example, if environmental uncertainty is high, the large trader accelerates
her execution to avoid the risk of future price ﬂuctuation. A similar behavior is conﬁrmed in the
case of high terminal execution cost per unit because the large trader avoids paying a high terminal
cost. We also ﬁnd that if a risk-averse large trader has an initial inventory at some level, there is
a possibility of a utility-based statistical arbitrage via a round-trip trading, under which the large
trader can increase her expected utility.
This paper contributes to the existing literature from ﬁnancial viewpoints as follows. First, we
explicitly derive an optimal execution strategy that incorporates the Markovian environment. The
explicit execution strategy implies that a large trader should incorporate a short-term predictive
feature (e.g., OBI) into her execution strategy. Furthermore, our numerical examples quantify how

environmental uncertainty, captured by the diﬀusion coeﬃcient of the Markovian environment, inﬂuences the optimal execution strategy. We observe that the optimal execution strategy becomes
adaptive to the diﬀerent realized values of the Markovian environment or environmental uncertainty. Besides, we deﬁne a new concept of statistical arbitrage, utility-based statistical arbitrage.
Intuitively, a ﬁnancial market admits a utility-based statistical arbitrage for a large trader with
CARA-type utility if there exists a trading strategy under which the sign of the initial inventory
and that of a trading speed at some time diﬀer, and by which the large trader can increase her
expected utility. Deﬁning a notion of statistical arbitrage from an expected utility maximization is
important from the following viewpoints: (i) the notion is often considered in the framework of an
expected cost minimization in the literature; (ii) an expected utility maximization and an expected
cost minimization are essentially diﬀerent. Much of the existing literature has focused on an optimal execution problem from the viewpoint of expected cost minimization with running inventory
costs and analyzed the existence of transaction-triggered price manipulation or (statistical) arbitrage. Unlike transaction-triggered price manipulation deﬁned in a cost-minimization framework,
our notion is rooted in expected utility maximization. It is therefore valuable to provide a rigorous
deﬁnition of utility-based statistical arbitrage.
Several papers are closely related to our paper, and we need to clarify the diﬀerences. Cartea
and Jaimungal [12] study an optimal execution problem with the eﬀect of the order-ﬂow from other
(small) traders under a temporary and permanent market impact model. Lehalle and Neuman [45]
then analyze a similar problem with transient market impacts. Neuman and Voß [50, 51] also extend these studies and examine an optimal execution problem with both transient and temporary
market impacts. Our model diﬀers from these studies in several aspects. First, we consider an
expected exponential-utility maximization problem. However, the joint eﬀect of temporary, permanent, and transient market impacts and an exogenous, stochastic factor under a CARA utility
criterion has not been fully understood. Second, we include a permanent impact and a transient
impact simultaneously, although most literature considers one of the market impacts. Moreover,
we tackle a relevant issue of proving the existence and uniqueness of the solution to a diﬀerential
equation arising from our optimal execution problem. Some studies (e.g., Ma et al. [47], Bergault
et al. [8]) analyze this point, which arises from an execution problem. Due to the permanent and
transient market impacts in our problem, proving the existence of a unique solution to the ordinary
diﬀerential equation (ODE) involving the HJB equation becomes diﬃcult.
From a mathematical viewpoint, we contribute to the literature by overcoming this diﬃculty.
Our theorem (Theorem 3.1) shows a suﬃcient condition under which a unique solution to a matrix
Riccati diﬀerential equation (MRDE) arising from our stochastic control problem exists. Speciﬁcally,
we consider the following type of MRDE:

This paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, we construct a market model in which the generalized market impact is characterized. We solve an expected utility maximization problem for
a risk-averse large trader and show an optimal execution strategy, an existence and uniqueness
theorem regarding an MRDE, and a veriﬁcation theorem in Section 3. Section 4 is devoted to
conducting numerical experiments to see how the Markovian environment inﬂuences the optimal
execution strategy. Finally, Section 5 concludes. All proofs are shown in the appendices.

environment aﬀects, in a linear functional form, the drift term of the price process of the ﬁnancial
asset. We assume that it follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type, Gaussian, and Markovian process.

Remark 2.4 (Related literature and the diﬀerences). There are some papers closely related to our
analysis. Cartea and Jaimungal [12] or Cont et al. [19] investigate an optimal execution problem in
the presence of temporary and permanent market impacts. Lehalle and Neuman [45] and Neuman
and Voß [50, 51] consider the eﬀect of market microstructure signal on the risky asset price in the
presence of transient market impacts. Our market model is similar to those papers, but some diﬀerences exist between their models and our model. First, as explicitly stated in the next section, our
model considers an expected CARA utility maximization problem, although the aforementioned
papers study an expected cost minimization problem. Examining the execution problem from an
expected utility maximization enables us to explicitly model the large trader’s risk aversion, in
contrast to formulations based on quadratic running inventory costs. Second, our problem includes
temporary, permanent, and transient market impacts. Lehalle and Neuman [45], and Neuman and
Voß [50, 51] do not consider the permanent market impact term as (10). Also, Cartea and Jaimungal [12] and Cont et al. [19] do not include the transient part of the market impact, although the
transient market impact plays a key role in our analysis. Our analysis sheds light on an optimal
execution problem in a uniﬁed framework incorporating temporary, permanent, and transient market impacts together with the large trader’s risk aversion. As a related problem, Bergault et al. [8]
examine an optimal execution problem when the price dynamics of multiple risky assets are described by a multidimensional OU process. Drissi [24] studies an optimal market-making problem

Remark 3.5. The optimal execution speed expressed by Eq. (60) implies that the optimal trading
strategy is adaptive to diﬀerent realizations of the Markovian environment. Lehalle and Neuman [45]
show that when considering the transient market impact, the optimal trading strategy only depends
on the initial value of the microstructural signal (or Markovian environment in our terminology) and
becomes deterministic and non-randomized. Contrary to that, the model considered in this paper
allows us to derive the optimal execution strategy for a transient market impact model with the
eﬀect of the Markovian environment on price dynamics. In the context of signal-adaptive optimal
execution strategy, Cartea and Jaimungal [12] and Neuman and Voß [50, 51] derive an optimal
execution strategy adaptive to the microstructural signal. Our analysis diﬀers from theirs in terms of
the problem setting: They examine an expected cost minimization problem with quadratic inventory
costs, while we analyze an expected utility maximization problem.

4.1
Comparative Statics for Coeﬃcients
We ﬁrst conduct comparative statics for the coeﬃcients of state variables in the optimal execution
speed (i.e., at, bt, ct, and dt). In particular, we focus on how these coeﬃcients are inﬂuenced by the
parameters deﬁning the Markovian environment (bI, cI, and σI) and execution cost at the terminal
(χT ). This analysis allows us to see how a large trader incorporates state variables into the optimal
execution strategy with the eﬀect of the Markovian environment.
Figures 1 to 4 illustrate the
comparative statics for the coeﬃcients with diﬀerent values of the parameters.
From Figures 1 to 4, we ﬁnd that the coeﬃcient of residual eﬀect of past market impacts (ct)
is negative under our parameter settings. The residual eﬀect is the discounted sum of the past
market impacts caused by the large trader. Thus, the negativity of ct implies that a large trader
delays the execution in accordance with the degree to which she has incurred the market impacts.
This result reﬂects the aspect that, at least in part, the more the large trader has caused market
impacts, the more unfavorable directions the future price moves. Thus, the large trader slows down
the execution if she poses large (split) orders before some trading time. Also, all of the coeﬃcients
are inﬂuenced by the uncertainty driven by the Markovian environment (σI) and the cost at the
end of the trading horizon (χT ). We observe other characteristics of the coeﬃcients (at, bt, ct, and
dt) as follows:
• The state-independent term (at) tends to decrease as environmental uncertainty (σI) increases.
This result implies that the large trader tends to avoid the loss from price ﬂuctuation driven
by environmental uncertainty. In addition, as the mean-reversion level (cI) increases, the
coeﬃcient increases more sharply, and the feature is remarkable at the early trading window.
When the mean-reversion level is positive, the Markovian environment tends to push up the
market price, which is undesirable for the large trader with a positive initial inventory. Therefore, our result implies that the large trader with positive initial inventory should liquidate her
orders as fast as possible when the mean-reversion level is positively high, which is consistent
with our intuition.

5
Conclusion
We have examined an optimal execution problem for a single large trader in a ﬁnite continuoustime framework. The large trader maximizes the expected CARA utility arising from her terminal
wealth in a ﬁnancial market.
We formulated a generalized market impact model wherein both
market impacts and a Markovian environment, capturing an exogenous, stochastic factor, aﬀect the
ﬁnancial asset price. Based on the dynamic programming principle, we derived an optimal execution
strategy in closed form, characterized by an aﬃne function of the state variables. Our theoretical
results show that the Markovian environment aﬀects the optimal execution strategy. Our numerical
results also provide the following insight: Uncertainty in the Markovian environment signiﬁcantly
aﬀects the optimal execution strategy and can lead to a utility-based statistical arbitrage through
a round-trip trading.
We also show the existence and uniqueness of the solution to an MRDE
under some mild conditions. Our framework will be explored from a more in-depth and extensive
perspective.
There would be room for extending our analysis as a stochastic diﬀerential game of an execution
problem played by multiple large traders. The extension would allow for strategic interactions among
multiple large traders who jointly inﬂuence execution prices. Other directions include multi-asset
execution problems or models with jump risk in either the asset price dynamics or the Markovian
environment.
