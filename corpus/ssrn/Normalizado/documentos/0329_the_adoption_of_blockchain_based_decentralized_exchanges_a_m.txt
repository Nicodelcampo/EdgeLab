Liquidity Provision on Blockchain-Based Decentralized
Exchanges ∗
Agostino Capponi†and Ruizhe Jia‡
Abstract
We show that the infrastructure of decentralized exchanges (DEXs) subjects liquidity providers (LPs) to a tragedy of the commons. LPs lack incentives to exit liquidity
pools to prevent arbitrage losses, as these are collectively shared, while withdrawal
costs are borne individually. Arbitrage rents primarily flow to validators as infrastructure fees, with the median arbitrageur transferring 96% of profits. Proposed solutions—speed technology, alternative sequencing rules, and flexible pricing curves—fail
to reduce these rents. Leveraging the Silicon Valley Bank crisis as a natural experiment, we show that LPs defensively adopt more convex pricing curves to mitigate
adverse selection risks.

Introduction
The advent of Bitcoin in 2008 has sparked extensive academic discourse around its underlying blockchain technology (Campbell (2016), Yermack (2017), Cong and He (2019), Chiu
and Koeppl (2019), Gan, Tsoukalas, and Netessine (2021)). While initially serving as a decentralized payment infrastructure, blockchain’s capability expanded significantly with the
introduction of smart contracts in 2015. These self-executing contracts enabled the emergence of decentralized finance (DeFi) - automated financial services that operate without
traditional intermediaries. In their book “DeFi and the Future of Finance”, Harvey, Ramachandran, and Santoro (2021) present an ambitious vision:
“We believe that DeFi will replace all meaningful centralized financial infrastructure in the future.”
This vision stems from a fundamental premise: blockchain-based finance, through its inherent
decentralization and transparency, should reduce costs and rent extraction compared to
traditional financial systems.
Traditional centralized exchanges (CEXs) face well-documented inefficiencies.
Highfrequency trading firms (HFTs) extract significant arbitrage rents through speed advantages,
while exchanges capitalize on infrastructure rents by selling preferential access through colocation services (see Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015); Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019);
Brolley and Zoican (2023a)). Moreover, these centralized venues have demonstrated reluctance to adopt more efficient market designs that might reduce infrastructure rents.
Blockchain-based decentralized exchanges (DEXs), now representing roughly 20% of
global spot cryptocurrency trading volume (The Block (2024)), aim to address these challenges. Unlike CEXs that utilize traditional limit order books, most DEXs employ automated
market makers (AMMs)—smart contracts that eliminate the need for active market makers. AMMs operate on predetermined pricing algorithms that facilitate automatic quoting
and risk sharing (Malinova and Park (2023)), allowing even retail investors to participate in
2

liquidity provision.
In this paper, we argue that, contrary to the idealized vision, the current infrastructure design of DEXs imposes significant costs on liquidity provision—costs that can surpass
those encountered in traditional markets. Specifically, we demonstrate that the integration
of blockchain order execution with existing AMM designs disadvantages liquidity providers
(LPs) and inevitably leads to arbitrage losses for them. These losses effectively transfer
wealth from LPs to arbitrageurs and ultimately to blockchain validators, who extract infrastructure rents. These rents increase the cost of liquidity provision, especially in volatile
markets, potentially leading to a liquidity freeze.
Arbitrage opportunities in AMMs typically arise from external shocks to asset fundamentals or from price impacts caused by uninformed DEX trades, leading to deviations between
AMM exchange rates and fundamental values. While LPs might attempt to withdraw their
liquidity to avoid being exploited by arbitrageurs, the blockchain’s transaction execution
mechanism prioritizes transactions based on gas fees rather than order timing. This creates
a “tragedy of the commons”: losses to LPs are shared across the entire liquidity pool, but
an arbitrageur offering the highest gas fee captures the entire arbitrage profit from trading
against the pool. Each LP, acting rationally, will only bid up to their individual expected
loss—a fraction of the total arbitrage gain—making it economically unfeasible to outbid arbitrageurs. Consequently, LPs collectively suffer from being arbitraged but lack individual
incentives to act.
Moreover, we demonstrate that intense competition among arbitrageurs leads to a Bertrandlike bidding war, with arbitrageurs offering gas fees up to their entire expected profit. As a
result, arbitrage rents are almost entirely transferred to validators as infrastructure rents.
Contrary to what one might expect given the decentralized nature of DEXs—where the
exchange is not owned by any single entity and the underlying blockchain is maintained
by a decentralized network of validators—our analysis shows that infrastructure rents can
be substantial. Validators extract a significantly higher proportion of arbitrage rents (up
3

to 100%) compared to the estimated 30% in CEXs (Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019)). This
disproportionality arises due to the block-by-block, first-price auction system that inherently
disadvantages cancellation orders and favors arbitrage activities. Additionally, individual
validators exert monopolistic control over transaction ordering within their assigned blocks,
enabling them to maximize rent extraction.
In contrast, market makers in CEXs wield
bargaining power over co-location fees by leveraging their capacity to redirect liquidity to
other exchanges (Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019)).
To address these challenges, we consider potential modifications in market design that
could mitigate arbitrage and infrastructure rents.
First, we explore the impact of time
priority and speed technology. While blocks are appended to the chain in discrete time
intervals, investing in speed technology can provide a competitive advantage, especially when
price shocks occur just before a new block is proposed or when block intervals are short.
Speed technology allows agents to submit transactions for immediate inclusion, outpacing
others who must wait for subsequent blocks. Our analysis reveals that speed technology
is predominantly adopted by arbitrageurs and primarily serves to redistribute rents from
validators to these fast arbitrageurs. Its overall effectiveness in reducing infrastructure rents
is limited. In rare instances where a single liquidity provider controls a majority of the pool
and can afford the technology, they can partially mitigate partially their own arbitrage risks.
However, we show that due to the nature of liquidity pooling, widespread adoption of speed
technology among multiple LPs is economically unfeasible.
Second, we investigate the impact of alternative transaction sequencing rules on infrastructure rents. We demonstrate that validators are unlikely to implement random sequencing
or prioritize liquidity providers, as such changes would diminish their ability to extract infrastructure rents. This behavior parallels the reluctance observed in centralized exchanges
to adopt market designs that curtail arbitrage opportunities (Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019);
Brolley and Zoican (2023a)). Similar to operators of CEXs, validators prioritize rules that
optimize their revenues, even if it disadvantages other market participants.
4

Third, we allow liquidity providers to choose from various pricing curves differing in
convexity levels. Higher convexity, while potentially reducing arbitrage rents, also leads to
lower trading volumes and diminished fees for LPs. We show that when arbitrage rents are
higher, LPs may choose more convex pricing curves in equilibrium.
Our empirical analysis supports our theoretical implications, highlighting the contrasting behaviors of two stablecoin pairs, USDT-USDC and DAI-USDC, during the Silicon
Valley Bank (SVB) crisis. While the USDT-USDC pair experienced significant volatility
and exchange rate fluctuations, the DAI-USDC pair maintained relative stability due to the
Peg Stability Module (PSM). This divergence provides a natural quasi-experimental setting.
Employing a difference-in-differences approach, we present statistical evidence indicating liquidity withdrawal and increased usage of more convex pricing curves during periods marked
by high volatility and elevated arbitrage rents.
Furthermore, our empirical investigation confirms our theoretical prediction that a significant portion of arbitrage revenues is captured by validators as gas fees. With a median
gas cost to arbitrage revenue ratio of 0.96, our findings suggest that at least half of the
transactions incur gas costs nearing the total revenue earned.
Literature Review.
Our paper contributes to the rapidly growing literature on decentralized exchanges.
The most closely related study to ours is Lehar and Parlour (2023). Their work highlights
the essential distinctions between AMMs and limit order books, noting that AMMs separate
price discovery, conducted by arbitrageurs, from liquidity provision, managed by liquidity
providers. They compare these two market structures to explore the conditions under which
trading through AMMs becomes more cost-effective.
Trading motives in Lehar and Parlour (2023)’s model are similar to ours, as arbitrageurs
either reverse liquidity traders’ transactions or exploit stale prices on DEXs. However, our
work diverges in two main significant ways. First, unlike their focus on adverse selection
5

due to private information, we examine public information shocks on fundamental values,
similar to Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015). This modeling choice enables us to isolate the
inefficiencies arising purely from market structure. We show how liquidity providers, even
when equally informed as arbitrageurs, have no incentive to withdraw from the pool due to
a “tragedy of the commons” problem. Second, we explicitly incorporate the settlement constraints imposed by the underlying blockchain infrastructure, and examine how competition
for transaction priority impacts market outcomes, resulting in infrastructure rents extracted
by validators.
On the empirical side, Lehar and Parlour (2023) demonstrate how volatility and uninformed flows impact pool sizes on Uniswap V2. Our empirical analysis complements theirs
by investigating how liquidity providers on Uniswap V3 strategically adjust the convexity of
their pricing curves or withdraw liquidity during periods of heightened volatility.
Milionis et al. (2024) link the costs of liquidity provision to token volatility with a
continuous-time model, and derive a formula that quantifies pick-off risk in a fixed-size
pool. In their framework, liquidity providers are not strategic, and the role of the underlying
blockchain infrastructure is abstracted away. In contrast, we incorporate strategic interactions between liquidity providers and arbitrageurs and examine the mechanisms that lead to
infrastructure rent extraction.
The “tragedy of the commons” arising in our setting provides a rationale for some of the
assumptions made in recent asset pricing studies on AMMs, including those by Hasbrouck,
Rivera, and Saleh (2023) and Milionis et al. (2024).
These studies assume that liquidity providers cannot adjust their positions or withdraw funds during specific periods. We
demonstrate that the blockchain’s execution priority enables arbitrageurs to outbid liquidity
providers, thereby justifying the use of fixed-duration constraints in these models.
A few other studies have analyzed the role of gas fees on market quality in DEXs. Brolley
and Zoican (2023b) suggest that DEXs may lower the overall computational costs linked to
latency arbitrage races, providing an alternative perspective on infrastructure fees. Capponi,
6

Jia, and Yu (2022) show that price discovery on DEXs occurs as informed traders bid higher
gas fees to gain execution priority over uninformed traders. Caparros†, Chaudhary, and
Klein (2024) find that high infrastructure fees are a friction for liquidity providers; lower gas
fees enable more frequent position updates, allowing for tighter price ranges on Uniswap V3.
Several studies have analyzed trading costs and liquidity provision in AMMs.
Park
(2023) illustrate how the liquidity invariance of AMM pricing functions, together with the
transparency of blockchain mempools, contributes to front-running and escalates trading
costs. Aoyagi and Ito (2021) examine trader behaviors in markets where centralized and
decentralized exchanges coexist. Barbon and Ranaldo (2021) compare price efficiency in
centralized and decentralized exchanges, and highlight the increasing competitiveness of
AMMs.
Lehar, Parlour, and Zoican (2023) examine liquidity fragmentation across AMMs with
varying fee structures and show how, in equilibrium, fragmented liquidity can outperform a
single-fee market. Foley, O’Neill, and Putnins (2024) demonstrate that AMMs offer deep liquidity for high-volume, low-volatility assets and reduce trading costs compared to traditional
markets across various asset classes, including foreign exchange, bonds, major commodities,
and certain equities. Malinova and Park (2023) argue that that well-designed AMMs could
substantially lower transaction costs in equity markets due to the risk-sharing properties of
liquidity pools. Relative to these studies, our contribution lies in integrating the blockchain
infrastructure with the AMM market design.
More broadly, we contribute to market microstructure literature, particularly regarding arbitrage rents associated with stale orders in traditional, centralized limit-order-book
exchanges, as explored in the studies of Foucault (1999) and Budish, Cramton, and Shim
(2015). We underscore the shift in rent extraction dynamics from centralized to decentralized
exchanges. Notably, while centralized platforms distribute these rents between exchanges
and arbitrageurs (Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019), Brolley and Zoican (2023a)), decentralized
exchanges see validators capturing the majority of arbitrage rents. Additionally, our study
7

offers a comprehensive comparison between DEXs and traditional equity markets, examining infrastructure, rent extraction, trade execution rules, settlement and pricing mechanisms,
and trading costs.
Our study also extends the literature on crypto trading. Prior research, such as Griffins
and Shams (2020), Cong et al. (2020), and Li, Shin, and Wang (2018), has focused on trading
activities and potential manipulation in centralized crypto exchanges. In contrast to studies
examining blockchain as the underlying technology for cryptocurrency transactions (e.g.,
Huberman, Leshno, and Moallemi (2021), Sockin and Xiong (2023), Easley, O’Hara, and
Basu (2019), Pagnotta (2021), Schilling and Uhlig (2019), Athey et al. (2016), Cong, Li, and
Wang (2020), Irresberger et al. (2020)), we incorporate the infrastructure layer of blockchain
within the context of DEXs.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides institutional details of DEXs, and relates them to CEXs. Section 3 develops our game-theoretical model. We solve for the model
equilibrium in Section 4, and conduct an in-depth analysis of arbitrage and infrastructure
rents. Section 5 provides empirical evidence supporting our model’s implications. We conclude in Section 6. Appendix A explores analogies between automated market makers and
traditional limit order books, while Appendix B provides robustness analysis for the key
testable implications of our model. Technical proofs are detailed in Appendix C.

DEXs: Institutional Details and Relations to Limit
Order Books
In this section, we discuss the order execution mechanisms of the blockchain infrastructure
and provide institutional details about decentralized exchanges (DEXs). We then highlight
the similarities and differences between DEXs and traditional limit order books (LOBs).
8

2.1
Blockchain Infrastructure
The management and execution of orders on a DEX are fundamentally determined by its
underlying blockchain technology, which plays a critical role in prioritizing and sequencing
order executions. Unlike traditional financial markets where orders are typically prioritized
based on time, blockchain transactions are often prioritized based on transaction fees rather
than submission time.
In DEXs, users submit trading orders to validators on the blockchain network and bid a
transaction fee, commonly referred to as a gas fee on platforms like Ethereum.1 Validators
function as service providers, selecting a subset of pending transactions from their memory
pools to construct a new block. These transactions are executed in a predefined sequence
before the block is appended to the blockchain.
Blocks are constrained by size, and transaction inclusion is determined by bid gas fees.
Transactions with higher fees are typically prioritized to maximize validators’ profits. Consequently, transactions with lower gas fees may remain pending in the memory pool for a
longer time before being confirmed. Validators also have discretion over the sequencing of
transactions within a block—that is, they decide the order in which transactions are executed.
2.2
Automated Market Makers (AMMs)
The majority of DEXs function as Automated Market Makers (AMMs), a market structure
characterized by the utilization of liquidity pools and algorithmic pricing mechanisms. While
non-AMM DEXs exist2, AMM-based DEXs account for at least 80% of total DEX trading
volumes as of September 2024 (see CoinGecko (2024)).

Liquidity Provision and Trading
We describe the core mechanism of AMMs, focusing
on the “constant product function” model used by leading decentralized exchange platforms,
including Uniswap V2, SushiSwap, and PancakeSwap. Consider a liquidity pool that supports the exchange of two token types, A and B. Participants who hold both token types
can act as liquidity providers (LPs) by depositing an equivalent value of both tokens into
the pool. In return, LPs receive pool tokens (also known as liquidity tokens), representing
their proportional stake in the pool.
For example, suppose a liquidity pool initially contains 1 unit of token A and 1 unit of
token B. If a LP contributes an additional 1 unit of token A and of token B, the pool now
contains 2 units of each token, and the LP owns 50% of the pool. The value of the LP’s
stake depends on the pool’s composition at the time of withdrawal. If, at withdrawal, the
pool contains 4 units of token A and 1 unit of token B, the LP would receive 2 units of
token A and 0.5 units of token B, assuming no other deposits or withdrawals occurred in
the interim.
Traders can exchange tokens with the pool using the constant product formula, which
ensures that the product of the quantities of the two tokens remains invariant, maintaining a
constant: xy = k, where x and y are the quantities of tokens A and B in the liquidity pool,
respectively. For instance, starting with x = 2 and y = 2, the invariant is k = 4. If a trader
wishes to withdraw ∆B = 1 token B from the pool, the constant product formula dictates
that they must supply ∆A tokens A such that (2 + ∆A)(2 −∆B) = 2 × 2 = 4. Therefore, the
trader must supply ∆A = 2 tokens A to withdraw 1 token B from the pool. The exchange
rate in this transaction is thus ∆B

Enhanced Flexibility in Liquidity Provision
Early AMMs, like Uniswap V2, used a constant product function, F(x, y) = xy, to determine
prices for all liquidity providers. Recent innovations, exemplified by Uniswap V3, introduce
greater flexibility by enabling liquidity providers to choose their preferred pricing schedules
from a range of options.

pricing function F:

Model
Our model consists of three periods, indexed by t = 1, 2, 3, and includes a representative
blockchain validator, multiple liquidity providers, two competing arbitrageurs, and two types
of investors. All agents are risk-neutral and do not discount future utility.
Token Fundamental Values
The liquidity pool supports trading between two tokens, A
and B. The fundamental value of token A at the end of period t is denoted by p(t), while
the fundamental value of token B is fixed at one, serving as the numeraire. These values are
public information. At the end of period 3, tokens can be liquidated at their fundamental
values.

Arbitrage Profits, Infrastructure Costs, and Rent
Mitigation Strategies
In this section, we solve for the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) of our game
theoretical model using backward induction. Our equilibrium analysis provides two main
insights. First, the blockchain’s transaction sequencing mechanism creates a tragedy of the
commons among liquidity providers: while arbitrage losses are shared across the entire pool,
each individual LP bears the full cost of submitting a withdrawal order, making it unprofitable to outbid arbitrageurs. Second, arbitrageurs engage in a Bertrand-like bidding war
competition for transaction priority, bidding gas fees up to their full expected arbitrage profit.
This intense competition results in redirecting nearly all arbitrage profits into infrastructure
rent that accrue to validators.
In Section 4.1, we characterize these equilibrium outcomes and describe the underlying
economic mechanisms. In Section 4.2, we analyze how infrastructure rents impact liquidity
provision, demonstrating that high exchange rate volatility can trigger liquidity freezes. In
Section 4.3, we evaluate potential modifications to the market design —including speed
technology, alternative sequencing rules, and enhanced flexibility in liquidity provision (e.g.,
Uniswap V3)—and assess their effectiveness in mitigating arbitrage and infrastructure rents.

Infrastructure Rents
The arbitrage profits extracted from liquidity providers are ultimately transferred to validators through gas fee bidding. In equilibrium, intense competition
among arbitrageurs (akin to Bertrand competition) for transaction priority drives gas fees
up to their expected arbitrage profits π. This process effectively converts arbitrage rents
into infrastructure rents captured by validators—a phenomenon commonly referred to as
Maximal Extractable Value (MEV) in the blockchain community.8
4.1.1
Infrastructure Rents: DEXs versus CEXs
One might intuitively expect that the decentralized nature of blockchain infrastructure would
lead to lower infrastructure rent extraction in DEXs compared to CEXs. After all, DEXs
eliminate centralized intermediaries, replace exchange operators with competing validators,
and operate on open-source protocols. However, our analysis reveals the opposite: infrastructure rents in DEXs systematically exceed those in traditional markets.
Both market structures require arbitrageurs to pay for execution priority—through gas
fees in DEXs and co-location services in CEXs. However, validators in DEXs capture up
8For an overview of MEV, see https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/mev/.
26

to 100% of arbitrage profits through gas fee bidding, far exceeding the approximately 30%
extracted by exchanges through co-location fees (Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019)). Moreover,
while fast liquidity providers in CEXs can often cancel orders to avoid adverse selection
(Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015)), DEX liquidity providers systematically fail to protect their positions from sniping due to the blockchain’s transaction sequencing mechanism
(Proposition 1). This counterintuitive result—higher rent extraction in decentralized versus
centralized venues—stems from two fundamental factors:
1. Transaction Execution Mechanisms: DEXs employ a block-by-block, first-price auction for transaction ordering. Arbitrageurs compete for profits from the entire pool,
while liquidity providers aim to protect only their proportional shares. Consequently,
withdrawal orders systematically lose in gas fee competition because it is not profitable
for individual liquidity providers to bid higher gas fees than arbitrageurs. This auction
structure induces intense competition among arbitrageurs, driving gas fees up to their
full arbitrage profits.
In contrast, CEXs generally operate on a continuous-time trading model, where traders
invest in low-latency technology and co-location services to secure execution priority.
In this environment, fast liquidity providers can quickly update and cancel limit orders
to avoid adverse selection, whereas slower liquidity providers remain vulnerable.
2. Market Structure and Competition: Despite the decentralized nature of blockchain networks, individual validators exercise temporary but complete monopolistic control over
transaction ordering during their block proposal windows. This monopoly inhibits the
adoption of mechanisms aimed at reducing infrastructure rents because validators have
little incentive to forgo their rent extraction opportunities. This structure contrasts
sharply with traditional exchanges, where market makers can negotiate infrastructure
fees by leveraging the threat of redirecting order flow to competing venues. As shown
in Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019), this bargaining power limits rent extraction in CEXs.

Time Priority and Speed Technology
Recent blockchain innovations have focused on reducing block times to increase transaction
throughput. This raises a natural question: Can faster block times mitigate the infrastructure rents we identified? Faster block times offer opportunities for speed advantages through
technological investment; however, our model demonstrates that when market participants
engage in speed competition, the underlying tragedy of the commons for liquidity providers
persists. In this scenario, arbitrageurs may even extract additional rents.
Speed advantages arise because transactions submitted immediately before block creation can secure inclusion in the current block, ahead of those submitted even slightly later.
Market participants can gain these advantages by investing in specialized technology—such
as optimized network connections or proximity hosting—that reduces latency in transaction
submission.

in a block.10

4.3.2
Alternative Transaction Sequencing Rules
An important question is whether alternative transaction sequencing rules could mitigate
infrastructure rents. Traditional exchanges have experimented with various design modifications—such as speed bumps—to protect liquidity providers from adverse selection. We
analyze whether similar approaches could succeed in decentralized markets.
We extend our baseline model by introducing an initial period (t = 0) where validators
choose among three possible sequencing rules:
1. Random Sequencing: Transactions within a block are executed in a random order,
independent of bid fees. This mirrors symmetrical speed bumps in CEXs, affecting
34

both incoming and cancellation orders equally.
2. Priority for Liquidity Providers: Transactions submitted by liquidity providers,
particularly withdrawal orders, are given execution priority over swap orders. This
approach resembles asymmetrical speed bumps in CEXs, which apply delays only to
incoming executable orders.
3. Fee-Based Sequencing: Transactions are sequenced by bid fees, ordered from highest to lowest, consistent with our baseline model and standard practice in many
blockchains.
The following proposition demonstrates why validators have no incentive to implement
sequencing rules that favor liquidity providers:
Proposition 4. For validators, adhering to the fee-based sequencing rule remains the dominant strategy. The alternative sequencing rules yield the following outcomes in the subsequent
subgame starting from period 1:
1. Random Sequencing: Infrastructure rents decrease, but arbitrageurs capture all the
rents.
2. Priority for Liquidity Providers: Prioritizing the processing of withdrawal orders
from liquidity providers eliminates arbitrage opportunities and prevents validators and
arbitrageurs from profiting through rent extraction.
Random sequencing eliminates validators’ ability to extract infrastructure rents through
gas fee auctions, as execution priority no longer depends on fees. In equilibrium, arbitrageurs
capture the entire arbitrage rents because they no longer need to bid high fees to secure
priority. 13 Priority sequencing for liquidity provider withdrawals would, in theory, eliminate

trades, similar to frequent batch auctions proposed by Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015).
However, neither alternative sequencing rule is incentive-compatible for validators. Implementing these rules reduces or eliminates the infrastructure rents that validators can
extract. Consequently, validators prefer fee-based sequencing, which allows them to maximize their revenues from gas fees. This resistance to efficiency-enhancing market design
parallels the reluctance of traditional exchanges to adopt mechanisms like frequent batch
auctions, as documented by Budish, Lee, and Shim (2019). These findings highlight the
necessity of accounting for validator incentive compatibility in DEX market design.
Multi-Blockchain Competition
Could competition among blockchains address the imbalance between validators and liquidity providers? This question is particularly relevant as
blockchain platforms increasingly compete for market share. However, the effectiveness of
such competition depends crucially on its nature.
In current blockchain systems, competition largely focuses on technical parameters such
as transaction speed and throughput. (TPS). Yet if competition remains limited to these
dimensions without altering the fundamental structure of block space allocation, validators
will maintain their monopolistic control over transaction ordering within their proposal windows. This control allows validators, acting as auctioneers, to extract infrastructure rents
through order sequencing, perpetuating rent-maximizing behavior that disadvantages liquidity providers. Moreover, as shown in Section 4.3.1, increasing block speed makes speed
technology more important, which primarily helps arbitrageurs capture a larger portion of
rents rather than eliminating infrastructure rents.
In theory, blockchains could compete by adopting sequencing protocols that favor liquidity providers, thereby attracting more liquidity and trading volume. However, competition on execution rules—specifically, protocols favoring liquidity providers—faces three
significant challenges. First, validator incentive compatibility: when lucrative arbitrage op36

portunities arise, validators controlling blocks retain strong incentives to prioritize high-fee
transactions, undermining any commitment to liquidity-provider-friendly sequencing. Without mechanisms to ensure credible commitment, stated sequencing rules remain effectively
unenforceable.
Second, the feasibility of establishing consensus-based sequencing rules remains questionable in blockchain design. Geographic and network-induced variances in transaction receipt
and ordering across decentralized validators make it difficult to establish and maintain consistent sequencing protocols across the network. This technical challenge compounds the
economic problem of validator incentives.
Third, it is difficult for liquidity providers to make ex-ante investments, such as paying
for preferential execution arrangements. Unlike concentrated high-frequency trading firms in
traditional markets that can invest in co-location, liquidity providers in DEXs are typically
dispersed participants. This decentralized structure, combined with their limited participation across pools, makes it economically unfeasible for them to bear the fixed costs of
sophisticated execution technology or priority arrangements. Moreover, their decentralized
nature makes it challenging to coordinate collective action, further weakening their ability
to advocate for and maintain liquidity-provider-friendly protocols.

a decrease in arbitrage rent for liquidity providers and a decline in fee income. When k
takes on lower values, the marginal benefit from diminishing arbitrage rents surpasses the
marginal cost associated with a reduction in fee revenue. This makes it advantageous for
liquidity providers to increase the pricing curve convexity. In contrast, at higher values of
k, the scenario reverses: the loss incurred in fee revenue becomes more significant than the
benefits derived from reduced arbitrage rents. As a result, any further increase in convexity
turns out to be disadvantageous. The equilibrium point Fk∗represents an optimal balance

Empirical Analysis
In this section, we present empirical evidence substantiating the primary conclusions drawn
from our model. The tested hypotheses are outlined in Section 5.1. In Section 5.2, we adopt
a difference-in-difference (DiD) methodology and leverage the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank
(SVB) as a natural, quasi-experimental event to show how increased arbitrage rents impact
liquidity provision, particularly prompting liquidity providers to withdraw from liquidity
pools and shift towards pricing curves with greater convexity. In Section 5.3, we show that a
substantial portion of the profits generated by arbitrageurs is passed on to validators through
gas fees.
5.1
Testable Implications
The main implications of our model are as follows:
(1) Arbitrage Rents, Volatility, and Withdrawals: Increased volatility in exchange rates elevates the value of arbitrage rents. Consequently, liquidity providers are incentivized to
withdraw their deposits from liquidity pools. This relationship is formalized in Theorem
2.
(2) Arbitrage Rents and Convexity of Pricing Function: Higher exchange rate volatility incentivizes liquidity providers to choose more convex pricing schedules to reduce arbitrage
rents, in line with the findings of Theorem 5.
(3) Infrastructure Rents:
gas fees consume a substantial portion of arbitrageurs’ trading

While implications (2) and (3) are unique to our model, alternative explanations exist for the relationship between volatility and pool liquidity described in implication (1).
For instance, Milionis et al. (2024) analyze “quote-sniping” risk—analogous to the sniping risk faced by traditional market makers (see Budish, Cramton, and Shim (2015))—in

cannot predict how liquidity providers react to increased arbitrage rents.
Lehar and Parlour (2023) employ extensive Uniswap V2 data and panel regression techniques to show that volatility, as a proxy for adverse selection, negatively impacts pool size
both cross-sectionally and over time. Our study complements these findings by employing a
quasi-experimental design centered on the SVB collapse, enabling us to identify the causal
effect of increased arbitrage rents on liquidity provision and the strategic adoption of more
convex pricing curves in Uniswap V3.
5.2

USDT. This includes contrasting pools where tokens maintain mechanical price relationships
(USDC-DAI) against those with more flexible exchange rates (USDC-USDT). Second, the
collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) serves as a natural quasi-experiment, enabling us to

In Section 5.2.1, we describe the stablecoin market structure and detail how the SVB

5.2.1
Stablecoin Market Segmentation
Our analysis examines trading pools involving three major stablecoins that employ distinct
mechanisms to maintain their dollar peg. The two largest, USDC and USDT, are fiat-backed

managed by a central issuer responsible for maintaining these reserves. As of November 2023,

5.3
Economic Significance of Infrastructure Rents
In this section, we empirically test our third hypothesis, which asserts that arbitrageurs bid
gas fees close to their overall profits, effectively transferring potential gains to infrastructure
providers (validators) due to intense competition. Our methodology aligns with the approach
outlined in Foucault, Kozhan, and Tham (2017). We specifically focus on cyclic (triangular)
arbitrage cases, where revenue streams are clearly identifiable and profits can be precisely
calculated.
Triangular arbitrage involves a sequence of trades that starts and ends with the same
token, typically executed atomically within a single transaction. For example, an arbitrageur
might start with WETH (Wrapped Ether), trade it for USDC, then for DAI, and finally back
to WETH, ending up with more WETH than initially held. The primary benefit of focusing
on triangular arbitrage is the straightforward calculation of profits: the profit is simply
the difference between the final and initial amounts of the starting token. This approach
avoids the complexities associated with tracking profits across multiple tokens and fluctuating
exchange rates.
As outlined by Foucault, Kozhan, and Tham (2017), triangular arbitrage typically occurs
under two conditions: (1) when at least one token’s price is outdated (analogous to our
as transaction throughput and scalability.
50

model’s stale price arbitrage), or (2) when trading pressures impact at least one token pair’s
price (similar to the reverse trade arbitrage in our model, also stemming from price impacts).
Data Collection.
We construct a dataset of cyclic arbitrage transactions executed on the
Ethereum blockchain, spanning blocks 14,982,026 to 16,326,370 (June 18, 2022, to January
3, 2023). To gather the data, we operated our own Ethereum node and employed the Geth
execution client to extract transaction receipts that triggered a Swap event in the smart
contracts of major DEX protocols: Uniswap V2, Uniswap V3, Balancer, SushiSwap, and
Curve. The resulting transaction-level dataset includes block-level timestamps, anonymized
participant identifiers (addresses), transaction hashes, gas fees, and the quantities of tokens
exchanged in each transaction.
To account for additional gas fees paid through private channels, we leverage the Flashbots Blocks API17, which records transaction hashes and total fees (including direct direct
payments or “bribes”) paid by arbitrageurs for all successfully executed Flashbots transactions.
We identify cyclic arbitrage transactions using the following criteria: the transaction
includes multiple swaps executed within a single atomic operation, forming a closed loop
where the output of each swap serves is the input for the next, except for one pair where
the profit is realized.18 Since transaction fees on Ethereum are paid in Ether (ETH), our
analysis centers on arbitrage trades where profits are realized in WETH, the ERC-20 token
representation of Ether. By focusing exclusively on arbitrage profits realized in WETH, we
eliminate additional variability introduced by currency conversion rates from other tokens
to WETH, ensuring more precise calculations of profits and costs.
To accurately measure arbitrage costs and liquidity providers’ revenues, we exclude transactions that combine cyclic arbitrage with unrelated DEX activities, such as liquidity provision or swaps in pools outside the arbitrage cycle. We retain transactions that involve

Concluding Remarks
DeFi holds the promise of creating a more transparent and efficient financial system. However, our analysis reveals that transitioning from traditional CEXs to blockchain-based
AMMs does not inherently eliminate the extraction of economic rents. In fact, blockchain
infrastructure introduces its own forms of rent extraction that can exceed those in traditional markets. Our key insight is that blockchain’s transaction sequencing mechanism,
while eliminating certain intermediaries, creates new forms of infrastructure rents and market
imbalance.
Our research highlights the critical role of blockchain validators in the operation of decentralized exchanges. By controlling transaction processing, validators can extract substantial
rents, reflecting dynamics similar to those observed in centralized exchanges.
This indicates that transitioning to decentralized systems alone does not eliminate the fundamental
incentives for infrastructure rent extraction. Crucially, our findings emphasize that aligning validator incentives is essential for the effective design of decentralized exchange (DEX)
markets. Moreover, recent developments such as Flashbots relays, Proposer-Builder Separation (PBS) frameworks, and Jito20 have raised concerns by providing mechanisms that,
intentionally or unintentionally, allow validators to maximize their rent extraction. While
these tools aim to increase transaction efficiency and reduce other problems such as negative
externalities from frontrunning, they can also enhance validators’ ability to capture arbitrage
rents and other forms of MEV (Capponi, Jia, and Wang (2024)).
Will market fix the market? We argue that blockchain competition may not effectively resolve the problem of rent extraction. Competition focused on transaction speed and throughput may inadvertently benefit arbitrageurs, who can better exploit speed advantages. Moreover, competition on sequencing rules faces significant constraints. Validators have little
incentive to adopt sequencing protocols that prioritize liquidity providers if such protocols
diminish their own rents. Technical challenges inherent in decentralized sequencing, as well
20Jito is a liquid staking platform on the Solana blockchain.
54

as coordination difficulties among liquidity providers—who are typically dispersed and lack
the ability to make significant ex-ante investments—further limit the potential for competition to address rent extraction.
Our analysis emphasizes the importance of transaction sequencing as a critical component
of the blockchain infrastructure. Effective solutions may require revising the infrastructure
to better align the incentives of validators with those of liquidity providers. One potential
interim solution is to implement centralized sequencing protocols that prioritize liquidity
providers over market-driven incentives, as seen in platforms like Hyperliquid. While this
strategy may grant liquidity providers execution priority, it introduces a level of centralization
into the underlying infrastructure.
This suggests that the pursuit of liquidity provision
incentives might lead to a partial re-centralization of certain blockchain functions.
Alternatively, recent developments in hardcoding sequencing rules within DEX smart
contracts—referred to as App-Specific Sequencing (ASS)—may help mitigate rent extraction
by enforcing liquidity-provider-friendly protocols. Nevertheless, validators might circumvent
these mechanisms by censoring or delaying LP transactions to maximize their own rent
extraction.
A different solution is to allow arbitrageurs to exploit their arbitrage opportunities while
redistributing the associated rents or MEV through auction mechanisms.
By designing
protocols that capture MEV and allocate it among ecosystem participants or the protocol
itself, the adverse effects of rent extraction on liquidity providers can be mitigated.
In conclusion, while DeFi and blockchain technologies hold the promise of creating more
open and efficient financial markets, our study underscores that without careful attention to
market design, the transition to decentralized infrastructures may not eliminate, and could
even exacerbate, economic rent extraction.

The Map of an Automated Market Maker’s Pricing
Curve to a Synthetic Limit Order Book
In this appendix, we outline a procedure for translating an AMM pricing curve into a synthetic Limit Order Book (LOB). This involves dividing the AMM’s bonding curve into
segments, determining the exchange quantities and rates for each segment, and organizing
them in a structure similar to a traditional order book. Additionally, we incorporate the
AMM’s transaction fees into this synthetic LOB as the half-spread. We remark that this
transformation serves primarily as a tool to understand the relation between AMM pricing
schedules and LOBs, without implying equivalence. Significant distinctions between AMMs
and centralized LOBs exist, as detailed in Table 1. For instance, unlike AMMs, LOBs do
not automatically quote around the last trade price.
The conversion procedure involves the following steps:
1. Segmenting the Bonding Curve: We partition the AMM’s bonding curve into
distinct intervals, each demarcated by end points (xi, yi, pi), i = 1, 2, . . .. Here, xi, yi
denote the quantities of tokens A and B in the pool, respectively, and pi is the marginal
exchange rate at that specific inventory level.
Each interval (pi, pi+1) delineates a
particular range of exchange prices. Figure 1 illustrates this segmentation.
2. Calculating Exchange Quantities: In each segment defined by points (xi, yi, pi)
and (xi+1, yi+1, pi+1), we calculate the pooled quantity of token B within this curve
section as qi = yi+1 −yi.
3. Determining Exchange Rates: We determine the exchange rate for each segment,
defined between points (xi, yi, pi) and (xi+1, yi+1, pi+1), as the average price across these
points. It is calculated using the formula pi = xi+1−xi
yi+1−yi .
4. Constructing the Synthetic LOB: Utilizing the calculated exchange rates and
quantities, we construct a synthetic LOB. Let (xk, yk, pk) be the current inventory
60

point in the liquidity pool. In the LOB, each segment’s exchange price and quantity
pair, (pi, qi), is represented as an ask order if i ≥k or a bid order if i < k. This
representation is based on the principle that purchasing B tokens with A tokens moves
the liquidity pool’s inventory to the right of the current point on the bonding curve,
while selling B tokens for A tokens moves it to the left. The layout of this synthetic
LOB is illustrated in Figure 2a.
5. Incorporating Transaction Fees as Half-Spread: In this synthetic LOB, we interpret the AMM’s transaction fee as the half-spread, which influences the effective
exchange rates. For each i ≥k, we adjust the exchange rate pi to (1+f)pi, accounting
for the cost of exchanging B tokens for A tokens, inclusive of fees. Conversely, for
i < k, we modify the rate pi to

Robustness Analysis Using Layer 2 Networks
One of our key testable model implications is that a higher exchange rate volatility causes
LPs to use more convex pricing schedules. Since each liquidity position adjustment requires
gas fee payments, it may be argued that liquidity providers might choose wider price ranges
during volatile periods primarily to minimize the frequency of costly position adjustments.
To rule out this alternative explanation, we analyze equivalent pools on two of the most
popular Layer 2 networks, specifically Optimism and Polygon, where gas fees are substantially lower than on Ethereum. For example, while the average gas fee per Uniswap V3
trade on Ethereum is approximately $14, trades on Polygon cost only $0.01 (see Caparros,

Proof of Proposition 4. To establish that fee-based sequencing is the dominant strategy for
validators, we analyze the subgames starting from period 1 under each alternative sequencing
rule and compare the resulting infrastructure rents to those under fee-based sequencing.
Random Sequencing: Under random sequencing, transactions within a block are executed in a random order, independent of the gas fees bid by agents. Under this rule, in period
3, following the emergence of an arbitrage opportunity, agents refrain from bidding any gas
fee. Since execution priority is random and not influenced by gas fees, agents have no incentive to bid positive gas fees. Any positive bid increases their cost without improving their
chances of early execution. Therefore, all agents—liquidity providers and arbitrageurs—bid
zero gas fees.
With random sequencing, the probability that a liquidity provider successfully withdraws
her liquidity before arbitrageurs execute their trades is 1
3 (assuming two arbitrageurs) Consequently, by the linearity of expectation, the total arbitrage rents extracted from liquidity
providers are decreased, amounting to only a third of π(d(2)
A , d(2)
B , p(2)).
Validators earn zero infrastructure rents because all agents bid zero gas fees. Arbitrageurs
capture the entire arbitrage rent that remains after accounting for the liquidity provider’s
potential withdrawal.
In period 2, there is no change in the optimal trading actions. In period 1, the reduced
cost of liquidity provision due to lower arbitrage rents leads to an increase in the liquidity
freeze threshold. However, a validator’s payoff in becomes zero, as agents lack the incentive
to bid higher gas fees.
Liquidity Provider Prioritized Sequencing: In this sequencing rule, transactions
submitted by liquidity providers—particularly withdrawal orders—are prioritized over all
other transactions, regardless of gas fees. Similar to the random sequencing case, agents
have no incentive to bid positive gas fees. Arbitrageurs understand that liquidity providers’
withdrawal transactions will always be executed before their arbitrage trades. Since liquidity
providers can withdraw their liquidity before arbitrageurs can act on price discrepancies,

arbitrage opportunities are effectively eliminated. Arbitrageurs cannot profit because the
pool’s reserves adjust before they can trade.
The strategic decisions for period 2 remain the same. In period 1, the lack of arbitrage
costs encourages all liquidity providers to make deposits, as they don’t face arbitrage risks.
However, validators earn zero infrastructure rents because agents bid zero gas fees, and
there are no arbitrage trades generating transaction fees. Neither arbitrageurs nor validators
extract rents. Liquidity providers are fully protected from arbitrage losses.
Comparison with Fee-Based Sequencing
Validators prefer fee-based sequencing because it maximizes their infrastructure rents. Both alternative sequencing rules—random
sequencing and priority for liquidity providers—result in zero infrastructure rents for validators. Therefore, adhering to the fee-based sequencing rule is the dominant strategy for
validators, as it allows them to maximize their revenues at the expense of liquidity providers
and, to some extent, arbitrageurs.
