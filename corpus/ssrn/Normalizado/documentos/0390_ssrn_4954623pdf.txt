The Variance Risk Premium over Trading and Non-Trading Periods   
 
Lucas Papagelis1 and George Dotsis2 
 
In this paper we decompose the variance risk premium into overnight and intraday components 
using model-free implied variance stock indices in US, Europe and Asia. We find that during the 
non-trading overnight period the variance risk premium is significantly negative, whereas during 
the intraday trading period the variance risk premium becomes positive and often insignificant.  
We also assess the predictive ability of the overnight and intraday variance risk premiums with 
respect to future equity returns. We find that the intraday component performs better at shorter 
prediction horizons whereas the overnight variance risk premium performs better at longer 
horizons. Our empirical results suggest that non-trading effects are an important determinant of 
the variance risk premium.

1. Introduction 
It is well known in the literature that average delta-hedged option returns are negative (see, 
for example, Bakshi and Kapadia, 2003) because the volatility embedded in option prices tends to 
be higher than the realized volatility (see, for example Bollerslev et al., 2009). The negative risk 
premium embedded in option prices indicates that investors are willing to pay an insurance 
premium to buy protection against unexpected changes in volatility. Recent studies have 
uncovered an interesting phenomenon related to the different behavior of option returns during 
trading and non-trading periods.  Muravyev and Ni (2020) find that daily delta-hedged overnight 
option returns in the S&P 500 are typically negative, whereas intraday option returns turn positive. 
Similarly, Bhat et al. (2024) find that Nifty options display negative overnight returns and positive 
intraday returns.  
Motivated by these intriguing findings, in this paper we decompose the variance risk 
premium into its overnight and intraday components. More specifically, we use equity model-free 
implied variance indices in the US and abroad and decompose the variance risk premium into its 
overnight and intraday components using the daily mark-to-market returns of a hypothetical 
portfolio of a variance swap. Variance swaps provide pure exposure to the return variance of the 
underlying asset and hence the return of a variance swap quantifies the variance risk premium 
better than delta hedged option returns. Then we use predictive regressions to test the predictive 
ability of the variance risk premium components with respect to future equity returns. To the best 
of our knowledge, our study is the first to decompose the variance risk premium into overnight 
and intraday components using a variance swap approach and test the predictive ability of the 
overnight and intraday components of the variance risk premium.  

3 
 
In the extant literature nmany studies use the difference between the model-free implied 
variance and realized variance in order to quantify the variance risk premium. Although it is a 
well-documented fact that variance carries a negative risk premium, the underlying factors that 
generate this premium are not well understood. The variance risk premium may reflect 
compensation for bearing the risk of unexpected changes in volatility (see for example, e.g., Bali 
& Zhou, (2016); Bollerslev & Todorov, (2011); Carr & Wu, (2009); Drechsler & Yaron, (2011)), 
it may be generated by market frictions or the presence of financial intermediaries in the option 
market (see for example, e.g., Chan et al., (1995); Fournier & Jacobs, (2020); Gârleanu et al., 
(2009); Muravyev, (2016); Santa-Clara & Saretto, (2009)), or it may be the result of mispricing 
and sentiment volatility (see for example, e.g., Bondarenko, (2014); Branger & Schlag, (2008); 
Goyal & Saretto, (2009); Han, (2008); Jones & Shemesh, (2018); Muravyev & Ni, (2020); 
Poteshman, (2001); Weinbaum et al., (2023)). Given the prevalence of the variance risk premium 
in asset pricing studies, the contribution of this paper is to provide some further insights into the 
nature of the variance risk premium by decomposing the daily variance risk premium into 
overnight and intraday components using model-free implied variance indices.  
Model-free implied variance represents the risk-neutral expected value of the return variance 
and is constructed using the prices of out-of-the-money calls and puts (see, for example, e.g., Carr 
and Madan, (1998); Demeterfi et al., (1999); Britten-Jones and Neuberger, (2000); Jiang and Tian, 
(2005); Carr and Wu, (2009), Dotsis and Vlastakis, (2016)).  Many studies in the literature show 
that the difference between realized variance and model-free implied variance is, on average, 
negative, and hence, variance swap buyers are willing to pay a premium to hedge unexpected 
changes in volatility. 

4 
 
In the empirical analysis, we use the daily mark-to-market profit and loss of a hypothetical 
variance swap to decompose the daily (close-to-close) variance swap return into its overnight 
(close-to-open) and intraday (open-to-close) components. We use model-free implied variance 
indices based on major stock indices in the US market (S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, and Russell 2000) 
and some major stock indices outside the US (Europe and Asia). In the empirical analysis we also 
employ implied variance indices on individual stocks. We find that the pricing of variance risk is 
significantly different during the overnight and intraday periods. In particular, we find that the 
overnight variance swap returns are persistently negative and statistically significant, while 
intraday variance swap returns are mostly positive and sometimes insignificant. We observe the 
same pattern of overnight versus intraday variance returns when we calculate the variance swap 
returns using implied variance indices in US, Europe and Asia or stock implied variance indices.  
We also estimate monthly predictive regressions of stock indices’ returns using the variance 
risk premiums as the predictor variable. In line with previous studies in the literature (e.g., 
Bollerslev et al., 2009) we find that in the case of the S&P 500 the variance risk premium is a 
significant predictor of stock returns at short-term horizons. When we decompose the variance risk 
premium into its two components, we find that the intraday variance risk premium has better 
predictive ability over short horizons whereas the overnight variance risk premium performs better 
at longer horizons. These results are confirmed using both panel and individual predictive 
regressions. 
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. The next Section presents a literature 
review. Section 3 describes the methodology for constructing variance swap returns. Section 4 
discusses the data and presents the empirical results and Section 5 concludes.

2. Literature review 
The different behavior of stock markets during the intraday period and the non-trading period 
is a well-documented fact and many explanations have been suggested in the extant literature. For 
example, Cliff et al. (2008) argue that the high opening prices are partly responsible for the positive 
overnight returns. Kelly & Clark (2011) consider whether this could be a result of the behavior by 
some traders that liquidate their positions towards the end of the session and reestablish them at 
the next opening or the result of not fully professional traders who feel increased confidence over 
their position during trading hours. Lou et al. (2019) argue that the heterogeneity of investors and 
the different participation of them during different periods of the day could be responsible for the 
persistence of the overnight and intraday different behavior of returns. As institutional investors 
and arbitrageurs avoid initiating trades at the opening of the session, this leaves space for the 
uniformed investors to define with their trades the return sign and the magnitude of the opening. 
As the initial momentum subsides, and the participation of arbitrageurs and institutional investors 
increases, the behavior of returns changes. Bogousslavsky (2021) argues that institutional 
constraints, margin parameters, and price pressure caused by the reluctance of arbitrageurs to trade 
at the opening of the session, while they are eager to reduce their positions near market closing, 
supports the different behavior in non-trading versus intraday returns. The phenomenon of 
overnight versus intraday return is also documented in futures markets. The electronically traded 
(mini) S&P futures contracts are practically traded 24 hours a day (with a short time interruption). 
However, their returns do not accrue linearly during the trading session (e.g., Boyarchenko et al., 
2021). Almost all of the average market return accrues around the opening of European markets 
(Bondarenko and Muravyev, 2023).  

6 
 
The difference between the intraday and non-trading period returns is also documented in 
option markets. Delta hedged option returns are on average negative (Bakshi & Kapadia, 2003) as 
the implied variance tends to be higher than the realized one (Bollerslev and Zhou, 2006). 
However, average options returns are not distributed evenly around the clock. It is well 
documented that options display different performance among different periods of the day. Sheikh 
and Ronn (1994) find that option returns are more negative during non-trading periods (mainly 
during weekends) and positive towards the end of the trading session. Jones and Shemesh (2018) 
confirm that during non-trading periods and especially on weekends, options present lower returns. 
As options present different behavior during trading and non-trading hours, so do their implied 
variances. While the underlying’s realized variance is decreased substantially during non-trading 
hours, implied variance seems to incorporate the expectation that variance will remain sizable even 
when markets are closed. 
Muravyev and Ni (2020) find that while (delta hedged) option returns are on average 
negative, overnight returns are on average more negative, while intraday returns become on 
average positive. They argue that for this puzzling change of signs, the most promising explanation 
is the “volatility seasonality” bias where market participants are acting “as if” the day and night 
instantaneous volatilities are the same while, as previously documented, the per-hour volatility is 
higher during trading hours compared to non-trading hours. Also implied variance seems to behave 
differently during trading hours and non-trading session. Chen et al. (2021) decompose daily 
changes of VIX and find different average behavior of VIX during intraday and overnight periods. 
They find that intraday and overnight VIX changes are negative correlated, and they argue that the 
unavailability of trading during the non-trading hours creates uncertainty while trading helps to 
resolve uncertainty. 

7 
 
Wang et al. (2022) argue that returns and volatility outside trading hours contain unique 
information rather than just being supplementary data to intraday data. Building on this insight, 
they develop a model that distinguishes between intraday returns and overnight returns and show 
that their model reduces significantly option pricing errors. Liang et al. (2023) also develop an 
option pricing model based on different intraday and overnight variance dynamics. They show that 
this model offers improved accuracy in pricing SPX options particularly during periods of high 
volatility. 
Motivated by these findings, we examine if the intraday versus overnight behavior is 
observed in variance swaps and if this behavior is similar in other markets and indices besides the 
S&P 500. In the empirical analysis, we decompose the variance risk premium into its overnight 
and intraday components using the mark-to-market returns of a hypothetical variance swap 
position with a maturity of one calendar month (21 trading days). In our paper, we find that the 
returns of variance swaps are different during the intraday and the non-trading periods not only in 
the S&P 500 but also in other markets. The overnight variance swap return tends to be negative, 
while the intraday return tends to be positive.

4. Data and Discussion of Empirical Results 
4.1 Data Descriptive Statistics 
The data used in the empirical analysis consists of model-free implied volatility indices with 
30-day maturity. From the available model-free implied volatility indices we choose those that 
have available close and open prices for both the indices and the underlying assets for a sufficient 
period of time. The implied volatility indices that satisfy our criteria are presented in Table 1. The 
data were downloaded from Bloomberg and the common sample period is from 30/01/2012 until 
30/09/2022. The implied volatility indices used in the empirical analysis have, as underlying 
assets, major stock indices in the US market (S&P 500, Nasdaq-100 and Russell 2000) and some 
major stock indices outside US (Europe, Japan, Germany, Korea, Switzerland). In the empirical 
analysis we also employ implied volatility indices on stocks (Apple, Goldman Sachs, Google, 
IBM, and Amazon).

We begin our analysis by investigating the behavior of implied volatility changes on closeto-open and the open-to-close periods. The results are reported in Table 2. As in Chen et al., 2021 
we find that changes in volatility indices are on average positive during the close-to-open (nontrading) period and negative during the open-to-close (trading) period. The negative average 
changes of implied volatility during trading hours and the average positive changes during nontrading hours is an indication that trading helps to resolve the uncertainty that has accumulated 
during the non-trading period (see also Chen et al., 2021). 
The results are statistically significant in all cases. For the close-to-open (Co) period from 
the 14 implied volatility indices twelve are statistically significant at 1% level and two at the 5% 
significance level. For the open-to-close (oC) period, eleven results are statistically significant at 
1% level and three at 5% significance level. The results regarding their difference (Co-oC) are all 
statistically significant at the 1% level. Since the same results are observed across different markets 
worldwide, it reinforces the idea that the observed relation is associated with the different behavior 
of implied volatility indices (and option markets) during trading and nontrading hours and it is not 
due to VIX or U.S. market specific characteristic.

The close-to-open (non-trading hours) P&L return presents a clearer pattern as almost all

most results are insignificant. Monday’s close-to-open P&L is the weekend’s P&L from Friday’s

4.2.2. P&L on Expiration Days 
Next we examine the average variance swaps P&Ls at option expiration dates (third Friday 
of every month or if that day is a holiday the previous trading day). More specifically, we test if 
the variance swap P&L at expiration days is different from the average P&L during the 
corresponding month. For each calendar month, we subtract from the expiration day P&L the 
average P&L of the month and then we take the average of these differences. Table 5 reports the 
results.  
[Table 5] 
The P&L on expiration days is not significantly different from the monthly average P&L, 
except for two cases where the P&L on expiration days is more negative than the monthly average. 
We conclude that that daily variance swap returns do not present significantly different behavior 
on expiration days. The same also applies for the case of the overnight period and the intraday 
period. Overall, the results presented in Table 5 suggest that on expiration days the overnight and 
intraday variance risk premiums do not display significantly different behavior compared to all 
other days.

4.2.3. The effect of Variance-of-Variance 
In this section we test if the behavior of variance swap returns varies with the level of 
variance-of-variance. Currently there are two indices of implied volatility of implied volatility 
available, the VVix (implied volatility of VIX) and VVstoxx (the implied volatility of VSTOXX). 
We hypothesize that during periods of high implied volatility of implied volatility, the variance 
swap returns, and variance risk premiums may become more negative.  
First, we split into quartiles the values of VVix and VVstoxx. Then, per quartile, we 
determine the dates that these values occurred, and for these dates, we calculate the next day's P&L 
of the variance swap and decompose it into close-to-close, close-to-open, and open-to-close. We 
examine if higher values of vol-of-vol indices are leading to a higher or lower P&L of the variance 
swap of the corresponding index over the next day. The results are presented in Table 6.  
[Table 6] 
The results presented in Table 6 suggest that higher values of vol-of-vol indices are related 
to significantly more negative overnight P&Ls. More specifically, both in the case of VVix and 
VVstoxx the overnight P&L decreases almost monotonically from the 1st quartile to 4th quartile. 
The intraday P&L in most cases is not statistically significant across the different quartiles. These 
results suggest that the level uncertainty regarding variance risk is an important determinant of the 
overnight variance risk premium.

the 1 to 3 month forecasting horizons, while the overnight variance risk premium (close-to-open)

Previous studies in the literature argue that the variance risk premium is a proxy for 
representative agent’s risk aversion or alternatively a proxy for macroeconomic uncertainty (see, 
for example, Zhou (2018) and the references therein). Our empirical results suggest that the total 
variance risk premium contains mixed information. The intraday variance risk premium 
component appears to capture short-term risks and performs better at shorter prediction horizons. 
Conversely, the non-trading period component of the variance risk premium serves as a better 
return prediction variable for longer horizons and seems to contain different information, likely 
reflecting longer-term risks. 
 
6. Conclusion 
This paper uses the P&L of a hypothetical variance swap position and breaks down the 
variance risk premium into its overnight and intraday components to provide some further insights 
on the nature of the variance risk premium. In the empirical analysis we use implied variance stock 
indices in US, Europe and Asia and find that the variance risk premium switches sign between 
overnight and intraday periods. During the overnight period the variance risk premium is negative 

18 
 
and during the intraday trading period the variance risk premium becomes positive.  Our findings 
suggest that the negative variance risk premium reported in numerous previous studies is primarily 
driven by the overnight period variance risk premium component. We also evaluate the ability of 
the intraday and overnight variance risk premium to predict future equity returns. We find that that 
the intraday component captures short-term risk and displays predictive ability at 1–3-month 
horizons, while the overnight component reflects longer term risk and displays predictive ability 
at 6-12-month horizons.  
A promising avenue for future research is to incorporate overnight variance risk into 
equilibrium models of consumption risk and examine whether these models can successfully 
capture key characteristics of asset returns and option prices.
