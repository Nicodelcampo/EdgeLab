Abstract
This paper develops a non-linear rational expectations equilibrium
(REE) solution for a class of economies under both asymmetric information and trading constraints. It then analyzes the properties of this
equilibrium in a one-risky-asset economy with borrowing and shortsale
constraints. The model suggests that 1) price informativeness varies
with the price level; and 2) compared with an economy with borrowing
constraints and information asymmetry, the asymmetry in large price
movements is more pronounced in the presence of both constraints.
A dynamic implication of this result is that crashes (large downward
price movements) are formed much faster than bubbles (large upward
price movements).
Journal of Economic Literature Classiﬁcation Codes: D82, D84, G14.
Keywords: Rational Expectations Equilibrium, nonlinear REE, Trading Constraints, Shortsale Constraints, Borrowing Constraints, Asymmetric Information.

Introduction
Information asymmetry is often used to explain why investors trade assets
in the market. This literature has studies extensively how information is
transmitted and aggregated to equilibrium prices, as well as how prices affect beliefs and how uninformed investors extract information and interact
with informed investors. In reality, to trade assets, investors also face various market imperfections, including trading constraints such as shortsale
and margin constraints. However, to incorporate these empirically relevant
features in the literature on information asymmetry is diﬃcult. The reason is that, on one hand, in an equilibrium uninformed investors extract
information from prices, and their demand depends on the distribution of
equilibrium prices; on the other hand, equilibrium prices must equal supply and demand in the market, and the distribution of equilibrium prices
also depends on uninformed investors’ own demand.1 In general, the diﬃculty arises because the ﬁxed point condition is characterized by a non-linear
system of equations. More speciﬁcally, since uninformed investors extract
information from equilibrium prices, the equilibrium price function is needed
to solve the uninformed investor inference and demand problem. However,
it is diﬃcult to conjecture this function explicitly.2 Furthermore, if there are
multiple equilibrium solutions, one particular conjecture on an explicit form
of the equilibrium price function may not be suﬃcient. Without an explicit
function, the rational expectations equilibrium (REE) is a challenging and
complicated ﬁxed point problem that can be approached only by numerical
methods. However, numerical methods are diﬃcult to apply in the case of
multiple equilibria, since it is diﬃcult to numerically approximate multiple
equilibrium prices.3

In this paper, we provide an alternative characterization of the ﬁxed point
problem. More speciﬁcally, instead of conjecturing an explicit form of equilibrium price function, we conjecture an implicit one where the price is a
function of the state variables and the uninformed investor demand. This
characterization utilizes two facts: First, the informed investor demand does
not depend on the distribution of equilibrium prices; second, the uninformed
investor demand is included in their information set. This approach allows
the uninformed investor inference and optimal demand problem and the resulting equilibrium condition to be expressed as non-linear solutions to a set
of algebraic equations.
This approach contributes to the literature in many respects. First, our
implicit function approach to solve non-linear REEs can be applied to multiasset settings with any generalized distributions, quasiconcave preferences,
and convex constraint sets. Second, at a minimum, our alternative implicit
price function approach can be used to improve the numerical methods. That
is, instead of numerically approximating the price function or the signal space
to solve for the uninformed investor inference and optimization problem (as
in Romer (1993) and Bernardo and Judd (2000)) we solve the equilibrium
using a partial numerical approach.
In this approach, only the algebraic
equations that characterize the equilibrium condition need to be solved numerically, even in the presence of multiple equilibria since there is no need
to numerically approximate the price function. Third, in some setups, especially those commonly used in the literature where investors have constant
risk averse preferences, constraints are linearized, and uncertainties are assumed to be normally distributed (CARA-normal),4 our indirect approach
conjectures a linear implicit price function (i.e., linear in state variables).

To illustrate the generality of this non-linear rational expectations equilibrium approach, we develop an example of a security market under asymmetric information and trading constraints. More speciﬁcally, we analyze
the impact of two prevalent trading constraints in the ﬁnancial market: borrowing and shortsale constraints. Consistent with the margin requirements
and shortsale constraints observed in the ﬁnancial market, we specify both
constraints as restrictions on investors’ demand. Little work has been done
on how information is revealed by equilibrium prices in the presence of shortsale constraints,6 and even less in the area of borrowing constraints.7 Not
surprisingly, we ﬁnd that the intuitions derived from linear REE asymmetric
information models do not carry over to a world with trading constraints.
For example, in a linear REE, equilibrium prices reveal the same amount
of information regardless of levels. However, with trading constraints, price
informativeness varies with the price level.

In a separate paper, we have applied this methodology to a modiﬁed
CARA-normal setting where investors face borrowing constraints.8 The addition of shortsale constraints in this paper provides further interesting insights in the information transmission mechanism and price dynamics in the
presence of asymmetric information. In the previous example, we ﬁnd that
price movement is asymmetric: Large upward price movements are less likely
than large downward price movements. The reason is that when asset prices
are low, informed investors are borrowing constrained and prices are uninformative about the fundamental value of the assets. As a result, uninformed
investors may decrease their demand for the risky asset and require a large
information-disadvantaged premium to hold it when prices are low. Information asymmetry has a multiplier eﬀect on downward price movements and
may further exacerbate informed investors’ borrowing constraints.
One may conjecture that the asymmetric price movement arises from the
asymmetric eﬀect of borrowing constraints, which only bind when prices are
low. When investors face symmetric trading constraints, such as shortsales
and borrowing constraints, which bind when prices are either too high or too
low, price movements should be symmetric. Counter to this intuitive conjecture, we ﬁnd more pronounced asymmetric price movements when investors
face both constraints. When informed investors are shortsale constrained
due to high prices, prices again become uninformative. In this case, information asymmetry has a dampening eﬀect on the upward price movement
because uninformed investors regard the informativeness of the price signal
deteriorated and would decrease their demand for the asset due to adverse
selection concerns. Hence, large upward price movements become even less
likely. On the other hand, the multiplier eﬀect of borrowing constraints on
the downward price movement is much higher, since the the degree of uncertainty about price informativeness is much higher. Our analysis, therefore,
shows that asymmetric price movements are not caused by asymmetric eﬀects
of borrowing constraints, but by the interaction of information asymmetry
and trading constraints.
One dynamic implication of this static result is
that crashes (large downward price movements) are formed much faster than
bubbles (large upward price movements).

(1980) in two aspects. First, it includes multiple assets. Second, it includes
some trading-constrained investors. There are two dates, time 0 and time 1.

2.2
The Constraint
A unique feature of our model is the introduction of a price-dependent constraint set on informed investor demand for risky assets. Empirically, investors face various price-dependent trading constraints. For example, borrowing constraints typically arise when the stock price is low relative to the
fundamental. In this case, borrowing-constrained informed investors cannot
justify a holding position on a beaten-down stock to outside lenders. Their
borrowing capacity is tied to asset values. Hence, borrowing constraints can
be modeled as a restriction on informed investor demand that depends on
asset prices. Another commonly observed trading constraint on investor’s demand is the shortsale constraint. Both constraints capture what we observe
in reality. Consequently, studying such constraints is essential for an in-depth
understanding of the properties of asset prices and trading volumes.12

The standard solution method requires an explicit conjecture of the functional form of the equilibrium price P (es, em), a form that allows for a closedform solution to the uninformed investor inference problem. In a CARAnormal setting, a linear price function form is typically conjectured. However,
within a constrained REE, since P (es, em) is non-linear, the explicit function
form of P (es, em) is diﬃcult to conjecture. Moreover, it is diﬃcult to express
the uninformed investor inference and the optimization problem in a closed
form.
Two studies that use numerical approaches to represent a non-linear REE
are Romer (1993) and Bernardo and Judd (2000). Romer (1993) considers
an N × N grid of signal space (there are two signals in his model) and N
equally-spaced price values. He ﬁrst posits the initial values of investor demand at the points on these grids, then solves for their optimal holdings, and
ﬁnally looks for convergence. He approximates integrals numerically when
calculating expected utilities. Similarly, Bernardo and Judd (2000) present
a model with general taste, return distribution, and information structures.
Using a numerical technique, they approximate the price through polynomials and replace the conditional expectation with an inﬁnite number of
integration conditions to obtain moment conditions on the investor optimal
demand functions. In either paper, a numerical approximation to the explicit
equilibrium price function has to be obtained.
We propose an approach in the following section that uses a conjecture of
the implicit equilibrium price function to solve for the uninformed investor
belief and demand functions in an equilibrium. This indirect approach improves upon the existing numerical methods from several dimensions. First,
it circumvents the need to numerically approximate the explicit equilibrium
price functional form. The functional form of an implicit equilibrium price is
known since the informed investor optimal demand is known for a given signal and a given price. Second, numerical approaches are necessary only when
investors have general taste, return distribution, and information structures.
In the case when investors have constant absolute risk aversion preferences,
constraints are linearized, and state variables are normally distributed, uninformed investor demand can be characterized by an algebraic equation.

3.2
The Equilibrium Properties
To illustrate the intuition, we conduct a numerical analysis for an example
chosen to reﬂect “reasonable” parameters, where the risky asset is interpreted as a stock. We assume that 15% of investors are informed to reﬂect
the fact that institutional investors other than pension funds and insurance
companies hold about 15% of total market capitalization. We further assume that, among informed investors, a majority (in this case, 14%) are
trading-constrained. A close examination of equilibrium prices shows that
the fraction of investors and their risk tolerances are not separately identiﬁed.
For simplicity, we normalize investor risk tolerance to two. A key parameter
of the model is the quality of the information signal received by informed
investors. We assume that informed investors receive a high-quality signal:
the signal-to-noise ratio is 30. We also specify the parameters that describe
noise trading (σm and m) and the underlying fundamental asset (σv and v).
These parameters are chosen to equate the expected return on the risky security to 6% and the standard deviation to 40%.18 A second key parameter
is the borrowing constraint. Since informed investors are also shortsale constrained, a and b are chosen so that there are price regions where informed
investors are either borrowing or shortsale constrained and to reﬂect the fact
that most investors face a 30% margin constraint.
Yuan (2004) has shown the equilibrium price has an asymmetric distribution when some informed investors face borrowing constraints. One may
conjecture that the asymmetric price movements arises from the asymmetric
eﬀect of borrowing constraints which only bind when prices are low. In Figure 1, we show that the price distribution is asymmetric even when informed
investors face symmetric trading constraints (i.e., shortsale constraints bind
when prices are extremely high and borrowing constraints bind when prices
are extremely low.).
Figure 2 shows that the asymmetric price movement is due to the interaction between information asymmetry and trading constraints. Prices are less
informative when informed investors are constrained out of the market and
unable to transmit their signals to prices. Hence, in the low price region where
informed investors are possibly borrowing constrained, uninformed investors
demand an information-disadvantaged premium and demand less, compared
with that in the standard Grossman-Stiglitz setup when informed investors

are unconstrained. This is due to the deterioration of the informativeness of
the public signal (i.e., price) and resulting adverse selection concerns. The information asymmetry between informed and uninformed investors multiplies
the eﬀect of borrowing constraints and exacerbates downward price movements. In this case, upward price movements in the high price region are
not aﬀected since borrowing constraints are not binding.
However, when
informed investors face both borrowing and shortsale constraints, Figure 2
shows that uninformed investors demand less in both high and low price
regions. This is because in both price regions informed investors are possibly constrained out of the market (either by shortsale or by borrowing
constraints) and uninformed investors are facing less informative prices. In
the high price region, information asymmetry plays a dampening eﬀect on
upward price movements. The degree of asymmetry in the price distribution,
hence, is much higher when both trading constraints are present.
Figures 3 and 4 show that the equilibrium price is a nonlinear function
of state variables (es, em) when informed investors are trading-constrained.
Moreover, compared with the case when informed investors face only borrowing constraints, Figure 3 shows that when informed investors are subject
to one more constraint – the shortsale constraint, prices are less responsive
to signal shocks, while Figure 4 shows that prices are more susceptible to
supply shocks, especially in the high price region. The ﬁrst ﬁnding indicates
that less information is transmitted to prices both when prices are high and
informed investors are possibly shortsale-constrained and when prices are low
and informed investors are possibly borrowing-constrained. Hence, the level
of price informativeness varies nonlinearly with prices. The second ﬁnding indicates that the ability of informed investors to arbitrage the price diﬀerences
arisen from the noise trading is much limited when they are facing shortsale
and borrowing constraints. The lower (higher) sensitivity to signal (supply)
shocks indicate that the degree of uncertainty about price informativeness
is much higher when informed investors face more trading constraints since
informed investors could be constrained in diﬀerent ways: either by shortsale
or borrowing constraints.

Concluding Remarks
This study explores an alternative characterization of the rational expectations equilibrium in securities markets with both asymmetric information and
15

trading constraints. To demonstrate the generality of this alternative nonlinear REE characterization, we also develop an example based on a setup
that is commonly used in the literature where investors have constant absolute risk averse utility functions and state variables are normally distributed,
with one additional feature – informed investors are borrowing and shortsale
constrained. Our analysis shows that price informativeness varies with the
price level in a non-linear REE. Our example also has interesting empirical
implications. For example, it predicts pronounced asymmetric price movements with symmetric trading constraints. Prices become less informative
when informed investors are constrained, be it borrowing or shortsale constrained. Since less informative prices cause uninformed investors to demand
an information-disadvantaged premium, this results in a multiplier eﬀect on
the downward price movement but a dampening eﬀect on the upward price
movement. One dynamic implication of this static result is that crashes (large
downward price movements) are formed much faster than bubbles (large upward price movements), which we will explore further in a future research.
