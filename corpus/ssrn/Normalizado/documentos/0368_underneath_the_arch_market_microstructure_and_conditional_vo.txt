Abstract 
 
Our understanding of risk, typically measured as return volatility, is central to the function of 
asset markets.  From a simple representation of the value process for a stock we show how 
market microstructure might influence conditional volatility in returns.  This admits effects that 
have borne significant empirical evidence. The model also illustrates however that these might 
be governed through market microstructure and in particular liquidity, spreads and the rate at 
which price adjusts to order flow. Implications for the specification of empirical models are 
discussed.  We also inform the development of theory, including a model specification for less 
restrictive value process for the underlying asset.

1. INTRODUCTION 
 
An extensive literature documents conditional volatility in the returns to financial and other 
assets.  The strength of these empirical findings is important as volatility is the common means 
by which risk is measured and is therefore central to determining the required return and, by 
association, the price of assets.  Models for asset pricing largely rely on the assumption of a 
consistent relationship between the second moments in returns.  Where the volatility in returns 
is conditional on past values or economic effects there is therefore the possibility that asset 
pricing models are unreliable if they fail to account for these effects.   
The importance of understanding risk is reflected in the substantial body of work that has 
followed the seminal contributions of Engle (1982) and later Bollerslev (1986).  Initial 
empirical approaches sought to model the univariate properties of return volatility, with later 
contributions examining the relationship between this and economic variables. A 
comprehensive overview of this literature is provided by Bauwens et al (2006). 
There are a number of plausible, empirically verified, explanations for conditional volatility.  
General economic conditions may lead to periods of relatively high volatility and stocks may 
respond differently to these (see among others, Christiansen et al, (2012) and Salisu et al 
(2022)). Where a stock price falls a leverage effect may also increase the volatility in 
shareholder returns.  Research has also sought to address the influence of market 
microstructure on volatility, although this literature is less extensive.  Anderson (1996) models 
return volatility as a function of trading volume, noting that information flows, spreads and 
market liquidity might be expected to affect liquidity. 

4 
 
To address this intuition more directly. After Amihud (2002) and later Florakis et al (2011) we 
expect illiquid markets to respond more to any given trade.  This would imply a greater 
volatility in asset returns and therefore an effect to consider in the modelling of conditional 
volatility1.  In examining the price discovery process we argue that spreads reduce order flow 
and may therefore have an effect on volatility.  We note that prior research has considered the 
relationship between market microstructure and volatility but what remains is theoretical 
representation of the relationship. 
The model presented includes a measure for volatility in the underlying asset value, changes 
in which might reflect much of the existing ARCH family of models brief discussed above.   
 
2. RELATION TO THE LITERATURE 
2.1. Market Microstructure 
Efficient markets will adjust prices rapidly to reflect changes in the latent demand for an asset as 
new information arrives on its fundamental value.  The trading environment that mediates this 
process was named market microstructure by Garman (1976).  As noted in a review by Biais et al 
(2005) prices may deviate from fundamental values as market microstructure admits frictions and 
strategic behaviour prompted by information asymmetry or market power.  In contributing to these 
market frictions bid-ask spreads have been found, theoretically and empirically to influence stock 
returns.  Roll (1984) presents the argument that if spread is set around a constant mean as 
successive trades are equally likely to fall above or below the mean, negative autocorrelation is 
the outcome.  This result is predicated on an informationally efficient market but there is a wealth 
of empirical evidence employing the approach both in seeking to understand short term returns

and estimating the spread, see for example Glosten and Harris (1988).  A further approach to 
understanding the effect of spreads is to consider information events with trades recorded in 
batches, as in Kyle (1985).  In this case we argue that spreads impede the price discovery process, 
resulting in positive autocorrelation in returns. To illustrate consider a probability distribution for 
trades by investors experiencing heterogeneous shocks centred on a fundamental value (set equal 
to 100 in Figure 1).  For ease of exposition if the value shifts by one standard deviation with a 
spread the same then the shaded area in Figure 1 indicates the reduced probability of purchase 
orders as a result of the spread (in this case equal to approximately 15%).  This is asymmetric and 
therefore induces a latent demand for the asset and positive autocorrelation in returns.   
The counter to this argument is that market makers would not make systematic errors in pricing, 
rather adjusting the response of price to order flow, denoted by λ in Kyle (1985).  A countervailing 
effect however is that the absolute size of the order flow is reduced relative to the market 
microstructure noise and so the informational content is lower, reducing the incentive to adjust 
price more rapidly.  Further, in practice spreads are not held constant and so the market maker then 
has to estimate the effect of the spread on order flow dynamically if prices are to fully adjust.  Note 
that the preceding is driven by the change in value and would still allow for a bid-ask bounce.

Evidence for positive autocorrelation in returns over longer time periods is reported empirically.  
Carhart (1997) for example notes a momentum effect in returns to mutual funds, suggesting 
transactions costs as an explanation.   This builds on the preceding work from authors including 
Hendricks and Zeckhauser (1993) who note ‘Hot Hands’ in mutual fund performance. 
 
2.2. Conditional Volatility 
Models that directly relate market microstructure to conditional volatility are rare, especially in 
the context of an extensive empirical literature.  As noted above an exception is the work of 
Anderson (1996) reflecting on a literature suggesting that volume and volatility are jointly 
determined.   As information arrives trading informs price discovery as the market moves towards 

7 
 
a temporary equilibrium.  In this setting the variance is a function of the intensity of trading, with 
spreads increasing when a market maker infers that an informed investor trades.  Empirical tests 
confirm persistence in, jointly determined, volume and volatility.   
Further work has sought to examine the relationship between aspects of market microstructure and 
volatility.  Bollerslev and Melvin (1994) report a positive effect of volatility on spreads in foreign 
exchange markets.  In contrast McGroarty et al (2006) offer market microstructure explanations 
for changes in spread and volatility for five currency pairs, reporting that factors including price 
clustering and discreteness are able to explain changes in spreads.  The mixture of distribution 
hypothesis discussed in Anderson (1996) is also applied by Khemiri (2012) in foreign exchange 
markets, although adapted to consider regime switches.  The confirmation of intuition that signed 
order flow determines prices whilst unsigned determines volatility leads to the suggestion that 
more work on the determinants of order flow is warranted. 
The relationship between market microstructure and volatility may differ a little when one moves 
from foreign exchange to equity markets, although this may be more a matter of degree.  In 
modelling high frequency data Engle (2000) finds that volatility is negatively affected by longer 
durations, concluding that asymmetric information consistent with market microstructure models 
offers an explanation for the effect.  Again in the context of high frequency data, Maganelli (2005) 
extends the work, developing an empirical specification that simultaneously considers the duration 
between trades, volume and returns (including variance).  Trading volumes are found to cluster 
and more frequently traded stocks reach equilibrium prices more rapidly.  This appears as a 
consequence, in part, of informed traders being more active for more liquid stocks.  Volatility 
interacts in this work, although volume and duration are found to have a strong effect only for 

8 
 
frequently traded stocks.  Suggested developments include the addition of depth and spread as 
explanatory variables. 
Wu and Xu (2006) consider the effects of transactions volume and imbalance as a means to 
measure the information content of trades.  They find these measures to carry a significant 
explanatory power over return volatility, in addition to that offered by variables measuring trade 
frequency and size.  This offers further empirical evidence of a relationship between (persistent) 
return volatility and market microstructure.   
That there is a relationship makes strong intuitive sense however we argue that theoretical work in 
this area has focussed on empirical specifications. The preceding argument that spreads, viewed 
through the lens of microstructure noise, impedes the price discovery process forms the basis for 
a simple representation of the effects on volatility.  In doing so we argue that there are insights for 
our general understanding of how risk evolves in markets and the approach to modelling this 
empirically.  In particular we find that market microstructure effects may interact non-linearly in 
influencing volatility.

4. Conclusions 
We offer a first formal model for a relationship between market microstructure and ARCH effects. 
This strengthens existing, plausible, arguments for conditional volatility in asset markets but in 
addition suggests further avenues for both theoretical and empirical research.  Intuition is that 

19 
 
noisy thin markets that respond more slowly to news and have larger spreads are expected to show 
bigger ARCH effects.  If spread, the rate of price adjustment to order flow and liquidity remain 
constant results suggest that existing approaches to estimating conditional volatility are 
appropriate.  We would however argue finding these effects are multiplicative implies that during 
periods of increased market volatility existing empirical approaches may underestimate ARCH 
effects.   There are consequent implications for understanding tail risks.   
Avenues for further work derive from the findings.  We have presented a model in an appendix for 
an asset value that follows a geometric Brownian motion.  The result for volatility is growing in 
time.  Solutions to this might be derived from a different time dependent price adjustment process, 
in essence one that drives stationarity in returns.  The preceding model sought to demonstrate a 
theoretical cause for ARCH effects, adopting a simple approach less likely to produce a 
relationship than a random walk for the underlying value.  Empirically the findings argue for a 
non-linear model for the ARCH family of models.  The basis for this is presented in the preceding.
