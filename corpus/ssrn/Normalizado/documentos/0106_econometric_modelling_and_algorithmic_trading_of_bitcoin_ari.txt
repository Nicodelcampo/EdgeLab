Applied Econometrics and Algorithmic Trading 
 
Part I: Financial Time Series Data Analysis. 
 I picked Bitcoin (BTC-USD) for this, since it's the biggest cryptocurrency. 
Why Bitcoin? 
• Its price jumps around a lot.  
• It trades all the time (unlike stocks).  
• Its volatility is easy to spot.  
• Its returns don't follow a normal pattern.   
• It's good for checking if data is stationary. 
Bitcoin gives us a good chance to look at financial time series stuff. This paper looks 
at Bitcoin's (BTC-USD) time series features. The goal is to look at old daily prices, 
figure out returns, run tests, and check the data for things like volatility and unusual 
distributions. 
2. Data Collection and Cleaning. 
I got Bitcoin's (BTC-USD) daily price history from Yahoo Finance using Python and the 
yfinance tool. The data is from January 2025 to January 2026. I used closing prices to 
get daily percentage returns. I got rid of missing numbers and cleaned up the data 
before starting. 
3. Price and Return Visualization. 

 
 
 
 
Bitcoin's price goes up and down a lot over time. This shows it's not stationary, 
which is normal for assets. Returns go up and down around zero, with times of high 
and low volatility. Returns seem stationary, and volatility jumps out. 
 
The return series fluctuates around zero with visible clusters of high volatility. This 
suggests stationarity in returns and evidence of volatility clustering.

Volatility clustering analysis. 

 
 
 
The squared return plot shows periods of high volatility followed by high volatility 
and calm periods followed by calm periods. This confirms the presence of volatility 
clustering, a well-known stylized fact in financial markets.

The autocorrelation plot of returns shows minimal significant lags, suggesting weak 
linear dependence. However, autocorrelation in squared returns is significant, 
reinforcing evidence of time-varying volatility. 
Stylized Facts Discussion. 
The analysis confirms several stylized facts of financial time series: 
• 
Asset prices are non-stationary. 
• 
Returns are stationary. 
• 
Returns exhibit fat tails. 
• 
Volatility clustering is present. 
• 
Linear autocorrelation in returns is weak. 
These findings are consistent with financial econometric theory and support the use 
of GARCH-type models for volatility modelling. 
To sum it up, Bitcoin acts like other financial time series, with prices, returns, outliers 
and volatility jumps. Knowing these things helps in managing risk and predicting 
prices. 
 
Part II. Time Series Modelling and Forecasting. 
The two models implemented are: 
• 
ARIMA 
• 
GARCH 
Together, these models address both the linear autocorrelation structure and the 
heteroskedastic variance dynamics inherent in Bitcoin's return series. 
Model 1: ARIMA 
Justification of Model Selection. 

 
 
The Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model is the go-to for 
financial time series. Because Part I says that Bitcoin log-returns are stationary (ADF 
p < 0.001), I don't need to difference the data, so d = 0. I picked the model using the 
Box-Jenkins way, looking at the ACF and PACF plots. 
• 
The ACF shows a significant spike at lag 1 followed by rapid decay, suggesting 
an MA (1) component. 
• 
The PACF similarly shows a significant spike at lag 1, suggesting an AR (1) 
component. 
ARIMA was selected as the optimal specification, confirmed by the lowest AIC value 
across competing orders tested. 
Parameter Estimation 
 
The model was estimated using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).  
The AR (1) coefficient of 0.1805 indicates a moderate positive relationship between 
today's return and yesterday's return. The  
MA (1) coefficient of –0.2725 captures a corrective effect from the previous period's 
shock. Both parameters are statistically significant, confirming the chosen 
specification is appropriate.

Model 2: GARCH. 
Justification of Model Selection 
The Generalised Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (GARCH) model, 
from Bollerslev (1986), models conditional volatility that changes over time. The 
ARCH-LM test on ARIMA leftovers showed changed volatility, making GARCH a good 
idea. GARCH (1,1) is good enough and beats fancier models for most returns (Hansen 
& Lunde, 2005). Student-t errors are better than Gaussian, dealing with the fat tails 
from Part I and the Jarque-Bera test. 
 

 
 
The conditional variance equation is: 
σ²ₜ = ω + α · ε²ₜ₋₁ + β · σ²ₜ₋₁ 
Where ω captures the long-run variance, α measures sensitivity to recent shocks 
(ARCH effect), and β measures persistence of past volatility (GARCH effect).

accurately calibrated forecast intervals than either model individually, since it 
accounts for both autocorrelation in returns and heteroskedasticity in volatility 
simultaneously. 
Part III. Algorithmic Trading Strategy Design. 
This section designs, implements, and evaluates two algorithmic trading strategies 
applied to Bitcoin (BTC-USD) over the period January 2025 to January 2026. The two 
strategies selected represent contrasting market philosophies: 
• 
Strategy 1: Trend-Following — Moving Average (MA) Crossover 
• 
Strategy 2: Mean-Reversion — Relative Strength Index (RSI) 
Strategy 1: Trend-Following (Moving Average Crossover) 
Strategy Logic and Justification. 
Trend-following is one of the oldest and most widely validated strategies in 
quantitative finance. The core premise is that assets which have been rising tend to 
continue rising, and assets which have been falling tend to continue falling. The 
Moving Average Crossover strategy operationalises this by comparing a short-term 
and long-term moving average to identify momentum shifts. 
Bitcoin is well-suited to trend-following strategies given its history of extended bull 
and bear market cycles, as confirmed by the non-stationary price behaviour 
identified in Part I. 
Entry and Exit Rules 

 
 
Rule 
Condition 
Entry (Buy) 20-day MA crosses above 50-day MA (Golden Cross) 
Exit (Sell) 
20-day MA crosses below 50-day MA (Death Cross) 
Position 
Long only (100% of capital deployed per signal) 
A Golden Cross occurs when short-term momentum accelerates above the longerterm trend, signalling the start of an upward move. A Death Cross signals the reverse 
and triggers an exit to avoid further losses. 
 Position Sizing Logic 
A full capital allocation approach is used: when a buy signal is triggered, 100% of 
available capital is deployed into Bitcoin. When the exit signal is triggered, the full 
position is liquidated. This binary positioning approach is appropriate for a singleasset strategy and simplifies performance attribution.

 Assumptions and Limitations 
The MA Crossover strategy assumes that trends are detectable and persistent 
enough to be exploited after accounting for transaction costs. It performs poorly in 
sideways or choppy markets, where frequent false crossovers (whipsaws) generate 
repeated small losses. The 20/50 day MA window combination was selected based 
on common industry practice; alternative window lengths could improve 
performance but risk overfitting to the historical period. Additionally, the strategy is 
purely price-based and ignores fundamental factors, news events, and on-chain 
Bitcoin metrics that may influence price direction. 

 
 
 
 
 
Strategy 2: Mean-Reversion (RSI Strategy). 
Strategy Logic and Justification 
Mean-reversion strategies operate on the premise that asset prices tend to revert to 
their historical average following extreme moves. The Relative Strength Index (RSI), 
developed by Welles Wilder (1978), measures the speed and magnitude of recent 
price changes to identify overbought and oversold conditions. 
While Bitcoin exhibits strong trending behaviour over longer horizons, short-term 
price movements often overshoot, creating mean-reversion opportunities at daily 
frequencies. The RSI strategy is designed to exploit these short-term overreactions 
— buying after sharp sell-offs and exiting after sharp rallies. 

 
 
Entry and Exit Rules 
Rule 
Condition 
Entry (Buy) 
RSI(14) drops below 30 — oversold signal 
Exit (Sell) 
RSI(14) rises above 70 — overbought signal 
Position 
Long only (100% of capital per signal) 
The 14-period RSI is the standard setting as originally specified by Wilder. An RSI 
below 30 indicates that Bitcoin has been sold excessively relative to recent history, 
creating a potential buying opportunity. An RSI above 70 indicates the reverse and 
triggers an exit. 
Position Sizing Logic 
As with Strategy 1, a full capital allocation approach is used per signal. The RSI 
strategy trades more frequently than the MA strategy — 42 trades over the year 
versus 13 — reflecting its shorter-term, higher-frequency nature.

Bitcoin's volatility (confirmed at ~1.87%/day in Part II) makes wider stops necessary 
to avoid premature exits on normal daily fluctuations.

Assumptions and Limitations 
The RSI strategy assumes that extreme readings reliably predict short-term reversals. 
This assumption can fail during strong trending markets — in a sustained uptrend, 
RSI can remain above 70 for extended periods without a reversal, and in a sustained 
downtrend, RSI can remain below 30, leading to repeated losing entries (the socalled "falling knife" problem). Additionally, the RSI thresholds of 30/70 are fixed 
parameters; in practice, dynamic thresholds or adaptive RSI windows may perform 
better. The high trade frequency (42 trades) also increases exposure to slippage — 
the difference between expected and actual execution prices — which is not fully 
captured by the flat 0.1% cost assumption.

strategy signals, even within a single asset.

Walk-Forward Analysis 
In addition to the static in-sample/out-of-sample split, a walk-forward analysis was

generating 21 independent test windows across the full dataset. This approach

periodically as new data becomes available, rather than fitting once and holding 
parameters fixed forever.

Critical Analysis of Backtesting Limitations 
Overfitting 
Overfitting occurs when a strategy is excessively tuned to historical data, capturing 
noise rather than genuine signal. The gap between in-sample and out-of-sample 

 
 
performance is the primary indicator of overfitting. For the MA strategy, the total 
return dropped from +32.70% in-sample to –3.61% out-of-sample — a deterioration 
of 36.31 percentage points. For the RSI strategy, the drop was from +44.45% to 
+1.60% — a deterioration of 42.85 percentage points. 
These performance gaps indicate a meaningful degree of overfitting, particularly for 
the RSI strategy. The MA windows (20/50 days) and RSI thresholds (30/70) were 
selected based on industry convention rather than in-sample optimisation, which 
partially mitigates overfitting concerns. However, had these parameters been gridsearched for optimal historical performance, the overfitting risk would be 
substantially higher and the out-of-sample deterioration likely worse. 
 Look-Ahead Bias 
Look-ahead bias occurs when a strategy inadvertently uses information that would 
not have been available at the time of trading — for example, using tomorrow's 
closing price to generate today's signal. In this backtest, all signals are generated 
using data available at the close of each trading day and executed at the same day's 
closing price. A more conservative approach would assume execution at the next 
day's open price, which would slightly reduce reported returns due to overnight 
price gaps. This is a minor but acknowledged limitation of the current 
implementation. 
 Survivorship Bias 
Survivorship bias typically applies to multi-asset strategies where only currently 
existing assets are included in the test universe, excluding those that failed or were 
delisted. Since this analysis focuses exclusively on Bitcoin — which has survived and 
grown substantially since 2020 — survivorship bias is less directly applicable. 
However, a related concern is asset selection bias: Bitcoin was selected partly 
because it is known to be a highly volatile, trend-exhibiting asset well-suited to these 
strategies. Applying the same strategies to a randomly selected cryptocurrency — 
many of which have lost 90%+ of their value — would likely produce substantially 
worse results. 

 
 
Model Risk 
Model risk refers to the possibility that the models themselves are misspecified or 
rely on assumptions that do not hold in live trading. Several model risks are present 
in this backtest. First, transaction costs are modelled as a flat 0.1% per trade, 
ignoring slippage — the difference between the expected and actual execution 
price, which can be significant for large Bitcoin orders or during periods of low 
liquidity. Second, the strategies assume perfect execution at daily closing prices, 
which is not guaranteed in live markets. Third, both strategies use fixed parameters 
across the full test period, ignoring the possibility that optimal parameters shift 
across different market regimes — as evidenced by the stark difference in Bitcoin's 
behaviour between the 2024 bull market (+138.30% Buy & Hold) and the 2025 
corrective phase (–17.21% Buy & Hold).

Proposed Improvements 
Several enhancements could improve strategy robustness and real-world 
applicability. First, integrating the GARCH (1,1) volatility forecasts from Part II into 
position sizing — reducing exposure during high-volatility regimes and increasing it 
during calm periods — would create a volatility-adjusted strategy more resilient 
across market conditions. Second, applying a regime filter (such as a 200-day moving 
average trend filter) before entering RSI signals would prevent the "falling knife" 
problem of buying into sustained downtrends. Third, conducting Monte Carlo 
simulation on the backtest returns would quantify the range of possible outcomes 
and provide confidence intervals around reported performance metrics. Finally, 
expanding to a portfolio of cryptocurrencies (e.g., BTC, ETH, SOL) would provide 
diversification benefits and reduce dependence on Bitcoin-specific market 
conditions.

# ── Backtest Functions ──────────────────────────────────────── 
def backtest_ma(data, short_w=20, long_w=50, sl=0.02, tp=0.05): 
    d = data.copy() 
    d['MA_s'] = d['Close'].rolling(short_w).mean() 
    d['MA_l'] = d['Close'].rolling(long_w).mean() 
    d['Sig']  = np.where(d['MA_s'] > d['MA_l'], 1, -1) 
    capital = INITIAL_CAPITAL; cap_list = [] 
    pos = 0; ep = 0; wins = 0; losses = 0 
    for i in range(len(d)): 
        price = float(d['Close'].iloc[i]) 
        sig   = int(d['Sig'].iloc[i]) 
        if pos == 1 and ep > 0: 
            pnl = (price - ep) / ep 
            if pnl <= -sl: 
                capital *= (1 + pnl - TRANSACTION_COST); losses += 1; pos = 0; ep = 0 
            elif pnl >= tp: 
                capital *= (1 + pnl - TRANSACTION_COST); wins += 1; pos = 0; ep = 0 
        if sig == 1 and pos == 0: 
            pos = 1; ep = price; capital *= (1 - TRANSACTION_COST) 
        elif sig != 1 and pos == 1: 
            pnl = (price - ep) / ep 
            capital *= (1 + pnl - TRANSACTION_COST) 
            if pnl > 0: wins += 1 
            else: losses += 1 
            pos = 0; ep = 0 
        cap_list.append(capital * (1 + (price-ep)/ep) if pos == 1 and ep > 0 else capital) 

 
 
    equity   = pd.Series(cap_list, index=d.index) 
    ret      = (equity.iloc[-1] - INITIAL_CAPITAL) / INITIAL_CAPITAL * 100 
    dr       = equity.pct_change().dropna() 
    sharpe   = (dr.mean() / dr.std()) * np.sqrt(365) if dr.std() > 0 else 0 
    max_dd   = ((equity - equity.cummax()) / equity.cummax()).min() * 100 
    bh       = (float(d['Close'].iloc[-1]) - float(d['Close'].iloc[0])) / float(d['Close'].iloc[0]) * 100 
    total_t  = wins + losses 
    win_rate = wins / total_t * 100 if total_t > 0 else 0 
    vol      = dr.std() * np.sqrt(365) * 100 
    return {'equity': equity, 'total_return': ret, 'sharpe': sharpe, 
            'max_dd': max_dd, 'bh_return': bh, 'win_rate': win_rate, 
            'trades': total_t, 'volatility': vol, 
            'calmar': abs(ret / max_dd) if max_dd != 0 else 0} 
 
def backtest_rsi(data, period=14, oversold=30, overbought=70, sl=0.02, tp=0.04):

    loss  = (-delta.where(delta < 0, 0)).rolling(period).mean() 
    d['RSI'] = 100 - (100 / (1 + gain / loss)) 
    d['Sig'] = np.where(d['RSI'] < oversold, 1, np.where(d['RSI'] > overbought, -1, 0)) 
    capital = INITIAL_CAPITAL; cap_list = [] 
    pos = 0; ep = 0; wins = 0; losses = 0 
    for i in range(len(d)): 
        price = float(d['Close'].iloc[i]) 
        sig   = int(d['Sig'].iloc[i]) 
        if pos == 1 and ep > 0: 
            pnl = (price - ep) / ep 
            if pnl <= -sl: 
                capital *= (1 + pnl - TRANSACTION_COST); losses += 1; pos = 0; ep = 0 

 
 
            elif pnl >= tp: 
                capital *= (1 + pnl - TRANSACTION_COST); wins += 1; pos = 0; ep = 0 
        if sig == 1 and pos == 0: 
            pos = 1; ep = price; capital *= (1 - TRANSACTION_COST) 
        elif sig == -1 and pos == 1: 
            pnl = (price - ep) / ep 
            capital *= (1 + pnl - TRANSACTION_COST) 
            if pnl > 0: wins += 1 
            else: losses += 1 
            pos = 0; ep = 0 
        cap_list.append(capital * (1 + (price-ep)/ep) if pos == 1 and ep > 0 else capital) 
    equity   = pd.Series(cap_list, index=d.index) 
    ret      = (equity.iloc[-1] - INITIAL_CAPITAL) / INITIAL_CAPITAL * 100 
    dr       = equity.pct_change().dropna() 
    sharpe   = (dr.mean() / dr.std()) * np.sqrt(365) if dr.std() > 0 else 0 
    max_dd   = ((equity - equity.cummax()) / equity.cummax()).min() * 100

    win_rate = wins / total_t * 100 if total_t > 0 else 0
