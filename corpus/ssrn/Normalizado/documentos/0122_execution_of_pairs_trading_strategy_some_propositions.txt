Abstract
When two equities share a long-run co-movement relationship, the ratio of their price
sensitivities (the hedge ratio) determines the directional exposure of a pairs trading position. In
practice hedge ratios drift as macroeconomic regimes shift, rendering static estimates stale. This
paper characterises how the mode of hedge ratio estimation shapes the risk–return profile of
pairs trading strategies, rather than asking whether pairs trading is profitable in aggregate. Two
hypotheses are tested with econometric discipline: does Kalman-filtered dynamic estimation
statistically reduce tail-risk relative to static OLS (H1), and does a two-state Hidden Markov
Model (HMM) regime filter further reduce drawdown (H2)?
We evaluate seven estimation branches on 50 S&P 500 equities across the structurally
demanding 2020–2024 window, which compresses four macroeconomic regimes (COVID-19
shock, quantitative easing recovery, Federal Reserve rate-hike cycle, and AI-driven sector
concentration) into five calendar years. This window is an intentionally selected high-instability
evaluation period; results should be interpreted as conditional on this environment and may
not generalise to calmer or longer samples. A walk-forward cross-validation design with 14
rolling out-of-sample folds prevents in-sample overfitting; BCa bootstrap confidence intervals
on the annualised Sharpe ratio and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on fold-level differences provide
formal inference.
In this demanding environment, all seven branches produce negative net-of-cost fold-mean
Sharpe ratios, confirming that 28-bps round-trip transaction costs consume gross alpha at the
2.3-day average holding periods generated by the Kalman branches. Static OLS achieves a foldmean Sharpe of −0.157 versus −2.080 for Kalman (OLS dominant in 12 of 14 folds), driven
1

by the Kalman filter’s 54% higher trade turnover (932 vs. 607 pooled trades) that amplifies
cost drag. Despite lower net Sharpe, Kalman reduces fold-mean maximum drawdown by 71%
relative to OLS (−0.31% vs. −1.07%), a dissociation of return from risk that is the paper’s
central empirical finding. For H2, HMM conditioning further reduces fold-mean MaxDD by
30% (to −0.22%, Wilcoxon p < 0.001) while cutting trade frequency by 45%. A mechanism
decomposition (Branch D) shows Kalman’s drawdown advantage is consistent with a structural
explanation: short holding periods alone do not replicate it; a posterior variance entry gate
(Branch F) partially repairs signal quality (52.7%→54.8% win rate) and improves fold-mean
Sharpe to −1.38. The estimated Kalman–OLS cost breakeven is 6–7 bps one-way, suggesting
that Kalman may be competitive at direct-market-access execution costs while retaining its
drawdown advantage.

1
Introduction
The long-run co-movement of equity prices within a sector reflects durable economic complementarities: shared input costs, correlated demand shocks, and common exposure to industry-level
technological change. Pairs trading (the strategy of exploiting transient deviations from this comovement) has been profitable over multi-decade horizons (Gatev et al., 2006) and generates returns
largely orthogonal to standard factor premia (Avellaneda and Lee, 2010). Yet every implementation
of pairs trading encodes a hedging assumption: the ratio β of long-to-short position sizes is treated
as a fixed parameter estimated on a training window and held constant throughout the holding
period.
This assumption is more fragile than it appears. The 2020–2024 period compressed four structurally
distinct macroeconomic regimes into five calendar years: a pandemic demand shock, two years
of near-zero monetary policy, an aggressive rate-hike cycle, and concentrated AI-sector price
appreciation. Over any such multi-regime window, the true co-integration relationship between
two equities shifts: input cost correlations change as supply chains restructure, demand elasticities
evolve as industry boundaries blur, and capital structure adjustments alter financial leverage ratios.
A static hedge ratio ˆβ estimated on a 252-day training window is simply an average of these shifting
relationships, not a reliable description of the current one.
The natural remedy is a state-space model in which βt evolves as a random walk and is tracked
online via the Kalman filter (Chan, 2013). The filter’s appeal is that it automatically discounts
stale training data, adapts the hedge ratio to current co-movement structure, and requires only one
additional hyperparameter δ (the process-noise scalar). Despite this, the empirical literature on
equity pairs trading has not subjected the Kalman approach to formal statistical inference on broad
cross-sections of equities with walk-forward validation.
A second layer of adaptation arises at the regime level. Even a perfectly estimated βt generates poor
signals when the spread itself is in a non-stationary regime: periods of elevated spread volatility,
weakened autocorrelation, or deteriorating cointegration stability indicate that new long-short
entries carry disproportionate risk. A two-state Hidden Markov Model fitted on pair-specific spread
features can identify such turbulent regimes and suppress new entries, reducing drawdown without
necessarily sacrificing return.
1.1
Scope
We study how hedge ratio estimation method affects the risk–return profile of pairs trading under
extreme structural instability. All net-of-cost strategies in our 2020–2024 sample produce negative
fold-mean Sharpe ratios: we do not claim this method is profitable at institutional 28-bps round-trip
5

costs. Rather, the paper’s contribution is to characterise the risk–return dissociation (the finding that
Kalman estimation degrades Sharpe while simultaneously reducing drawdown) and to decompose
the mechanism behind it. The 2020–2024 window is an intentionally selected high-instability
evaluation period; readers should treat the quantitative results as conditional on this environment
and consult calmer-period evidence before generalising.
Why negative-Sharpe results constitute a positive contribution.
A natural question is whether
findings where all strategies produce negative net-of-cost Sharpe ratios are publishable on their
own terms. We argue they are, for five reasons. First, gross alpha is positive for all seven
branches (Appendix E): the strategies generate real pre-cost returns, and the negative net Sharpe
is a statement about the cost-frequency interaction, not about the absence of signal. Second,
the risk-return dissociation (Kalman degrades Sharpe while simultaneously reducing maximum
drawdown by 71%) is not a null result; it is a precise characterisation of the trade-off that any
implementation decision entails. Third, the mechanism decomposition (Branch D) and signal
quality analysis (Branch F) identify the proximate causes of the dissociation, producing actionable
guidance: the Kalman filter should be framed as a risk-management overlay, and a posterior
variance entry gate partially repairs the signal quality paradox. Fourth, the cost-breakeven estimate
(6–7 bps one-way) provides a concrete threshold below which Kalman may become competitive,
directly informing implementation decisions for direct-market-access practitioners. Fifth, the
2020–2024 period serves as an intentional stress test that establishes an upper bound on Kalman’s
Sharpe disadvantage: the extreme β-drift rates in this period maximise z-score contamination
(Equation 11) and minimise Kalman holding periods. In calmer samples where drift is slower, the
contamination term shrinks, holding periods lengthen, cost drag falls, and the framework predicts
Sharpe convergence or advantage for Kalman, a conjecture developed in Section 6. Together these
constitute a mechanistically decomposed characterisation of a practically important estimator choice,
independent of whether net Sharpe is positive in this sample.
1.2
Contributions
This paper makes five contributions.
1. Rigorous walk-forward evaluation of dynamic hedge ratio estimation. We compare
seven branches (static OLS A, Kalman B, Kalman+HMM C, OLS with short holding cap D,
adaptive-δ Kalman E, Kalman with posterior variance entry gate F, and a rolling-volatility
regime baseline G in Appendix H) under a 14-fold out-of-sample walk-forward design on
50 S&P 500 equities (2020–2024). BCa bootstrap 95% CIs and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
provide formal inference. Contrary to standard practitioner expectation, continuous hedge

ratio adaptation degrades net Sharpe ratio through higher turnover while simultaneously
reducing tail risk by 71%, a dissociation that holds across all δ values and sub-specifications.
2. Formal decomposition of the signal quality paradox. Dynamic estimation reduces foldmean MaxDD by 71% (−0.31% vs. −1.07%) but at substantially lower Sharpe (−2.08 vs.
−0.16), driven by a signal quality paradox with a formal theoretical basis: the Kalman
spread decomposes as εKalman

as 81.5% of forced time-limit exits generate 3.5× standard turnover without adaptive hedging.
This result is consistent with a structural explanation: Kalman’s 71% drawdown advantage
cannot be explained by shorter holding periods alone and appears to arise from genuine online
hedge ratio adaptation (Branch D is not a single-variable control; it changes both holding
period and exit mechanism simultaneously).
4. Signal quality repair via posterior variance entry gate (Branch F). Scaling the entry
threshold proportionally to Kalman posterior variance Pt raises the signal-exit win rate from
52.7% to 54.8% and improves fold-mean Sharpe from −2.08 to −1.38 while preserving
drawdown containment, making it the first mechanism-based intervention addressing the
signal quality paradox.
5. Adaptive-δ Kalman and cost-breakeven analysis. Per-pair drift-proportional δ (Branch E)
maintains drawdown containment (−0.28%) with fold-mean Sharpe −1.93. A cost-breakeven
analysis estimates the Kalman–OLS Sharpe crossover at approximately 6–7 bps one-way:
below this threshold, Kalman may be competitive with OLS while retaining its drawdown
advantage, suggesting its underperformance is primarily a cost-frequency interaction rather
than a fundamental return deficit.
1.3
Economic Framing
The time-varying hedge ratio βt admits a natural economic interpretation beyond its statistical
role. In the log-price cointegration framework, βt encodes the intensity of economic co-movement
7

between the pair — a rising βt is consistent with increasing product-market proximity (Hoberg and
Phillips, 2016), convergence in factor exposures, or shared macroeconomic sensitivity; a falling
βt may signal incipient divergence arising from competitive dynamics or supply-chain decoupling.
The regime state St ∈{calm, turb} identified by the HMM similarly maps to economically interpretable conditions: turbulent regimes correspond to periods in which the equilibrium relationship
is temporarily suspended, consistent with microstructure distress or transient sector rotation.

Related Work
2.1
Pairs Trading and Statistical Arbitrage
The modern pairs trading literature traces to Gatev et al. (2006), who document that a distance-based
relative-value strategy on U.S. equities earns mean annual excess returns of approximately 11% over
the period 1962–2002. Their methodology pairs stocks by minimising the sum of squared deviations
in normalised price histories, applies a static opening threshold of two standard deviations, and
is evaluated on a rolling one-year formation / six-month trading window, a design that explicitly
avoids look-ahead bias. Gatev et al. conclude that the strategy’s returns cannot be fully explained by
standard risk factors and attribute the source to arbitrage capital constraints and convergence risk.
Avellaneda and Lee (2010) extend the framework by modelling the spread as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
(OU) process and deriving optimal entry and exit thresholds from a mean-variance criterion. Their
empirical application to 750 U.S. equities generates Sharpe ratios above 1.0 in the pre-2008 period,
declining markedly in the post-crisis era as volatility regimes shift. Both papers treat the hedge ratio
as a fixed parameter estimated on the formation window, a simplification this paper relaxes.
More recent work has examined the microstructure of pairs trading profitability. Do and Faff (2010)
find that post-2002 returns are substantially lower, attributing the decline to increased competition
among arbitrageurs. Engelberg et al. (2009) decompose pairs returns into fundamental news and
noise components, finding that roughly two-thirds arise from noise reversion rather than fundamental
convergence. The most comprehensive cross-method comparison to date is Rad et al. (2016), who
evaluate distance, cointegration, and copula methods across the full U.S. equity market (1962–2014)
and find that all three remain profitable before transaction costs, with the cointegration approach
8

most robust during periods of market stress. All methods in Rad et al. (2016) use static hedge ratios.
Relative to this body of work, the present paper focuses not on whether pairs trading is profitable in
aggregate, but on whether the mode of hedge ratio estimation (static versus dynamic) materially
affects risk-adjusted performance in the demanding 2020–2024 multi-regime window.
2.2
Cointegration-Based Methods
The distance-based approach of Gatev et al. (2006) was formalised statistically by Vidyamurthy
(2004), who applied the Engle–Granger two-step cointegration test (Engle and Granger, 1987) to
equity pairs selection. The Engle–Granger framework yields a static OLS hedge ratio ˆβ from the
regression log At = ˆβ · log Bt + εt, and the residual εt constitutes the spread. Under cointegration,
εt is I(0) and mean-reverting, justifying the pairs trading signal.
Johansen (1988) provides a maximum-likelihood framework for testing and estimating multiple
cointegrating vectors, yielding a more efficient hedge ratio estimate when the number of common
stochastic trends is unknown. The Johansen trace statistic is asymptotically more powerful than
Engle–Granger for identifying cointegrated systems with near-unit-root residuals. We apply both
tests jointly in our pair-selection filter, requiring both the Engle–Granger p-value to be below 0.05
and the Johansen trace statistic to exceed its 95% critical value—a conservative dual screen that
reduces false-positive pair selection.
The stability of estimated cointegrating vectors has received attention in macroeconometrics (Hansen,
1992) but has been largely ignored in the pairs trading literature, where pairs are typically reselected
on each training window without testing for structural breaks in the hedge ratio. Huck (2015)
document that pairs selected for cointegration on historical data frequently fail to maintain the
relationship out-of-sample, a finding consistent with the time-varying βt hypothesis motivating this
paper.
2.3
Dynamic Hedge Ratio Estimation
The Kalman filter has a long history in finance for estimating time-varying parameters (Harvey,
1990). Applied to the pairs trading hedge ratio, it treats βt as a latent state evolving as a random
walk with process noise Q = δ/(1 −δ), and updates the estimate sequentially using the observation
log At = βt · log Bt + εt (Chan, 2013). The key hyperparameter δ controls the adaptation speed:
small δ produces a slowly adapting ratio that approximates OLS on long windows, while large δ
may over-fit to measurement noise.
The foundational reference for the state-space perspective on statistical arbitrage is Pole (2007), who
develops a systematic framework connecting Kalman filtering, cointegration, and dynamic position
9

sizing. Building on this, Zeng and Wei (2014) derive a generalised Kalman filter for pairs trading
that unifies observation-noise and process-noise estimation within a single EM step, demonstrating
superior hedge ratio accuracy versus single-hyperparameter variants in simulation. Nourahmadi
and Norahmadi (2023) apply Kalman-filtered hedge ratio estimation to pairs trading in the Iranian
automobile sector and report improved signal stability relative to OLS, but their evaluation covers a
single sector with limited pairs and no formal bootstrap or walk-forward inference. The theoretical
grounding for Kalman-based hedge ratio estimation in the OU context was formalised by Elliott
et al. (2005), who derive the optimal filter under a state-space model with OU measurement errors.
Hwang and Rubesam (2019) study pairs trading under a mean-reverting jump-diffusion spread
model and show that accounting for jump risk in the spread substantially alters optimal entry and
exit thresholds, a finding consistent with the regime-switching intuition motivating Branch C of this
paper. In contrast to all prior applied work, we evaluate the Kalman approach across a 50-stock
cross-section with walk-forward validation and BCa bootstrap confidence intervals, enabling formal
hypothesis testing of H1.
An alternative to the Kalman filter is the exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) regression, which is computationally simpler but lacks the principled prediction-correction structure that
allows the Kalman filter to propagate uncertainty through the hedge ratio estimate. DCC-GARCH
models (Engle, 2002) provide another route to time-varying covariance estimation but are substantially more parameter-intensive than the one-parameter Kalman model we study. We restrict
attention to the Kalman filter as the practitioner-standard approach against which formal inference
has been absent.
2.4
Regime-Switching and HMM Methods in Trading
Hidden Markov Models for financial time series originate with Hamilton (1989), who applied a
two-state Markov-switching model to U.S. GNP growth. Applied to equity trading, HMMs have
primarily been used to model market-wide regimes (bull, bear, and sideways states) using broad
market index features such as realised volatility and returns (Kim and Nelson, 1999).
The application most directly related to this paper is Fanelli et al. (2023), who fit a two-state HMM
to crude oil futures pairs and find that suppressing entries in turbulent HMM states improves both
Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown relative to an unfiltered strategy. Their feature set, however,
uses market-wide petroleum market indicators rather than pair-specific features, making it unsuitable
for application to a diverse equity universe where individual pair dynamics vary considerably. We
depart from Fanelli et al. (2023) by constructing pair-specific HMM features (spread realised
volatility, spread autocorrelation, and rolling ADF p-value) that directly measure the health of the
pair’s mean-reversion dynamic rather than the ambient market environment.
10

The combination of Kalman-filtered hedge ratio and HMM regime conditioning has been explored
by Avalos-Gaytán et al. (2017), who implement a blended Kalman-HMM system on a single equity
pair (Coca-Cola / PepsiCo) using OU parameter transitions as regime features. Their work validates
the broad architecture but does not evaluate the approach on a cross-section of pairs, does not use
pair-specific statistical-test features (spread volatility, autocorrelation, ADF p-value), and provides
no walk-forward or bootstrap inference. The present paper addresses all three gaps.

Data and Pair Selection
3.1
Universe and Data Source
We study 50 large-capitalisation equities drawn from the S&P 500 index, spanning eight GICS
sectors (Information Technology, Health Care, Financials, Consumer Discretionary, Communication
Services, Industrials, Consumer Staples, and Energy). Daily adjusted closing prices are sourced
from Yahoo Finance via the yfinance Python library for the period January 2, 2020 to December
31, 2024 (1,258 trading days).
Survivorship Bias
The universe reflects S&P 500 composition as of January 2020, with one material adjustment: Tesla,
Inc. (TSLA) was added to the index on December 21, 2020, and is included from that date. Meta
Platforms (META), which changed its ticker from FB in June 2022, is treated as a continuous series.
Appendix A quantifies the directional survivorship bias bound; for a large-capitalisation universe
with infrequent constituent changes, the estimated bias is small (≤3% Sharpe inflation).
3.2
Walk-Forward Cross-Validation Design
To prevent in-sample overfitting, we employ a standard walk-forward (“rolling-window expanding
train”) cross-validation design:
• Train window: 252 trading days (one calendar year)
• Out-of-sample (OOS) window: 126 trading days (six months)
• Step size: 63 trading days (quarterly roll)
• Total OOS folds: 14 rolling test periods (step = 63 days; consecutive folds share ≈63 days
of OOS data) spanning late 2020 to October 2024
Pair selection, hedge ratio estimation, and HMM training are performed exclusively on each fold’s
11

training window. Parameters are never re-estimated on OOS data. All performance statistics are
computed on the OOS windows only.

Step 1: Dual Cointegration Test
We apply the Engle–Granger two-step test (Engle and Granger, 1987) (regressing log A on log B)
and the Johansen trace test (Johansen, 1988) with one lag. A pair passes only if both the Engle–
Granger p-value is below 0.05 and the Johansen trace statistic exceeds the 95% critical value
(equivalent to rejecting the null of no cointegration at the 5% level). This dual screen substantially
reduces the false-positive rate relative to single-test approaches.
Step 2: OU Half-Life Filter
The spread residual εt = log At −ˆβ log Bt is modelled as an OU process dεt = −κεt dt + σ dWt,
and the half-life of mean reversion is estimated as τ1/2 = log 2/ˆκ. We require τ1/2 ∈[5, 60] trading
days: pairs with faster reversion are susceptible to transactions costs, while pairs with slower
reversion provide weak trading signals within the six-month OOS window.
Step 3: Top-k Selection
Pairs passing both filters are ranked by their Engle–Granger p-value in ascending order (most
strongly cointegrated first), and the top k = 10 pairs per fold are selected for trading. This selection
is performed independently within each fold’s training window using only that fold’s training data;
no pair is selected based on information outside the fold.
Note: Residual False Discovery Rate
Under the dual screen (p < 0.05 for both Engle–Granger and Johansen), the expected number
of spuriously cointegrated pairs per fold under the null of no cointegration is approximately
1,225 × 0.05 × 0.05 ≈3 pairs, roughly 17–30% of the 10–17 passing pairs in a typical fold.

4
Methodology
We evaluate seven hedge ratio estimation branches that share identical pair-selection, signalgeneration, and backtest parameters and differ only in how βt is computed (and, for Branches
C and G, whether regime-based entry suppression is applied). Branches A–C are the pre-specified

Comparisons among D–G should be interpreted directionally, not as confirmatory hypothesis tests.
Branches D–G are described in Sections 4.6–4.9.

Signal Filtering
During OOS evaluation, the Viterbi algorithm is run on the complete OOS feature sequence to
decode the most likely state path for the full OOS window. This is the global (batch) Viterbi pass
rather than a strictly causal online implementation: technically, state assignments for early OOS
days are informed by the full OOS observation sequence. Because all OOS features are constructed
from contemporaneous spread data and no post-OOS information enters the feature construction,
the practical look-ahead is limited to within-OOS future data and does not violate the fold boundary.
This is the standard backtesting implementation; an online recursive decoder would be preferable for
a production deployment. The practical magnitude of the look-ahead cannot be precisely quantified
without either the fitted transition matrices (which are not stored in the current pipeline) or a re-run
with an online forward-pass decoder. Qualitatively, the contribution of observation t + k to the
Viterbi state at day t decays geometrically in k at a rate determined by the second eigenvalue of the
transition matrix; for states with high self-loop probability (consistent with the persistent 77%/23%
occupancy), this decay is rapid. The look-ahead is therefore most consequential at regime-transition
boundaries (where the global decoder correctly identifies the new state a few days before a causal
decoder would) and negligible during stable-regime periods, which constitute the large majority of
OOS time. We flag a pipeline re-run with online Viterbi as a direction for future work; we expect
the qualitative MaxDD advantage of Branch C over Branch B to survive, since that advantage
holds robustly across all 14 folds and is largest during the multi-week rate-hike transitions, not
at single-day regime boundaries. On turbulent days (St = 1), new entry signals are suppressed
and direction reversals on open positions are also filtered; open positions are not forcibly closed,
preventing unnecessary round-trip transaction costs.

Execution and Cost Assumptions
Trades are executed at the next-day open price following signal generation, eliminating look-ahead
bias. Transaction costs are modelled as 5 basis points per leg per direction (spread + commission),
with an additional 2 bps slippage per leg, applied to both the long and short legs at entry and at exit.
The effective round-trip cost is 2 × 2 × 7 = 28 bps of pair notional (7 bps per leg × 2 legs × 2
directions). Position sizes are capped at 10% of the initial capital per pair, with an overall capital of
$1,000,000.

Overlap correction. Because the step size (63 days) is smaller than the test window (126 days),

Branches D, E, F: Mechanism Isolation and Signal Repair
Table 6 reports out-of-sample performance for the three extended branches designed to disentangle
the drivers of the core findings.
Branch D: Mechanical vs. Structural Drawdown Reduction
Branch D applies OLS hedge ratios with a 3-day maximum holding period, matching Kalman’s
empirical 2.3-day average. The result is unambiguous: fold-mean MaxDD worsens to −1.24%
relative to standard OLS (−1.07%), while the forced time-limit exits account for 81.5% of the 2,150
pooled trades, a 3.5× increase in turnover over Branch A. Artificially capping holding periods on
OLS does not replicate Kalman’s drawdown reduction; it amplifies cost drag without the structural
benefit of adaptive hedging. This is because Kalman’s short holdings arise from z-score reversion
driven by daily βt updates: as the adaptive hedge ratio tracks co-movement drift, spreads revert more
quickly and positions exit at favourable prices. A time cap, by contrast, forces closure regardless of
spread state, realising losses rather than waiting for reversion.
The Branch D result is consistent with a structural explanation for Kalman’s 71% drawdown
advantage. Branch D simultaneously changes both the holding period and the exit mechanism
(forced time-limit exits replace z-score reversion exits), so it is not a single-intervention control;
a cleaner counterfactual would be OLS with entry suppression on the calendar days that Kalman
happens to be flat. That said, Branch D’s worsened MaxDD (−1.24% vs. OLS −1.07%) strongly
argues that short holding periods alone do not deliver drawdown reduction and that continuous βt
revision plays a genuine structural role in limiting mishedge accumulation during regime transitions.
The Sharpe ratio of Branch D (−1.15) remains substantially below OLS (−0.16), confirming that
27

forced short holds destroy the OLS strategy’s return-to-cost ratio without delivering any tail-risk
benefit.
Branch E: Adaptive-δ Kalman
Scaling δ per-pair to the estimated β-drift rate (Equation 13) yields a fold-mean Sharpe of −1.93,
modestly better than the fixed-δ baseline (−2.08), with trade frequency rising to 1,088 pooled trades.
The MaxDD of −0.28% is modestly better than the standard Kalman branch (−0.31%), confirming
that the drawdown containment property derives from the filter’s online updating mechanism and is
largely insensitive to the specific δ value. The shorter mean hold of 1.6 days (vs. 2.3 for Branch B)
reflects fast-drift pairs receiving higher δ, causing more frequent z-score crossings and earlier exits.
Branch F: Posterior Variance Entry Gate
Branch F directly addresses the signal quality paradox by widening the entry threshold proportionally
to Pt/ ¯P20. The signal-exit win rate improves from 52.7% (Branch B) to 54.8%, a modest but
directionally correct improvement confirming that a portion of Kalman’s poor signal quality is
attributable to entries during high-β-uncertainty periods. Trade count falls to 553 (vs. 932 for
Branch B), and the fold-mean Sharpe improves to −1.38 (the best of the three Kalman variants,
0.29 Sharpe units ahead of Kalman+HMM), with MaxDD of −0.22% comparable to Branch C.
The variance gate therefore achieves Sharpe recovery without sacrificing drawdown containment,
making it the most balanced extended branch. The residual gap between Branch F signal-exit win
rate (54.8%) and OLS (85.3%) indicates that the variance gate filters the most extreme noise-driven
entries but cannot fully eliminate the structural β-contamination inherent to all z-scores computed
from an adaptively updated hedge ratio.

6
Discussion
6.1
The Kalman Filter as Tail-Risk Manager
The central empirical finding is a dissociation between Sharpe performance and drawdown containment. Static OLS achieves fold-mean Sharpe −0.157 while incurring fold-mean maximum
drawdown of −1.07%. The Kalman branch reverses this profile: fold-mean Sharpe falls further
to −2.080 but fold-mean MaxDD compresses to −0.31%, a 71% reduction in tail loss. This dissociation arises because the Kalman filter operates on two timescales simultaneously. On the fast
timescale (daily), continuous βt updates generate z-score fluctuations that trigger entries and exits
with 2.3-day average holding periods. On the slow timescale (multi-week), those same updates
track genuine co-movement drift and reduce mishedge during regime transitions, the mechanism
that limits drawdown. The net Sharpe impact is negative because 28 bps round-trip costs (7 bps
per leg × 2 legs × 2 directions) at 2.3-day holds consume approximately the gross P&L from each
short-lived trade. Gross (pre-cost) Sharpe is positive for all branches, confirming the strategy has
alpha before cost drag (Appendix E).
6.2
Economic Interpretation of βt Trajectories
The time-varying hedge ratio βt encodes dynamic co-movement structure. An increasing βt indicates
that asset A has become more sensitive to the common factor driving B, consistent with increasing
factor-exposure overlap, supply chain integration, or demand complementarity as measured by
product-market proximity (Hoberg and Phillips, 2016). A declining βt may reflect competitive
substitution, supply-chain decoupling, or idiosyncratic capital structure change. The β-drift quartile
results in Table 5 show that the Sharpe penalty from Kalman tends to decrease with drift speed,
consistent with genuine co-movement evolution in fast-drifting pairs justifying more frequent hedge
ratio revision.
6.3
When Is Dynamic Estimation Warranted?
The conditional nature of the Kalman penalty suggests a practical selection heuristic. The βdrift rate estimated on the most recent training window is a forward-looking indicator of hedge
ratio instability. Pairs in Q4 (drift > 0.011/month) show the smallest Kalman underperformance
(∆SR = −1.03); pairs in Q1 (drift < 0.009/month) show the largest (∆SR = −2.46). In a costsensitive implementation, practitioners might restrict Kalman estimation to pairs with high estimated
drift while using static OLS for stable relationships. A longer sample period or higher-frequency
data may be required to recover the Kalman Sharpe advantage entirely.

Theoretical Foundation of the Signal Quality Paradox
The spread decomposition (11) formalises why dynamic estimation degrades signal quality rather
than improving it. The OLS spread εOLS

6.5
Testable Prediction for Calmer Samples
The conditional framing of this study implies a specific out-of-sample prediction. The Kalman
strategy’s cost drag scales as Ntrades × c, where Ntrades depends on the frequency with which βt
updates cross the z-score threshold (and hence on the ambient β-drift rate). In a lower-volatility
environment such as 2010–2019, where equity pairs exhibit slower co-movement evolution, the
contamination term in (11) is smaller, fewer noise-driven z-score crossings occur, and Kalman’s
average holding period lengthens toward OLS’s 9.6-day benchmark. If the average Kalman hold
reaches 7–10 days in such a sample, cost drag falls by 60–75% at the same 7 bps per-leg cost, placing
the strategy well above the 6–7 bps breakeven identified in Section 5.6 and implying Sharpe parity
or advantage relative to static OLS. The framework thus generates a testable conjecture consistent
with the decomposition and the within-sample quartile pattern: Kalman’s Sharpe underperformance
relative to OLS should shrink as the sample’s average β-drift rate declines, with parity plausible at
drift rates consistent with 6–7 bps breakeven holding periods. (We use “conjecture” deliberately:
32

this extrapolates a within-sample quartile pattern to between-sample dynamics, which requires an
independent out-of-sample test for confirmation.) The β-drift quartile results in Table 5 provide
within-sample support (∆SR shrinks from −2.46 in Q1 to −1.03 in Q4), but a genuine test requires
an out-of-sample evaluation in a structurally distinct calmer regime, a priority for future work.
6.6
Limitations
Data and sample scope. The 50-name universe is drawn from a single index (S&P 500) and covers
January 2020 to December 2024. This window is intentionally chosen as a high-instability evaluation
period: four structurally distinct macroeconomic regimes are compressed into five calendar years,
providing unusually rich regime variation for studying dynamic estimation. However, this choice has
a direct cost: the results are conditioned on a period of extraordinary structural instability and should
not be extrapolated to calmer or longer samples without additional empirical evidence. In particular,
the uniformly negative net-of-cost Sharpe ratios likely reflect the short holding periods induced
by high-frequency β updates in the post-COVID volatility environment; strategies evaluated over,
for example, a 2010–2019 lower-volatility window might exhibit a more favourable cost–return
balance. The negative net Sharpe is, however, consistent with the broader literature: Avellaneda
and Lee (2010) document declining pairs trading Sharpe ratios after 2008 as volatility regimes shift
and arbitrage capital increases; our 2020–2024 results extend this declining trend into a further
regime of extraordinary instability. Results may also not generalise to smaller-capitalisation equities,
international markets, or pre-2020 periods. All prices are sourced from Yahoo Finance adjusted
closes, which apply retrospective dividend and split adjustments that may introduce minor lookahead bias in the adjusted series. Within each fold, the top k = 10 pairs by cointegration quality
are selected; the sensitivity of results to this choice of k has not been systematically varied and
represents a limitation of the current study.
Transaction costs and implementation. The 7 bps per-leg one-way cost model (28 bps round-trip
for both legs at entry and exit) is conservative for institutional trading. At the Kalman strategy’s
2.3-day average holding period, costs represent a dominant fraction of gross per-trade return and
are the proximate cause of Sharpe underperformance. Implementations with lower execution costs
(direct market access, internalised crossing, or lower-frequency rebalancing) may recover positive
Sharpe while retaining the drawdown benefit. The estimated 6–7 bps breakeven is sensitive to
the Kalman strategy’s very low annualised return volatility (0.26%). If realised volatility were
50% higher (0.39%), the same gross return would produce Sharpe ratios approximately 33% lower,
shifting the breakeven cost downward; if volatility were 50% lower (0.13%), breakeven would shift
upward. Practitioners should re-estimate this figure using their own execution cost and volatility
assumptions.
33

Mechanism of drawdown reduction. The paper attributes Kalman’s 71% drawdown reduction to
continuous hedge ratio adaptation limiting directional exposure during regime transitions. Branch D
(OLS with a matched 3-day holding cap) directly tests the alternative mechanical explanation: if
shorter holding periods per se drove drawdown reduction, Branch D would approach Kalman’s
MaxDD. The data reject this hypothesis: Branch D worsens MaxDD to −1.24% (vs. −1.07% for
standard OLS), as the forced time-limit exits generate 3.5× standard OLS turnover without adaptive
hedging benefit. This result is consistent with a structural explanation: Kalman’s drawdown
advantage cannot be explained by shorter holding periods alone and appears to arise from genuine
online hedge ratio adaptation. Branch D is not a single-variable control (it changes both holding
period and exit mechanism simultaneously), so a complete causal attribution requires a cleaner
counterfactual; the result nevertheless strongly argues against the purely mechanical interpretation.
Statistical power and bootstrap dependence. With 14 paired fold observations, the Wilcoxon test
has limited power against small effect sizes. The Sharpe results are nevertheless highly significant
(W = 7, p = 0.002) because the Kalman penalty is large and consistent across folds (OLS
superior in 12 of 14). The drawdown result is similarly robust. Block-bootstrap corrected p-values
(Appendix D, block size b = 2 folds, B = 10,000) confirm the directional conclusion under the
fold-overlap correction; b = 2 is the theoretically appropriate block size because only immediately
adjacent folds share OOS data under the 63-day step / 126-day window design. Bootstrap confidence
intervals for the main table are computed with i.i.d. resampling of pooled OOS daily returns. Because
multiple pairs trade simultaneously in each fold, daily returns contain intra-fold cross-sectional
correlation that i.i.d. bootstrap does not account for. A circular block bootstrap would be more
conservative; we expect the qualitative conclusions to be unaffected given the large effect sizes.
Batch Viterbi within-OOS look-ahead. As disclosed in Section 4, the HMM state sequence is
decoded using the global Viterbi algorithm on the complete OOS window rather than a causal
online decoder. State assignments on early OOS days are therefore technically informed by the
full 126-day observation sequence. The practical magnitude of this look-ahead is bounded by the
HMM’s mixing properties: the contribution of observation t + k to the Viterbi assignment at day t
decays geometrically in k at a rate determined by the second eigenvalue of the transition matrix.
For the estimated 77%/23% calm/turbulent occupancy, which implies high self-loop probabilities,
this decay is rapid, so the look-ahead is consequential primarily at regime-transition boundaries
(where the global decoder identifies the correct state several days before a causal decoder would)
and negligible during stable periods (which constitute the majority of OOS time by definition).
Branch C’s reported drawdown reduction is therefore modestly optimistic near regime-transition
points; the aggregate inflation relative to a fully causal implementation cannot be precisely quantified
because the fitted transition matrices are not stored in the current pipeline and the OOS re-run with
34

an online forward-pass decoder has not been performed. We flag this re-run as a priority for future
validation; we expect the qualitative MaxDD advantage of Branch C over Branch B to survive
because it holds robustly across all 14 folds and is largest during extended multi-week regime
transitions (rate-hike cycle, COVID shock) rather than at single-day state boundaries.
Survivorship bias. The one material universe addition (TSLA, December 2020) affects early folds.
Appendix A bounds this bias at a level that does not alter the qualitative conclusions.
6.7
Broader Economic Implications
The economics of co-movement persistence in equity markets has implications beyond trading
strategy design. The estimated βt trajectories constitute a time-series proxy for inter-firm complementarity (Hoberg and Phillips, 2016) that could be used to study industry dynamics, competitive interactions, and supply-chain integration. The four-regime 2020–2024 window provides a
quasi-natural experiment: exogenous macroeconomic shocks (COVID, Fed rate-hike cycle) allow
attribution of βt drift to aggregate demand shocks versus firm-specific adjustments, potentially
informing structural models of industry co-movement.
7
Conclusion
This paper provides a rigorous walk-forward characterisation of how hedge ratio estimation method
shapes the risk–return profile of equity pairs trading, addressing a gap between widespread practitioner adoption of the Kalman filter and the absence of formal statistical inference in the published
literature. All seven branches produce negative net-of-cost fold-mean Sharpe ratios in the demanding 2020–2024 multi-regime environment: our contribution is not to claim profitability but
to characterise the dissociation between Sharpe performance and drawdown containment, and to
decompose its mechanism. Across 50 S&P 500 equities, we find:
• Static OLS significantly outperforms Kalman on fold-level Sharpe ratio (∆SR = −1.92,
Wilcoxon W = 7, p = 0.002), driven by Kalman’s 54% higher trade turnover (932 vs. 607
pooled trades) at 2.3-day average holding periods. Both strategies are marginally negative in
this demanding multi-regime sample, but OLS (−0.157) is dramatically less negative than
Kalman (−2.080).
• Despite lower Sharpe, dynamic estimation reduces fold-mean maximum drawdown by 71%
relative to OLS (−0.31% vs. −1.07%). The Kalman filter operates as a tail-risk manager: its
continuous hedge ratio revision limits directional exposure during regime transitions even as
it generates excess trades.
35

• HMM regime conditioning provides statistically significant further drawdown reduction (foldmean MaxDD −0.22%, a 30% reduction from Kalman, Wilcoxon W = 102, p < 0.001)
while also improving fold-mean Sharpe from −2.080 to −1.671 by selectively suppressing
the weakest turbulent-regime trades. This is the most robust finding in the paper.
• The Kalman Sharpe penalty decreases monotonically from Q1 (∆SR = −2.46) to Q4
(∆SR = −1.03), consistent with dynamic estimation being conditionally warranted when
genuine co-movement shifts occur rapidly.
• Sensitivity analysis across δ ∈{10−5, 10−4, 10−3} confirms the drawdown containment
finding is robust to adaptation speed.
• Mechanism decomposition (Branch D) shows that an OLS strategy with a 3-day holding
cap worsens fold-mean MaxDD to −1.24% (worse than standard OLS at −1.07%), as forced
time-limit exits generate 3.5× standard turnover. Because Branch D simultaneously alters
both holding period and exit mechanism, it is not a single-intervention control; nonetheless,
the result strongly supports a structural explanation: Kalman’s drawdown advantage cannot
be attributed solely to shorter holdings and appears to reflect genuine hedge ratio adaptation.
• Signal quality repair (Branch F) via posterior variance entry gating raises the signal-exit
win rate from 52.7% to 54.8% and improves fold-mean Sharpe from −2.08 to −1.38 while
preserving drawdown containment. This demonstrates that β-contamination of z-scores is
diagnosable and partially correctable via uncertainty-aware entry filtering.
• Adaptive-δ Kalman (Branch E) maintains drawdown containment (−0.28%) with foldmean Sharpe −1.93. A cost-breakeven analysis estimates the Kalman–OLS crossover at
approximately 6–7 bps one-way, suggesting the strategy may be competitive at institutionalgrade execution costs.
The core message for practitioners is that the Kalman filter should be framed as a risk-management
overlay rather than a return-enhancement technique at typical 28-bps round-trip transaction costs.
Among the variants studied, Branch F (posterior variance entry gate) provides the best signal-quality
and drawdown balance. Its value proposition is drawdown containment at a quantifiable Sharpe
cost; whether that trade-off is acceptable depends on the investor’s risk tolerance and execution cost
structure. The results are conditional on the highly volatile 2020–2024 period; empirical evaluation
over a longer or calmer sample remains a priority for future work.
Beyond trading applications, the β-drift trajectories constitute a time-series proxy for inter-firm
economic complementarity. Future work could apply these estimates to study industry dynamics,
test whether drift rates predict future earnings correlation, extend the methodology to intraday data
where regime shifts are more frequent, or explore neural-augmented Kalman filtering (Milstein
36

et al., 2024) as a further refinement of the variance entry gate.

Survivorship Bias Audit
The 50-name universe is drawn from S&P 500 index constituents. Over the 2020–2024 window,
two events affect the universe relative to the January 2020 composition:
1. Tesla, Inc. (TSLA), added to the S&P 500 on December 21, 2020. TSLA is included in our
universe from that date. For folds with training windows beginning before December 2020
(Folds 1–3), TSLA is excluded from pair selection in the affected training window. TSLA
participates fully in Folds 4–14.
2. Meta Platforms (META), formerly Facebook (FB), ticker changed June 2022. The price
series is treated as continuous under the META ticker. No data discontinuity arises.
Bias bound estimation.
To estimate the directional effect of TSLA inclusion, we re-run pair
selection for Folds 4–14 (the folds in which TSLA is eligible) with and without TSLA in the
candidate universe. The difference in mean fold Sharpe is +0.03 annualised units with TSLA
versus without, a difference well within the bootstrap confidence interval width. We conclude that
survivorship bias inflates reported Sharpe by at most ≤3% relative to a perfectly survivorshipneutral universe, and does not alter the qualitative results.
For large-capitalisation indices with infrequent constituent changes, survivorship bias is generally
smaller than for small-capitalisation or composite indices (Brown et al., 1992). Our conservative
large-cap universe construction further limits this concern.

requires 2 × 6 = 12 additional parameters per model, which at 252 training observations and 10
active features may increase over-fitting risk. The Branch G ablation provides indirect evidence that
the HMM nonetheless captures genuine signal beyond a univariate volatility screen, suggesting the
diagonal approximation does not eliminate the multi-feature value.
Note on convergence: The HMM pipeline stores only the fitted-state indicator per fold (not the
model object or convergence monitor), so systematic convergence diagnostics are not available
from the existing pipeline run. In the 100 EM iterations used, the Baum–Welch algorithm is
expected to converge for most fits given the 3 × 252 feature matrix; the 0.7% total fallback rate (all
triggers combined) is consistent with rare non-convergence. Future work should store the hmmlearn
monitor_ object to report per-fit log-likelihood trajectories.

b = 2. The corrected p-values confirm the directional conclusions of the nominal Wilcoxon test; as
