Abstract 
Artificial intelligence is fundamentally reshaping modern financial markets, particularly through 
its integration into algorithmic trading systems. This paper presents a systematic literature review 
of five key studies published between 2024 and 2025, examining how AI techniques—including 
deep learning, reinforcement learning, and natural language processing—are being applied to 
algorithmic trading and what impact these developments have on market efficiency, volatility, 
liquidity, and regulatory frameworks. The findings reveal that while AI-driven trading systems 
demonstrably enhance predictive accuracy and market liquidity, they also introduce significant 
systemic risks, including the potential for emergent collusive behavior among autonomous 
agents and increased susceptibility to flash crashes. The review further identifies a critical gap 
between theoretical model development and real-world deployment, suggesting that future 
research must prioritize empirical validation and cross-market applicability. These insights carry 
important implications for financial regulators, institutional investors, and technology developers 
navigating the evolving landscape of AI-driven finance. 
 
1. Introduction 
The last decade has witnessed an unprecedented transformation in the way financial markets 
operate, driven largely by rapid advances in artificial intelligence and machine learning 
technologies. What was once the exclusive domain of human traders and rule-based automated 
systems has evolved into a complex ecosystem of intelligent algorithms capable of processing 
vast amounts of market data, identifying subtle patterns, and executing trades at speeds and 
 

scales unimaginable to human participants. According to Addy et al. (2024), AI-powered 
algorithmic trading now accounts for a significant and growing proportion of daily trading 
volume across major global exchanges, fundamentally altering market microstructure and the 
dynamics of price discovery. 
This shift raises important and timely questions for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers 
alike. On one hand, AI-driven trading systems promise enhanced market efficiency, deeper 
liquidity pools, and more sophisticated risk management capabilities. On the other hand, they 
introduce novel and poorly understood risks—from the potential for autonomous algorithms to 
engage in collusive pricing behavior (Dou, Goldstein & Ji, 2025) to the amplification of market 
volatility during periods of stress (Yang et al., 2025). Understanding both the benefits and risks 
of AI in algorithmic trading is, therefore, a matter of considerable academic and practical 
importance. 
This paper addresses this need by conducting a systematic review of five prominent studies 
published between 2024 and 2025, each of which examines a distinct but complementary 
dimension of AI's role in financial markets. The selected studies collectively cover the strategic 
evolution of algorithmic trading, the diversity of AI techniques employed, the empirical impact 
on market volatility, the theoretical risks of algorithmic collusion, and the practical challenges of 
deploying deep learning models in real trading environments. By synthesizing these 
perspectives, this paper aims to provide a comprehensive and up-to-date overview of the field, 
identify areas of consensus and disagreement, and highlight gaps that future research should 
address. 
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides a detailed review of the 
literature organized around four major themes. Section 3 presents a comparative analysis and 
discussion of the findings. Section 4 concludes with key takeaways, limitations of this review, 
and directions for future research. 
 
2. Literature Review 
The following review organizes the selected studies into four thematic areas: (1) the evolution 
and diversity of AI-driven trading strategies; (2) the application of specific AI techniques in 
 

financial trading; (3) the impact of algorithmic trading on market dynamics; and (4) emerging 
risks and regulatory concerns. 
 
2.1 Evolution and Diversity of AI-Driven Trading Strategies 
The transformation of algorithmic trading from simple rule-based automation to sophisticated 
AI-driven systems represents one of the most significant developments in modern finance. Addy 
et al. (2024), in their comprehensive review published in the World Journal of Advanced 
Engineering Technology and Sciences, trace this historical evolution and examine the wide range 
of strategies that AI has enabled. The authors identify several core categories of AI-enhanced 
trading strategies, including high-frequency trading, statistical arbitrage, sentiment-driven 
trading, and portfolio optimization, each of which leverages different AI capabilities to exploit 
distinct types of market inefficiency. 
Addy et al. (2024) find that the increasing sophistication of these strategies has had measurable 
positive effects on market efficiency and liquidity. AI systems are able to process and react to 
new information far more rapidly than human traders, facilitating faster price discovery and 
reducing bid-ask spreads. However, the authors also note that this same speed and complexity 
creates new vulnerabilities. When multiple AI systems respond simultaneously to the same 
market signals, the potential for feedback loops and sudden, destabilizing market movements 
increases substantially. This tension between the efficiency gains and instability risks introduced 
by AI trading is a theme that recurs across the reviewed literature. 
A significant limitation acknowledged by Addy et al. (2024) is the review's reliance on existing 
published studies without new empirical validation. The authors call for more rigorous 
real-world testing of AI trading strategies across diverse market conditions and asset classes, a 
gap that subsequent studies in this review have begun to address. 
 
2.2 AI Techniques Applied in Financial Trading 
A detailed picture of the specific AI techniques being deployed in financial trading is provided 
by two complementary systematic reviews. Dakalbab et al. (2024), publishing in the Journal of 
King Saud University—Computer and Information Sciences, present a systematic literature 
review of 143 studies from 2015 to 2023, offering one of the most comprehensive surveys of AI 
 

methods in financial trading to date. Their analysis reveals that technical analysis—the use of 
historical price and volume data to forecast future price movements—remains the dominant 
application framework, with machine learning models such as support vector machines, random 
forests, and gradient boosting widely employed in this context. 
Complementing this broad survey, Bhuiyan et al. (2025), writing in ScienceDirect, focus 
specifically on deep learning techniques, examining how architectures such as Recurrent Neural 
Networks (RNN), Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTM), Convolutional Neural Networks 
(CNN), and various hybrid models are being applied to predict financial market signals, 
including price movements, volatility, and trend direction. Their systematic review finds that 
deep learning models consistently outperform traditional statistical methods on standard 
benchmarks, particularly in capturing complex, non-linear patterns in high-dimensional financial 
data. The authors highlight LSTM networks as especially well-suited to financial time-series 
forecasting due to their ability to retain information over long sequences, modeling the kind of 
temporal dependencies that characterize market behavior. 
Despite this impressive predictive performance, both Dakalbab et al. (2024) and Bhuiyan et al. 
(2025) identify a persistent and troubling gap between model performance in controlled research 
settings and practical deployment in live markets. Issues such as overfitting to historical data, 
sensitivity to regime changes, high computational costs, and difficulties in real-time execution 
present significant barriers to the reliable application of advanced AI models in actual trading 
environments. Dakalbab et al. (2024) further note that inconsistencies in evaluation metrics 
across studies make direct comparison of model performance difficult, underscoring the need for 
standardized benchmarking in the field. 
 
2.3 Impact of Algorithmic Trading on Market Dynamics 
Moving from AI techniques to their observable market consequences, Yang et al. (2025) provide 
important empirical evidence on how algorithmic trading affects stock market volatility. 
Published in Scientific Reports (a Nature portfolio journal), their study focuses on the Chinese 
stock market—an important but often underrepresented context in the algorithmic trading 
literature—and employs a serial multiple mediation model to investigate the behavioral 
mechanisms through which algorithmic trading influences price volatility. 
 

Contrary to some earlier theoretical predictions, Yang et al. (2025) find that algorithmic trading, 
on balance, reduces market volatility rather than amplifying it. Their analysis identifies investor 
sentiment as the primary mediating mechanism: by continuously processing market information 
and executing trades based on rational, data-driven signals, algorithmic traders help to counteract 
the noise and irrationality introduced by emotional human investors, thereby stabilizing prices. 
Herd behavior—the tendency of investors to follow the crowd—also plays a mediating role, 
though a smaller one than sentiment. 
These findings make an important contribution to the ongoing debate about the stabilizing versus 
destabilizing effects of algorithmic trading. However, the authors are careful to note that their 
results are specific to the Chinese market context and the particular proxy measures used to 
capture algorithmic trading activity and may not generalize straightforwardly to other markets 
with different regulatory environments, investor compositions, or levels of market development. 
This market specificity is an important limitation that future cross-national studies should seek to 
address. 
 
2.4 Emerging Risks: Algorithmic Collusion and Regulatory Challenges 
Perhaps the most consequential and theoretically innovative contribution in the reviewed 
literature comes from Dou, Goldstein, and Ji (2025), whose working paper—distributed through 
SSRN and affiliated with the Wharton School and NBER—examines a largely overlooked but 
potentially systemic risk of AI-powered trading: the emergence of autonomous collusive 
behavior among competing algorithmic traders. 
Using a theoretical model populated by reinforcement-learning agents, Dou et al. (2025) 
demonstrate that AI trading algorithms can, without any explicit coordination or communication, 
independently learn to sustain collusive pricing strategies that undermine market competition and 
price efficiency. This emergent collusion arises because reinforcement-learning agents, in 
optimizing their individual reward functions, may discover that tacit cooperation—for example, 
avoiding aggressive competition on certain trade types—yields higher long-term returns than 
competitive behavior. The implications are profound: existing antitrust and market manipulation 
regulations, which were designed to detect and penalize explicit coordination among human 
 

actors, may be entirely inadequate to address collusion that arises spontaneously from the 
independent learning dynamics of AI systems. 
Addy et al. (2024) similarly raise concerns about the regulatory and ethical dimensions of AI 
trading, noting that the current regulatory framework in most jurisdictions lags significantly 
behind the pace of technological change. Together, these studies paint a picture of a financial 
system in which the efficiency gains from AI are real but unevenly distributed, and in which new 
categories of systemic risk are emerging faster than regulators' ability to understand and respond 
to them. 
 
3. Analysis and Discussion 
Taken together, the five studies reviewed in this paper present a nuanced and sometimes 
contradictory portrait of AI's role in algorithmic trading. Several important areas of consensus 
emerge alongside notable tensions and unresolved questions. 
On the question of performance and efficiency, there is broad agreement that AI 
techniques—particularly deep learning models—offer genuine advances in predictive accuracy 
and market efficiency compared to traditional methods. Dakalbab et al. (2024) and Bhuiyan et al. 
(2025) both confirm that AI models consistently outperform conventional statistical approaches 
on standard benchmarks, while Addy et al. (2024) and Yang et al. (2025) find evidence of 
improved market liquidity and reduced volatility attributable to algorithmic trading activity. 
However, the literature is considerably less settled on the question of risk. While Yang et al. 
(2025) find a net stabilizing effect on volatility in the Chinese market, Dou et al. (2025) 
demonstrate theoretically that AI trading systems can generate new and potentially more 
dangerous forms of market instability through emergent collusion. Addy et al. (2024) further 
highlight the risk of flash crashes and other extreme events triggered by the simultaneous 
reaction of multiple algorithms to the same market signals. These findings suggest that the net 
effect of AI on market stability may be highly context-dependent, varying with market structure, 
the degree of AI adoption, and the diversity of strategies employed by different participants. 
A critical and consistent finding across the literature is the gap between research performance 
and practical deployment. Both Dakalbab et al. (2024) and Bhuiyan et al. (2025) emphasize that 
 

models performing impressively in backtesting often fail to deliver comparable results in live 
markets, due to overfitting, transaction costs, latency, and the non-stationarity of financial data. 
Bridging this gap is arguably the most pressing practical challenge facing the field. 
Looking at how markets are currently governed, it is clear that existing rules were simply not 
built with AI in mind. The kind of collusion that Dou et al. (2025) describe — where algorithms 
quietly learn to cooperate without ever being programmed to do so — is something regulators 
have no real tools to catch or punish. Traditional competition law assumes human intent and 
explicit coordination, neither of which applies here. This is not a minor gap; it points to a 
fundamental mismatch between how markets actually work today and how they are supposed to 
be overseen. 
 
4. Conclusion 
This paper set out to understand how artificial intelligence is changing the world of algorithmic 
trading, and what that means for markets, investors, and regulators. After reviewing five studies 
from 2024 and 2025, three things stand out clearly. 
To begin with, there is little doubt that AI has made trading systems smarter and markets more 
efficient. Deep learning models, especially LSTM networks and hybrid architectures, have 
shown a genuine ability to spot patterns in market data that older methods simply miss — and 
that translates into better predictions and faster price discovery. At the same time, however, these 
same systems are creating risks that nobody fully understands yet. The possibility that competing 
algorithms might independently develop collusive strategies — without any human instruction 
— is deeply troubling, and current regulations offer no meaningful response to it. Finally, there is 
a frustrating gap between how well these models perform in research papers and how they 
actually behave when deployed in real markets. That gap needs to close before AI trading can be 
considered truly mature. 
Like any review, this one has its limits. Only five studies were examined, which means the 
picture is incomplete. The studies also use quite different methods — some build theoretical 
models, others analyze real data, others review existing literature — so drawing direct 
 

comparisons between them is not always straightforward. And given how fast both AI and 
financial markets move, some of what these papers concluded in 2024 may already look different 
today. 
Going forward, the field needs bigger empirical studies that test AI trading systems across 
different countries and asset classes — not just the US or China in isolation. Researchers also 
need to agree on common standards for measuring model performance, so that findings from 
different studies can actually be compared. Most importantly, academics need to work more 
closely with regulators, because the policy conversation is lagging dangerously behind what is 
happening in the market. AI is not slowing down, and the frameworks meant to keep markets fair 
and stable need to catch up.
