Order Imbalance, Order Book Slope and the VolumeVolatility Relation 
 
Huu Nhan Duong, Liwen (Emily) Fang, and Petko S. Kalev*  
 
 
Abstract 
This paper investigates the effect of number of trades, average trade size, order imbalance and 
order book slope on volatility. Examining three interest rate futures (90-day Bank Accepted 
Bill, 3-Year Treasury Bond and 10-Year Treasury Bond) traded on the Australian Securities 
Exchange, it is documented that the number of trades has a more significant impact on 
volatility than the average trade size. Supportive evidence is also observed for the explanatory 
power of order imbalance and order book slope on volatility but these two variables are not 
the main factors determining the relation between volatility and number of trade and size of 
trades.  
 
JEL classification: C35, G15, G25 and G29 
Keywords: Number of trades, trade size, order imbalance, order book slope, volatility

1. Introduction 
This paper investigates the relation between trading volume and stock price volatility for 
three interest rate futures contracts: the 90-day Bank Accepted Bills Futures, the 3-Year 
Treasury Bond Futures and the 10-Year Treasury Bond Futures, on the Australian Securities 
Exchange (ASX). More specifically, the following research questions are addressed. First, 
which of the two components of trading volume, the number of trades or the average trade 
size, has a greater impact on volatility? Second, are order imbalance and order book slope the 
main factors driving the volume-volatility relation?  
  
Investigating the volume-volatility relation has been a topical area of research interest for a 
long time. Karpoff (1987), in his review paper, outlines several reasons why the price-volume 
relation is important. First, this relation provides evidence on issues such as the rate of 
information flow to the market, how information is disseminated in markets, and the extent to 
which market prices convey information. The answers to these issues offer insights into the 
structure of financial markets. Second, the relation is important for event studies that use a 
combination of price and volume data to draw inferences. Thirdly, the price-volume relation 
is also critical to the debate over the empirical distribution of speculative prices.  
 
Although there has been extensive literature on the volume-volatility relation, current 
literature does not agree on the key factor behind the volume-volatility relation. The 
importance of information arrival as the latent factor driving both volume and price volatility 
is highlighted by the Mixture of Distributions Hypothesis of Clark (1973), Epps and Epps 
(1976), Tauchen and Pitts (1983), Harris (1986), Lamoureaux and Lastrapes (1990), and 
Andersen (1996). The role of strategic action between informed and uninformed investors as 
the key factors behind the volume-volatility relation is emphasized by the information models

A positive relation between trading volume and volatility is often observed in prior empirical 
studies.1 Jones et al. (1994) address the question whether the positive volume-volatility 
relation is driven by the number of trades-volatility relation or the trade size-volatility 
relation. The authors document that for NASDAQ stocks, the positive volume-volatility 
relation reflects the positive relation between volatility and the number of trades. The average 
trade size has no information content beyond the number of trades. This finding is consistent 
with strategic microstructure models such as Kyle (1985), Admati and Pfleiderer (1988), and 
Foster and Viswanathan (1990), in which a monopolist informed trader can attempt to 
camouflage his or her trading activity by splitting one large order into several orders of 
smaller sizes. This strategic behaviour attenuates the impact of trade size on volatility and 
implies that the number of trades can contain significant information on prices. Consistent 
with Jones et al. (1994), Gopinath and Krishnamurti (2001) and Huang and Masulis (2003) 
find that the number of trades has a more significant impact on volatility than average trade 
size on the NASDAQ and the London Stock Exchange, respectively.  
 
The role of order imbalance and order book slope in explaining the volume-volatility relation 
is analyzed in more recent empirical studies. After controlling for the effect of order 
imbalance on return, Chan and Fong (2000) document that the volume-volatility relation is 
much weaker for a sample of NYSE and NASDAQ stocks in the last six months of 1993. 
This finding suggests that order imbalance plays a major role in explaining the volumevolatility relation. In contrast, Chan and Fong (2006) find that order imbalance does not add

significant additional explanatory power to realized volatility beyond that of the number of 
trades for Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) index stocks between 1993 and 2000. Naes 
and Skjeltorp (2006) find that the order book slope is negatively related to volatility, trading 
volume, and the volume-volatility relation. Viewing the order book slope as a proxy for 
investors’ divergence of opinions, the findings in their study support the role of divergence of 
opinions in explaining the volume-volatility relation, as proposed by Harris and Raviv (1993) 
and Shalen (1993).   
 
The current study extends the analysis of the role of number of trades, trade size, order 
imbalance and order book slope in explaining the volume-volatility relation to futures 
markets. Examining the sample period between April 2004 and March 2010, a positive 
relation between trading volume and volatility is documented. Consistent with Jones et al. 
(1994), the number of trades is found to play a more significant role in the volume-volatility 
relation than the average trade size. A negative relation between order book slope and 
volatility is also observed, while no significant impact of order imbalance on volatility is 
found. The significance and the explanatory power of order book slope and order imbalance 
on volatility are also less than those of the number of trades and average trade size. The role 
of order imbalance and order book slope in the volume-volatility relation is also examined by 
analyzing the relation between volatility and number of trades and average trade size before 
and after controlling for the impact of daily order imbalance and order book slope on daily 
returns. The results indicate that there is a minimal change to the volatility-number of trades 
and average trade size relation after the return impact of daily order imbalance and order 
book slope is taken into consideration. Therefore, it is concluded that order imbalance and 
order book slope are not the main factors influencing the volume-volatility relation. Other

2. Data 
Intraday data for the 90-day Bank Accepted Bill futures, 3-Year Treasury Bond futures and 
10-Year Treasury Bond futures for the period between April 1, 2004 and March 31, 2010 are 
collected from the Securities Industry Research Centre of Asia-Pacific (SIRCA). Two 
different datasets are obtained from SIRCA. The first dataset contains information on the 
three best limit prices on the bid and ask side, in association with the total volume (number of 
shares) and the total number of orders at each price level. This dataset is updated whenever 
there is a change to the price and/or volume to any of these three best limit prices. All of the 
observations in which the bid price is greater than the ask price at any of the three limit price 
levels or where the bid (ask) prices are not in strict descending (ascending) order from the 
first to the tenth best prices are removed from the dataset. The second dataset provides 
information on all the transactions executed in the 90-day Bank Accepted Bill Futures, 3-year 
Treasury Bond Futures and 10-year Treasury Bond Futures including the transaction date and 
time to the nearest hundredth of a second, transaction price and trading volume (number of 
futures contracts traded).  
 
These two datasets are matched to identify the best prevailing bid and ask quote for all 
transactions. A method similar to the Lee and Ready (1991)’s algorithm is then followed to

closest-to-maturity contract are included. The closest-to-maturity contract is “rolled over”

5. Conclusions 
The volume-volatility relation and the role of order imbalance and order book slope in 
explaining this relation are investigated in this study. Although there are many empirical 
studies explored the volume-volatility relation, there is still no general consensus about what 
is the actual reason for this relation. This paper provides new insights into this issue in futures 
markets. Examining the 90-day Bank Accepted Bill futures, 3-Year Treasury Bond Futures 
and 10-Year Treasury Bond futures on the ASX for the period between April 2004 and 
March 2010, it is observed that the number of trades is substantially more significant than 
average trade size in explaining volatility. This finding suggests that the number of trades 
carries more volatility information than the average trade size. One plausible rationale behind 
this could be that informed investors strategically split their large orders into several smaller 
orders to hide their information. A negative relation between volatility and the order book 
slope is also documented. However, little change is found in the relation between volatility 
and number of trades and average trade size after controlling for the impact of order book 
slope and order imbalance on daily returns. It is therefore concluded that order book slope 
and order imbalance are not the main factors influencing the volume-volatility relation.
