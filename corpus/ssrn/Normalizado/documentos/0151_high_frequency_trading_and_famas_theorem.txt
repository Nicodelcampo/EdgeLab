High Frequency Trading and Fama’s Theorem① 
Xie Ping, Wu Liang 
China Investment Corporation, Beijing, China 
 
Abstract: This study explores the impact of high-frequency trading (HFT) on 
market dynamics, drawing an analogy to the distortions in time and space in relativistic 
theory. It extends the scope of HFT order learning, incorporating the shared learning of 
high-frequency traders with fundamental investors (FI) and retail orders (RI). Analyzing 
within the Kyle two-stage model framework, the study concludes that learning from retail 
orders disrupts the price martingale process, while learning from fundamental investor 
order flow does not. It highlights a decrease in market fundamental information and the 
potential for market collapse termination. This research establishes a link between HFT 
order learning and market stability, challenging Fama’s Theorem. It suggests that HFT 
may introduce time and information unfairness, emphasizing the need for financial 
theories to address technological impacts and regulatory focus on ensuring fairness in 
time and information. 
Keywords: high frequency trading, Fama’s theorem, order anticipation, time, market 
crash 
1. Introduction 
This study is aimed to elucidate the profound changes in financial market 
microstructure caused by HFT and their impact on financial theory. The rise of HFT has 
sparked continuous controversy, primarily concerning unfair competition and market 
instability. The IPO application filed by Virtu, a HFT company, in 2014, stating that they 
only incurred losses on 1 out of 1238 trading days, stirred significant debate. This 
anomaly contradicts the principles of the "Fama’s Theorem," forming the starting point 
for our analysis of microeconomic changes in finance. In May 2014, Charlie Munger 
criticized HFT as "legalized front-running" in an interview with CNBC. Simultaneously, 
stock markets in Europe and the U.S. often experienced various flash crashes, with the 
most notable being the 2010 "Flash Crash," where U.S. stocks plummeted 10% in a single 
day. Despite regulatory responses, flash crash incidents have continued, albeit on a 
smaller scale. These controversies persist, and concerns about HFT remain prevalent in 
media reports. The root causes of frequent flash crashes still lack a standardized 
theoretical analysis (Cespa and Vives, 2023). 
Penetrating these controversies is no easy task. Michael Lewis's 2014 book, "Flash 
Boys," contends that "until 2008, major telecommunications operators were not aware of 
the significant changes occurring in the financial markets, more directly, they did not 
recognize the value of '1 millisecond'" (Lewis, 2010). O'Hara (2015) notes that HFT has 
brought revolutionary changes to the market. It not only accelerates trading but 
fundamentally alters market trading and organizational structures. The ways in which 
market liquidity supply and price discovery function have also undergone complete 
transformation. 
Information learning is a vital aspect of the market microstructure, and current 
research indicates that HFT has significantly altered market information. Specifically,

order substitution, where orders replace trades, is now considered the fundamental unit 
of information learning. Beyond a company's fundamental value, additional knowledge 
is seen as information, challenging its perceived value. Some argue that information from 
HFT lacks value and raises costs for fundamental researchers, reducing market

Concerns arise from frequent market flash crashes, with O'Hara suggesting that HFT 
as market makers (MM) may exit when suspecting others with superior information, 
potentially contributing to instability. Yet, underlying issues are not fully understood. 
O'Hara proposes redefining information learning and liquidity in the HFT era, requiring 
new microstructure models. 
How to address these challenges? Einstein's theory of relativity offers valuable 
insights. Just as time slows down and space warps as objects approach the speed of light, 
could the trading speed in financial markets, approaching the speed of light, distort the 
time in financial markets? Building on this intuition, this study explores the impact of 
HFT information learning on market price formation, with a core conclusion that HFT 
introduces time unfairness, potentially challenging Fama’s Theorem. In contrast to 
O'Hara's (2015) viewpoint that time becomes a meaningless concept in the era of HFT, 
this paper makes a breakthrough by suggesting the distortion of time in the highfrequency era. 
In the realm of modern microeconomics shaping the fundamental definition of 
financial markets, time is characterized as an "independently objective variable." It is 
expected to be fair and non-informative for all market participants. Financial markets 
commonly adhere to the bidding pricing principle of "time priority, price priority," 
meaning that, under equal time conditions, precedence is given to the order with a 
superior price, and under equivalent prices, the order submitted earlier takes precedence. 
This implies, from a mathematical standpoint, the independence and lack of correlation 
between order time and price. In essence, time is deemed fair for all investor-placed orders. 
Furthermore, as prices serve as a representation of market information, they exhibit a lack 
of correlation with time. 
This conceptual foundation finds its underpinning in Fama’s Theorem. Grounded in 
the Efficient Market Hypothesis, Fama’s Theorem posits that prices consistently "fully 
reflect" available information, leading to market categorization into weak-form, semistrong-form, and strong-form efficient markets based on varying information sets. 
Mathematically, these categories are quantified by the martingale properties of price 
sequences pertaining to distinct information sets (Shiryaev, 1999). Fama’s Theorem, 
encapsulating the notion that time and price changes are unrelated within a specified 
information set, stands as a pivotal cornerstone in modern classical financial theory. It 
plays a crucial normative role in governing the smooth operation and regulation of 
contemporary financial markets. 
This study examines the significant changes brought by HFT to the microstructure 
of financial markets, specifically focusing on the new information learning mechanism. 
Our hypothesis regarding the information advantage of HFT draws inspiration from Yang 
& Zhu (2020), who introduced the concept of HFT learning from the order flow of 
fundamental investors and executing preemptive trades. This paper expands their concept 
to including learning from both fundamental investors and noise investors (retail traders).

Their approach is rooted in Madrigal's (1996) concept of non-fundamental speculators, a 
category of traders profiting not from private fundamental information but from a 
sophisticated understanding of the trading process or environment, estimating 
fundamental information from publicly available data. Yang & Zhu (2020) apply 
Madrigal's framework to the modern market, proposing that HFT can learn from the order 
flow of fundamental investors, predict their future trading behavior, and implement 
preemptive "front-running" trading strategies. 
This study contends that the order flow information of RI also possesses a plausible 
and realistic learnable nature. Theoretically, all order flows in the market are potential 
subjects for HFT learning. Unlike traditional market participants, HFT's primary 
advantage lies in information technology, primarily targeted at the computer servers 
central to modern exchanges (O'Hara, 2015). Orders in the market are sent and aggregated 
to these servers, sorted based on specific rules (such as time priority, price priority). HFT, 
leveraging advantages like hosting on exchange servers and high-speed computing 
capabilities, can better grasp this data in terms of volume, speed, and quality. Therefore, 
regardless of the investor type generating the order flow, it falls within the potential 
learning scope of HFT. Furthermore, the traditional focus on order anticipation strategies 
has mainly centered on HFT predicting "large orders" from fundamental investors, such 
as SEC (2010) and Yang & Zhu (2020). The underlying logic is that these "large orders" 
exhibit a strong correlation with future price fluctuations, allowing profitable anticipation 
through predictive strategies. However, retail orders also exhibit a correlation with future 
price fluctuations. While traditional theory suggests noise characteristics in retail orders, 
increasing empirical evidence indicates that RI exhibit diverse types and corresponding 
behavioral characteristics, providing some predictability for future price fluctuations. 
Lastly, the predictive characteristics of RI have become stronger, not weaker, in many 
markets. For instance, in the United States, retail trading volume accounted for 20% of 
the overall market in 2021, doubling from a decade ago (McCabe, 2021). Particularly 
since the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, a significant amount of savings 
from U.S. residents flowed into the stock market. With commission-free brokerage, 
simplified and gamified trading platforms, and significantly lowered account opening 
thresholds (even as low as $1), a large influx of first-time investors, often referred to as 
"Robinhood" investors, entered the market. These investors exhibit a strong tendency for 
momentum trading and chasing hot trends, and empirical evidence suggests that they have 
a more significant impact than ordinary RI (Eaton, Green, Roseman, and Wu, 2021). 
Methodologically, this study employs the two-stage model proposed by Kyle (1985), 
assuming the existence of two stages of centralized auctions. In each stage, fundamental 
investors and retail traders submit orders to MM, who aggregate the orders and determine 
prices to clear the market. Building on Yang & Zhu (2020), HFTs in the market learn with 
a certain precision from the order flow of fundamental investors and retail traders in the 
first stage and subsequently submit orders only in the second stage. 
This study extends the approach in two ways: Firstly, HFT firms not only learn from 
fundamental investors but also from retail traders. Regarding the learning from retail 
traders, this study references Madrigal (1996) and sets the learning to encompass only 
partial information about retail trader order behavior. However, unlike Yang & Zhu (2020), 
where there are multiple HFT firms and fundamental investors, this study has only one of 
each. This implies the omission of considerations for internal competition among HFT 
firms and approximates the information intensity of fundamental investors' private 
information with that of a single fundamental investor. This relaxation is deemed

Secondly, this study takes a more macroscopic analysis approach. In contrast to the 
emphasis of Yang & Zhu (2020) on the specific strategic behaviors of participants, this 
study focuses on the overall market impact, including the martingale conditions of Fama’s 
Theorem, market information levels, and stability. 
The main conclusions and contributions of this study are as follows: Firstly, the 
learning capability of HFT orders leads to a decrease in the "fundamental information 
content" of the market. It may even break Fama’s Theorem when learning from retail 
traders, causing the breakdown of the martingale conditions and a malfunction in the price 
process. This breakthrough and malfunction have not been previously addressed in the 
literature. Secondly, the study provides a reasonable theoretical explanation for market 
flash crashes. The learning ability of HFT orders endows time with information, exclusive 
to HFT firms, resulting in information asymmetry. This contravenes the principles of time 
fairness and information fairness in financial markets, leading to market instability. 
Thirdly, the study suggests regulatory directions and priorities. The impact of HFT is 
underestimated primarily because modern financial pricing theories have not considered 
the influence of technological advancements. Looking ahead, with the rise of artificial 
intelligence, this challenge is growing. This points to future regulatory priorities, 
emphasizing the maintenance of time fairness and information fairness in the market. 
The structure of this study is as follows: Part two introduces related literature, Part 
three details the extended information learning model's setup and solution, Part four 
examines the impacts received on the overall market level, and Part five presents 
conclusions, insights, and limitations. 
2. Related Literature 
This study intersects with two primary facets of existing research. Firstly, it is closely 
associated with the field of HFT order anticipation. The present work extends prevailing 
assumptions in a methodologically robust manner, providing fresh insights from a distinct 
analytical perspective. Secondly, it is particularly relevant to research on market quality 
during the HFT era. This study underscores the crucial role of order anticipation in 
influencing the overall market, contributing to the broader discourse on market dynamics 
within the context of HFT. 
2.1 Research on HFT order anticipation 
HFT leverages its speed advantage to infer implicit information in the orders of other 
investors. However, theoretical research on how HFT performs such inference in practice 
involves various assumptions. Early discussions on prediction were relatively simple and 
passive. For instance, Menkveld & Zoican (2017) and Budish et al. (2015) assumed that 
HFT seizes "stale" orders resulting from the rush to incorporate new public information. 
Baldauf & Mollner (2020) discussed how HFT captures identical orders across different 
markets and exploits their delays for prediction and front-running. These predictive 
behaviors are relatively "unintelligent," merely applying the speed advantage of HFT. 
Recent research places more emphasis on sequence learning and prediction of order 
flow by HFT, exhibiting greater intelligence and proactivity. However, the focus has 
primarily been on learning from large orders of institutional investors. Notable studies in 
this area include Brunnermeier & Pedersen (2005), Boulatov et al. (2016), Yang & Zhu 
(2020), and Xu & Cheng (2023). The latter two are particularly relevant to this study, as

both exclusively consider learning from institutional large orders. The difference lies in 
the incorporation of HFT inventory management constraints in the latter, allowing the 
model to comprehensively consider both order prediction and market-making liquidity 
provision behaviors. In contrast, this study, building on learning from institutional large 
orders, introduces learning from retail traders and focuses its discussion on the overall 
market impact, diverging from their emphasis on the strategic behaviors of market 
participants. 
Furthermore, theoretical research on learning from retail trader orders in the era of 
HFT is relatively scarce. Madrigal (1996) introduced the assumption of learning from 
noise traders in Kyle's two-stage model. However, the empirical basis is not robust, 
relying on the assumption that some investors understand the trading process or 
environment better, enabling them to infer fundamental information from public 
information. The analysis is confined to the strategic behavior of participants, and the 
conclusions generally suggest improved liquidity but potentially reduced market 
informativeness. 
In contrast, this study, with the inclusion of learning from retail traders, focuses its 
analytical perspective on the overall market, considering aspects like martingale 
conditions and the possibility of market collapse. These perspectives have been 
overlooked in previous studies, resulting in significantly different conclusions in this 
work. 
In empirical research, there is a considerable body of work focusing on the empirical 
analysis of order prediction for institutional investors. One notable study is by Hirschey 
(2020), which utilizes Nasdaq market data to discover that HFT firms often submit 
aggressive buy or sell orders ahead of other investors' intensive orders in the same 
direction. This finding, after controlling for factors like HFT's faster response to public 
information such as news, is attributed primarily to HFT firms' ability to identify patterns 
in past trades and orders. They can predict and execute trades ahead of other investors' 
subsequent order flow. Additionally, Korajczyk & Murphy (2019) and Van Kervel & 
Menkveld (2019) validate similar results using data from the Canadian and Swedish stock 
markets, respectively. These studies, along with Yang & Zhu (2020), exhibit mutual 
citation, suggesting potential cross-inspiration among them. 
There is relatively limited empirical research on predicting retail trader orders in 
HFT. Hirschey (2021) conducted an event study using the regulatory policy changes for 
HFT in Canada in 2012. The study found that HFT, overall, benefits RI, especially those 
using limit orders. However, the analysis primarily focuses on the improvement in 
market-wide liquidity after the participation of HFT, presenting a relatively broad 
perspective. 
In recent years, the substantial growth of individual investors, exemplified by 
platforms like Robinhood, has attracted research attention. Eaton et al. (2022) 
investigated how the heterogeneity in the maturity of RI influences their impact on 
financial markets through broker platform outages. They found that Robinhood investors 
tend to follow momentum, while the overall retail order flow is contrarian. The most 
relevant evidence for the discussion of learning from retail trader orders, as explored in 
this study, comes from Barber et al. (2022). They demonstrated that attention-induced 
herding behavior among Robinhood investors is associated with significant price 
fluctuations and subsequent reversals. The study also highlighted that savvy investors 
might exploit this trading pattern for profit. This provides some inspiration for empirical 
research on retail trader orders in the era of HFT, aligning with the themes discussed in

2.2 Market Quality Research in the Era of HFT 
The research on the impact of HFT on market quality is extensive, but the common 
pattern is to consider the speed advantage of HFT as the starting point. The specific 
application of this speed advantage or the adoption of certain strategies is crucial in 
determining its impact on the market. Menkveld (2016) categorized related studies into 
seven aspects, including research on the speed advantage of HFT in limit order markets, 
the use of speed advantage to capture large orders, seizing "stale" orders after the release 
of public information, connecting multiple markets, employing flash-cancel orders, 
establishing beneficial intermediary chains, and meeting customer demands for fast 
trading. This article takes a different approach, emphasizing that HFT is not only fast but 
also "smart" due to its information technology advantage. As information technology 
continues to advance, challenges arising from order prediction strategies become more 
significant, emphasizing their crucial impact on market quality in the era of HFT. 
The "flash crash" that occurred in the U.S. stock market in 2010 raised widespread 
concerns about market stability, with HFT as major suspects (SEC, 2010). As research 
progressed, opinions became more diverse. Brogaard et al. (2018) pointed out that HFT, 
acting as MM, also face limitations in providing liquidity during extreme market volatility, 
challenging the perception that they cause extreme market fluctuations. Madhavan (2013) 
suggested that an excessively diversified and complex market structure could foster the 
possibility of extreme price volatility. Bongaerts et al. (2016) introduced the concept of 
HFT technology investment costs, concluding that when the input-output ratio of 
technology is high, severe information asymmetry can occur, leading to market 
interruptions in equilibrium. Cespa & Vives (2023) attributed the issue to market opacity 
resulting from factors such as information friction caused by algorithmic trading and 
market segmentation, leading to an upward-sloping market liquidity demand curve. This 
curve implies that when participants' liquidity demand increases, market liquidity supply 
becomes insufficient, creating market fragility. This article provides a more 
comprehensive theoretical explanation for the current market instability, extending from 
technological advantages to information advantages and market instability driven by 
strategic behavior. It distinguishes the different impacts of HFT learning predictions on 
institutional large orders and retail orders, suggesting that the latter is more likely to lead 
to market collapse, particularly when time becomes unfair.

4. Market Dynamic Analysis 
This section examines the impact of high-frequency trading on the market, including 
characteristics of martingale processes, information rents, and market crashes. As 
mentioned earlier, high-frequency trading holds significant advantages in information 
technology, leading to new forms of market information learning. Understanding how this 
transformation alters the market microstructure is a crucial issue in current research on 
market dynamics (O’Hara, 2015). The Fama’s Theorem is a cornerstone of modern 
financial theory, and the martingale process is a test assumption for market prices to 
adhere to the law. Therefore, this section employs both mathematical derivation and 
numerical simulation to examine of these aspects. Additionally, FIs earn rents based on 
their privately held fundamental information. Higher rents incentivize FIs to engage in 
fundamental research, contributing to more effective price discovery and resource 
allocation (Stiglitz, 2014). Consequently, this section calculates the expected returns of 
FIs to measure the level of rent. Finally, frequent occurrences of market crashes indicate 
issues with market liquidity. To address this, the paper introduces a constraint on the 
market maker's risk capital to ensure it is not less than zero, based on a risk-neutral market 
maker.

4.2 Information Rent 
The primary function of financial markets is to facilitate the efficient allocation of 
resources. Therefore, although the concept of information in the Efficient Market 
Hypothesis (EMH) is not precisely defined, it is generally considered to be information 
related to the fundamental aspects of the companies represented by securities (Stiglitz, 
2014). However, the Kyle two-stage model implicitly assumes that the information level 
of fundamental investors is predetermined. Therefore, the commonly used indicator 
1var( | )
v y , representing the market's information level in the Kyle model, is not suitable. 
Madrigal (1996), Bond et al. (2010) introduced the concept of research costs, meaning 
that, based on Kyle's two-stage model, fundamental investors pay research costs in 
advance to obtain information about the fundamental value. Following a similar line of

thought, we consider the expected benefits of fundamental investors as the "information 
rent" they obtain. The higher the level of rent, the greater the incentive they receive, 
corresponding to a market formation that is more favorable for the efficient allocation of 
resources.

4.2.3 Further Discussion

4.3 A model explanation for the market crash 
Corollary 4: When HFT only learns from FI, a market collapse will not occur; 
When the degree of HFT from RI increases, there is a possibility of market-making 
cessation and market crash. 
Proof: It’s quite obvious that assuming that the market maker is completely riskneutral is too idealistic, and MM are bound to face certain upper limits on losses. For 
example, in reality, MM face capital constraints, and once the capital is lost, they face 
the risk of bankruptcy. For simplicity, this paper further assumes, based on the 
assumption of market maker risk-neutrality, that the market maker's losses have an 
upper limit Ω, and when the losses exceed Ω, the market maker will stop quoting. 
The importance of the martingale process lies in ensuring the normal operation of 
the market. When the market's price martingale is established, the market maker's 
expected profit and loss should be zero. The expected returns of FI and HFT all come 
from the losses of RI. Therefore, in numerical simulations, the market maker's expected 
loss is equal to the sum of the losses of RI and the expected returns of FI and HFT. 
Figures (2f) and (3f) simulate the expected profit and loss of the market maker 
when HFT only learn from FI and only learn from RI, respectively. The results show: 
firstly, Figure (2f) shows that the simulated market maker's expected loss is strictly 
equal to 0, so when HFT learn from FI, a market collapse will not occur; secondly, 
Figure (3f) shows that the market maker may incur losses, and as the degree of HFT 
learning from RI increases, the losses may increase exponentially. Therefore, there is a 
possibility as shown in Figure (4), when m is less than m*, the market maker's losses 
exceed Ω, and the rational behavior of the market maker is to exit the market.

5. Conclusions 
This study establishes a stylized model to demonstrate that HFT may distort the 
market, leading to the breakdown of Fama’s Theorem. Expanding the existing research 
on HFT order prediction, the paper introduces novel insights by incorporating the joint 
learning of HFT on both fundamental investor and RI orders within the Kyle two-stage 
model framework. The examination focuses on key aspects, including the price 
martingale process, market information rent, and the potential for market collapse. 
Results indicate that learning from RI orders disrupts the price martingale process, 
while learning from fundamental investor orders does not. The study underscores the 
nuanced differences in information learning, emphasizing that HFT gain an advantage 
over MM through retail order learning, leading to pricing deviations and potential 
market collapse. This research provides a comprehensive perspective on the challenges 
posed by HFT to market stability, contributing to a deeper understanding of frequent 
flash crashes in contemporary financial markets. 
One of the insights from this paper is that time becomes unfair in the era of HFT. 
HFT possesses the ability to extract informative signals from market-related temporal 
variables (i.e., order flows of other investors), rendering time informational. However, 
due to the extension of HFT's information technology advantage to extremely short 
time dimensions, surpassing human physiological limits, machines gain a significant 
edge. This temporal informativeness is exclusive to HFT, resulting in unfairness. The 
high technical barriers of entry in HFT sustain this unfairness, allowing for the 
possibility of a "continuous free lunch." In efficient markets, occasional free lunches 
are permissible, but sustained free lunches are excluded. Shleifer (2004) suggests that 
due to low entry barriers and high competition, the "half-life" of proprietary competitive 
advantages in financial markets should be relatively short. However, the advent of HFT 
has altered this landscape: sustained time advantage equates to information advantage. 
This article's second insight is that modern classical financial theory needs to pay 
more attention to the highly impactful technological changes. From a methodological 
perspective, the foundation of financial economics lies in general equilibrium, which is 
also the core foundation of the entire economics. Market competition occurs among 
"economic agents," achieving equilibrium through "effective" competition for 
economic "rents" to achieve the efficient allocation of societal resources. This 
equilibrium relies on core concepts in economics such as rational individuals, marginal 
utility, and welfare. The assumption that individuals are the smallest and most basic 
units in the economic world, and that all economic phenomena can be decomposed into 
the decisions and behaviors of individuals, forms the foundational hypothesis. However, 
this assumption differs significantly from the actual situation since the advent of the 
industrial society, where technology has played the most crucial role. Nevertheless, 
finance remains a relatively young discipline. The efficient market hypothesis emerged 
in the 1970s when market mechanisms were still predominantly manual, and semiautomated technologies were gradually being applied. Taking a broader historical 
perspective of 300 years of financial market history, there has never been continuous 
competition between humans and machines in financial markets. It's no wonder that 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4708532

 
23 
 
figures like Walras, Markowitz, Sharpe, and Fama, whether in the past or later, did not 
consider the influence of technology. 
The third insight of this article highlights the need for current regulatory efforts to 
place greater emphasis on technological impacts, particularly ensuring fairness in terms 
of time and information. Ongoing technological advancements continue to exert a 
widening influence. Despite the saturation of HFT in Western markets, the essence of 
technological progress lies in its continual transcendence of limits (Stigler, 2004). 
Recent breakthroughs in artificial intelligence (AI) position it as a potential core 
technology, operating on the assumption that human intelligence can be simulated and, 
once accuracy reaches a certain level, machines can replace human intelligence. The 
alignment of AI's algorithmic advantage with the data advantage of HFT suggests the 
potential for more disruptive trading patterns. Regulatory focus should preemptively 
address these innovations, avoiding a repetition of the delayed response seen after the 
2010 U.S. flash crash. Financial markets, designed to facilitate efficient resource 
allocation, should prioritize time fairness and information fairness, as emphasized in 
this article, in future regulatory measures. 
Furthermore, the model's limitation lies in its adoption of a specialized setting with 
concentrated auctions by MM, deviating significantly from the prevalent continuous 
trading with limit order books in real-world exchanges. Considering that HFT often 
execute trades on behalf of RI in the current U.S. stock market, where virtually all 
participants engage in HFT, the model's applicability may be limited. Additionally, the 
model's treatment of liquidity is at a preliminary stage. Future improvements should 
aim at refining the model's design to better align with real-world conditions.
