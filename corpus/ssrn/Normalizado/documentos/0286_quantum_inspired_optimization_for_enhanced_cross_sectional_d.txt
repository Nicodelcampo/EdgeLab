Simulated Annealing (SA)
We optimize over discrete states x (basket pairs, subsets) with kernel:
1
Propose small random edits to x (add/drop/swap elements).
2
Accept improvements with probability 1.
3
Accept deteriorations with probability exp(∆/T), ∆< 0, at temperature
T > 0.
At fixed T, the chain targets πT(x) ∝ef (x)/T (detailed balance). With slow
cooling Tq ↓0, SA converges (in probability) to global maximizers under classical
conditions.

Metropolis-Hastings
Neighborhood. From state x (subject to constraints), propose x′ via a local
edit. Let
∆:= f (x′) −f (x),
T > 0.
Acceptance (Metropolis).
α(x →x′) = min{1, exp(∆/T)}.
Improvements (∆≥0) are accepted; deteriorations (∆< 0) are accepted with
probability exp(∆/T), the ”accept-worse” rule.

time-varying inverse temperature β(s); this is equivalent after sign conventions
for maximization vs. minimization.

Simulated Annealing: Convergence Guarantees
(1) Stationarity at fixed T. With symmetric proposals (or Metropolis–Hastings
ratio), the Markov chain has invariant distribution
πT(x) ∝ef (x)/T,
πT(x) P(x →y) = πT(y) P(y →x)
(detailed balance).
(2) Global convergence under slow cooling. On a finite state space with
irreducible proposals, if the cooling schedule is
Tq =
v
log(1 + q),
q = 1, 2, . . . ,
(c large enough),
then the SA trajectory visits a global maximizer
x⋆∈arg max f (x)
with probability →1 as q →∞(Geman & Geman, 1984; Hajek, 1988). q is the
iteration index; v sets the cooling speed. Larger v means slower cooling, which
strengthens theoretical convergence guarantees but may be impractical in finite
time.
In applications, a linear inverse–temperature ramp β(s) is often effective;
warm-starting near a continuous relaxation further accelerates convergence.

Experiments
Key Findings
1) Lower cardinality (k) reverts faster & more often.
k=2: bulk of mass in [1−10] business day; short right tail; low Never.
k=3: slower and less reliable than k=2.
k=5: long right tail; many signals never hit the target.
2) 1% targets are much quicker than 2%.
Across all k, 1% distributions are left-shifted (steeper cumulatives).

Conclusion
Conclusion
Experiments confirm that larger baskets revert faster and more cleanly due
to reduced idiosyncratic noise, but with lower cardinality (k = 2) mean
reversion is sharper and more frequent.
For enhanced pairs trading, higher cardinality (k = 5 ≫k = 3) consistently
outperforms across all setups, even when using hierarchical indicators.
⇒In pair-trades, cardinality and hierarchy matter more than the number
of assets.
Deeper A–vs.–B contrasts lead to faster pair reversion, while basket size
stabilizes z-scores.
→Use large baskets for stable signals; within them, exploit deeper contrasts.
iQ algorithms enable these computations. Classical approaches (MIP, brute
force) are infeasible for these nonlinear, combinatorial problems, not even
solvable with mixed-integer relaxations.
We are extending to credit, where products depend even more on qualitative
(rating, issuer, capital structure) and quantitative variables.
