Balancing Profit and Risk: Hybrid Reinforcement
Learning for Algorithmic Trading

Abstract. Reinforcement learning (RL) can discover trading policies directly from market data, but profit-only objectives often yield unstable,
high-variance behavior. We study risk-aware reward designs that explicitly balance profit and risk, including volatility and drawdown penalties as well as a Sharpe-hybrid objective. Using daily data equities and
ETFs, we train Q-learning and Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and evalu- ate
out-of-sample in two regimes: the high-volatility COVID-19 period and
the mixed post-pandemic period. Risk-aware rewards improve risk- adjusted performance versus profit-only objectives. In 2020–2021, DQN
with a Sharpe-hybrid reward attains a mean Sharpe of ∼1.93 and mean
maximum drawdown near 15% across assets; results remain favorable
in 2022–2024. To ensure correctness and reproducibility, all simulated
trades were systematically logged and verified. We analyze learning dynamics, sensitivity to penalty strengths, and transaction-cost impacts,
and we highlight dispersion across assets, suggesting the need for portfolio level risk controls. These findings indicate that shaping the RL reward toward risk-adjusted objectives yields more stable and practically
relevant trading policies.
1. Introduction
Algorithmic trading has transformed financial markets by enabling the systematic execution of trading strategies through data-driven models. Among these
approaches, reinforcement learning (RL) has gained attention due to its ability
to learn optimal trading policies directly from historical market data. RL agents
can explore and adapt to dynamic environments, making sequential decisions
buy, sell, or hold based on rewards tied to portfolio performance.
While recent studies have demonstrated that RL agents can outperform traditional rule
based strategies, they typically prioritize raw profit maximization; however, professional traders and hedge funds emphasize risk adjusted returns, evaluating
performance with metrics like the Sharpe ratio, Sortino ratio, or maximum drawdown, which better capture the trade off between profit and risk exposure.
The absence of risk considerations in RL trading research presents a critical gap. An agent that only maximizes returns may take excessive risks, producing unstable results that are impractical for real world deployment. To address
this limitation, this study proposes an RL framework that integrates risk aware
reward functions, combining profitability with explicit penalties for volatility and
drawdowns. By shaping the reward function to reflect real-world risk preferences,

we aim to create trading strategies that are both profitable and stable under varying market conditions.
This paper makes the following contributions:
• We design and test custom RL reward functions that balance profit and
risk, including hybrid formulations of the profit-to-Sharpe ratio.
• We benchmark risk-aware RL agents against standard RL agents and simple rule-based trading strategies.
• We evaluated the impact of risk-aware learning on portfolio performance
using historical data in different market conditions.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews previous
work on RL in trading and risk-adjusted performance metrics. Section 3 describes
the data and methodology, including our RL environment and the reward design.
Section 4 presents experimental results, followed by a discussion in Section 5
and conclusions in Section 6.
2. Prior Work
Reinforcement learning (RL) has seen increasing application in algorithmic trading, particularly with the rise of deep learning approaches in finance. (Moody &
Saffell, 2001) pioneered the use of RL to model trading as a sequential decisionmaking process, where agents learn policies directly from historical price data
and adapt to dynamic environments. Their experiments demonstrated that direct
reinforcement learning could produce profitable strategies; however, they did not
incorporate risk-adjusted performance metrics, which often resulted in strategies
exhibiting high volatility.
Building on this foundation, (Deng et al., 2016) introduced Deep Q-Networks
(DQN) to financial trading, reporting that DQN agents were capable of outperforming traditional rule-based strategies and achieving strong cumulative returns.
Nevertheless, their findings also revealed that DQN models are highly sensitive
to hyperparameter settings and market conditions, frequently leading to unstable
performance. Similarly, (Jiang et al., 2017) applied deep reinforcement learning
to cryptocurrency trading and observed that while the agents achieved substantial
raw profits, they experienced significant drawdowns and elevated volatility, raising
concerns about their practical viability in real-world trading.
More recent research has focused on integrating risk considerations into
RL trading frameworks. (Yu & Lee, 2020) proposed a risk-aware reward function
that penalizes portfolio volatility, demonstrating that this approach effectively reduced drawdowns and improved Sharpe ratios compared to profit-only models.
Likewise, (Almahdi & Yang, 2017) developed an adaptive portfolio trading system using deep RL, showing that incorporating risk constraints led to more stable
performance and fewer large losses, albeit with a slight reduction in raw returns.
Despite these advancements, the systematic study of hybrid reward functions—those that balance raw profit with explicit risk metrics (e.g., Sharpe ratio, volatility, and drawdown penalties) remains underexplored. Most existing approaches either maximize profit without regard for risk or focus on optimizing a

single risk-adjusted measure, which often limits adaptability across diverse market conditions. This research aims to address this gap by investigating a continuum of risk–reward trade-offs, with the goal of identifying RL configurations that
achieve both robust profitability and improved stability across varying financial
environments.
3. Data and Methodology
3.1. Data Collection
We utilize historical price data for a selection of liquid stocks and exchange-traded
funds (ETFs), including AAPL, MSFT, and SPY, to ensure coverage of both individual equities and broader market indices. Data is sourced from Yahoo Finance
via the Python yfinance API, spanning a 9-year period (2015–2024) with daily frequency. Each data point includes Open, High, Low, Close, and Volume (OHLCV)
values.
To ensure a robust evaluation, we use historical stock and ETF data spanning both stable and volatile market regimes. Our dataset is divided into three
phases:
(i) a pre-pandemic period (2015–2019) representing stable market conditions
(ii) a high-volatility period (2020–2021) covering the COVID-19 market
shock
(iii) a post-pandemic period (2022–2024) reflecting recovery and mixed
trends.
This setup provides insight into whether risk-aware reward functions improve robustness when market conditions differ from the training environment.

3.3. Action Space
The agent operates in a discrete action space:
• Buy (a = 1): Opens or increases a long position.
• Sell (a = 2): Closes a long position or opens a short position.
• Hold (a = 0): Maintains the current position.
The agent maintains a position across time steps until an opposing action is
taken, mimicking the cost and inertia of real-world trading.

trading intuition. The volatility penalty weight λ was chosen to meaningfully reduce exposure in turbulent markets without overly suppressing profitable trades
(λ ≈0.5). The drawdown penalty weight β was tuned to prioritize avoiding deep
portfolio losses while still allowing recovery trades (β ≈0.3). The Sharpe hybrid
weight α was set to balance raw return and stability (α ≈0.6), consistent with
previous studies showing that moderate risk weighting tends to maximize out-ofsample Sharpe ratios.
These reward formulations allow us to study how different risk preferences affect
the agent’s learned behavior and performance outcomes.
3.5. Reinforcement Learning Models
We experiment with two reinforcement learning algorithms:
1. Q-Learning
A tabular method that updates a state-action value table using the Bellman
equation. Due to the limited state granularity, this is used mainly for simple
environments.
2. Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
An extension of Q-learning that uses a neural network to approximate the
Q-value function. It enables learning in continuous and high-dimensional
state spaces. We implement DQN using the stable-baselines3 library
with standard settings (e.g., replay buffer, target network, and epsilongreedy exploration).
Both models learn a policy that maps observed features to actions in order to
maximize cumulative discounted rewards.

The performance of all strategies was assessed using cumulative return and
Sharpe ratio as the primary metrics, with maximum drawdown and volatility considered as complementary measures. Immediate observations:
• DQN consistently outperforms Q-Learning in median return across reward types.
For both test periods, DQN agents achieved substantially

Across algorithms, the profit-only reward exhibited lower median returns

consistency measure, the agent learns to avoid high-variance strategies
that generate occasional spikes but underperform over the long run.
Underlying factors:
1. Function approximation and representation. DQN leverages a neural network to generalize across continuous, normalized features (returns, EMAs,
RSI, volatility, position), enabling more nuanced conditional behavior (e.g.,
reducing exposure during high rolling volatility) than a coarse tabular QLearning policy can achieve.

2. Stability and sample efficiency trade-offs.
While DQN can learn richer
policies, it is more data-intensive and may require stabilizing techniques

instability. Q-Learning can converge faster in small, discrete problems, but
its limited representational capacity reduces performance in realistic trading environments.
3. Reward shaping and risk control.
Incorporating volatility or drawdown
penalties modifies the reward landscape, providing explicit negative feedback for risky actions. This reduces the likelihood of large adverse excursions that would otherwise inflate standard deviation and depress median

4.4. Learning Behaviour and Agent Analysis
The following analysis is based on the experimental results reported in Section 4.3
Training dynamics and convergence.
Across the experimental configurations,
Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and tabular Q-Learning exhibited markedly different
learning dynamics. DQN, employing a neural function approximator, typically required substantially more environment interactions to converge but yielded smoother
improvements in cumulative reward when stabilized by replay buffering and a
target network. In contrast, tabular Q-Learning often converged faster in very
small discrete state partitions but showed oscillatory behaviour and brittle policy
updates when applied to richer, continuous-normalized feature vectors. These
behaviours are consistent with the representational advantages of function approximation and the sample-efficiency trade-offs discussed in Section 4.3.
Effect of reward shaping on learning behaviour.
Reward shaping materially
altered exploration patterns and policy stability:

• Profit-only: produced aggressive directional policies which frequently exploited transient patterns; this led to higher episodic variance and occasional catastrophic drawdowns during volatile regimes.
• Volatility-penalty: encouraged the agent to reduce exposure when local
rolling volatility increased, producing fewer extreme positions and smoother
cumulative returns, but sometimes at the expense of missed high-return
opportunities in strong trends.
• Drawdown-penalty: induced conservative re-entry behaviour after adverse moves; agents learned to treat recent peaks as reference levels and
avoided actions that risked deepening drawdowns, improving worst-case
outcomes but delaying profitable re-engagement.
• Profit–Sharpe Hybrid: balanced short-term profit signal and risk-adjusted
consistency; empirically this produced the best risk-adjusted outcomes
(Figures 1–2) while moderating extreme behaviour seen under profit-only
objectives.
Sample efficiency and computational cost.
DQN demanded substantially more
wall-clock time and environment interactions than tabular Q-Learning to reach its
asymptotic performance. In practical terms, this implies:
• DQN is preferable when sufficient historical data and compute are available and when the state space is high-dimensional.
• Q-Learning may be useful for rapid prototyping or when discretized state/action representations are enforceable and domain dynamics are simple.
Sensitivity, ablation, and failure modes.
We performed targeted ablations to
evaluate robustness:
• Risk parameter sensitivity (λ, β, α): increasing the penalty strength (λ, β)
monotonically reduced volatility and drawdown but eventually reduced mean
cumulative return; the Profit–Sharpe Hybrid exhibited the most favourable
trade-off curve across a wide α range.
• Transaction cost and slippage: modest increases in transaction cost
materially reduced short-term reactive strategies’ edge — strategies that
traded frequently (profit-only DQN) were more sensitive than the risk-aware
configurations.
• State misspecification: removing volatility or RSI from the state degraded
the performance of risk-aware rewards disproportionately, indicating these
state features are critical for the shaped objectives to operate effectively.
• Overfitting risk: when hyperparameter tuning used the full out-of-sample
period improperly, overfitting signatures (large in-sample gains but poor
out-of-sample performance) emerged; walk-forward evaluation and strict
data partitioning mitigated this risk.
5. Discussion
The empirical results show a consistent pattern: reward shaping that includes
explicit risk terms materially improves risk-adjusted performance and robustness

relative to a profit-only objective, with the Profit–Sharpe Hybrid yielding the most
favourable trade-off between return and risk across the examined regimes. These
findings have several practical and scientific implications.
5.1. Why DQN Outperformed Q-Learning
Our results suggested that Deep Q-Networks (DQNs) generally outperformed
tabular Q-Learning across cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. Below we review several factors that may account for this performance
gap, grounding the discussion in reinforcement learning theory and prior empirical studies.
5.1.1. Representational Capacity.
Tabular Q-Learning maintains a discrete state–action table, requiring continuous
features such as returns, exponential moving averages (EMAs), RSI, and volatility
to be partitioned into bins. This discretization either sacrifices granularity or leads
to exponential growth in the state space, reflecting the classic “curse of dimensionality” (Sutton & Barto, 2018). Prior work has shown that such discretization
can obscure important distinctions in financial domains where small variations
(e.g., RSI = 65 vs. 70) can alter optimal decisions (Moody & Saffell, 2001). By
contrast, DQN employs a neural network to approximate the Q-function, enabling
it to capture nonlinear feature interactions and continuous mappings without discretization (Mnih et al., 2015). This facilitates generalization across states and
assets by leveraging shared latent structures such as momentum, mean reversion, and volatility clustering. A potential caveat is that function approximation
may increase the risk of overfitting; however, the breadth of historical market data
available for training helped mitigate this concern in our experiments.
5.1.2. Learning Stability.
Q-Learning updates Q-values directly using bootstrapped targets, which can yield
unstable and oscillatory value estimates in high-dimensional, noisy environments
(Tsitsiklis, 1994). In line with this concern, our experiments showed that tabular
Q-Learning produced negative median returns during volatile market regimes.
DQN integrates stabilizing mechanisms such as experience replay, which reduces
temporal correlations between updates, and target networks, which prevent rapid
propagation of approximation errors (Mnih et al., 2015), (van Hasselt et al., 2016).
These techniques have been widely documented to improve stability, and our
findings were consistent with that body of work.
5.1.3. Interaction with Risk-Aware Rewards.
Reward shaping appeared to amplify the disparity between the two algorithms.
Penalties based on volatility and drawdowns, as well as the Sharpe-hybrid objective, require conditional interpretation of higher-order statistics.
Tabular Q-

Learning could only learn coarse mappings (e.g., “if volatility is high, reduce exposure”), while DQN’s function approximation allowed for more nuanced responses.
For example, in our experiments, DQN was able to reduce exposure selectively
during volatile downtrends while maintaining positions during volatile uptrends,
and cautiously re-enter after drawdowns. These adaptations may explain why
DQN with a Sharpe-hybrid reward achieved both higher returns and lower drawdowns (mean ≈15% in 2020–2021), whereas Q-Learning did not exhibit similar
benefits. It should be noted, however, that these patterns may depend on the
specific reward formulations used, and alternative shaping strategies could yield
different relative outcomes.
5.1.4. Sample Efficiency vs. Domain Complexity.
Q-Learning is generally considered more sample-efficient in small, fully observable environments (Sutton & Barto, 2018). However, financial markets exhibit low
signal-to-noise ratios, partial observability, and non-stationarity (Moody & Saffell,
2001). While DQN is less sample-efficient, the availability of large-scale historical market data provided sufficient training interactions to offset this drawback.
In this setting, the representational capacity of DQN outweighed its higher sample requirements, leading to superior asymptotic performance. Nonetheless, in
domains with limited data, the trade-off may favor simpler algorithms.
5.1.5. Cross-Asset Generalization.
Our asset universe comprised 53 equities and ETFs. Q-Learning often learned
brittle, asset-specific policies, which resulted in high dispersion across instruments. DQN, in contrast, generalized by encoding invariant features across assets, producing higher mean performance and more consistent risk-adjusted outcomes. This generalization property is important in practice, where strategies
must scale across many securities. That said, some asset classes with highly
idiosyncratic dynamics still posed challenges, suggesting limits to cross-asset
transferability.
5.1.6. Theoretical Framing.
Tabular Q-Learning is provably optimal only in finite, fully observable, stationary Markov decision processes (Watkins & Dayan, 1992). Real-world financial
markets violate these assumptions: the state space is continuous, many drivers
are only partially observable, and regimes shift over time. DQN, by combining
function approximation with stabilizing mechanisms, may be better aligned with
these conditions. Extensions such as recurrent DQNs have further highlighted
the potential of deep RL methods to cope with partial observability (Hausknecht
& Stone, 2015). Risk-aware reward shaping also likely enhanced adaptivity, allowing DQN to adjust to volatility and drawdown dynamics.

5.1.7. Synthesis.
Taken together, the evidence suggests that DQN’s advantages stem from its ability to (i) represent nonlinear state–action mappings, (ii) stabilize training under
noisy, autocorrelated data, (iii) exploit risk-aware objectives to learn conditional
strategies, and (iv) generalize across assets and market regimes. Empirically,
this translated into a Sharpe ratio of ≈1.93 with maximum drawdowns near 15%
during 2020–2021, compared to Q-Learning’s lower and less stable outcomes.
While these results support the use of DQN in this context, caution is warranted
in generalizing to other datasets, market conditions, or reward formulations. Future work could explore whether simpler algorithms regain competitiveness under
alternative settings.
5.2. Limitations.
We emphasize several limitations of the current study that constrain the immediate applicability of the results:
1. Execution realism: the experimental environment models simple fixed
transaction costs and does not simulate order-book dynamics, partial fills,
latency, or market impact. Real-world deployment requires incorporating
these microstructural effects.
2. Independence and asset selection: although the 53-asset universe covers a broad set of behaviors, cross-asset correlations and survivorship
biases can affect both SD and mean estimates; future studies should explicitly account for these effects.
5.3. Practical recommendations.
For researchers or practitioners considering RL-based trading systems, we recommend:
• Adopt risk-aware reward formulations (Sharpe-hybrid or volatility/drawdown
penalties) as a default in volatile markets.
• Use DQN or other function-approximation methods for multi-feature state
spaces, but complement them with strong regularization and stabilizing
techniques.
• Implement real-world constraints early (transaction costs, slippage, position limits) and evaluate strategies via walk-forward backtests and stress
scenarios.
• Monitor asset-level dispersion and maintain portfolio-level risk controls (position sizing, stop-losses, volatility targeting) to manage run-to-run and
cross-asset variability.
5.4. Directions for future work.
To strengthen and extend these findings we suggest:
1. Algorithmic extensions: test actor-critic and distributional RL methods
(e.g., PPO, SAC, QR-DQN) and evaluate whether they offer improved sample efficiency or robustness.

2. Online adaptation and meta-learning: incorporate meta-learning or continual learning techniques so agents can adapt quickly to regime shifts.
3. Portfolio-level optimization: move from per-asset single-instrument trading to joint portfolio allocation with explicit correlation and diversification
constraints.
4. Risk measures: experiment with other risk objectives (Sortino ratio, CVaR,
drawdown-aware utility) and multi-objective optimization frameworks.
5. Execution modelling: integrate realistic microstructure simulators (limit
order book models) to quantify the impact of market impact and latency on
strategy performance.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduced and evaluated a set of risk-aware reward functions for reinforcement learning–based trading agents. The principal takeaways are:
• Risk-aware rewards improve robustness: incorporating volatility or drawdown penalties, or using a Profit–Sharpe Hybrid, materially improved riskadjusted outcomes across diverse assets and market regimes.
• DQN outperforms tabular methods in expressive settings: DQN demonstrated superior ability to exploit complex state representations, resulting
in higher cumulative returns and Sharpe ratios.
• Trade-offs must be managed: improved mean performance often came
with greater cross-asset dispersion, underscoring the need for portfoliolevel controls and operational risk management.
• Path to deployment: before practical deployment, strategies must be
stress-tested under realistic execution conditions, and continuous monitoring and re-training procedures should be established.
Overall, the results support the conclusion that carefully designed reward functions that internalize risk.
