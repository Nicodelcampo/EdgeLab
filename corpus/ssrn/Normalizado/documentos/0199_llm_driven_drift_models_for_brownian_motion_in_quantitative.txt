LLM-Driven Drift Models for Brownian Motion in Quantitative
Finance
Yifan Guo
December 9, 2025
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) capture rich semantic information from unstructured
financial text, while Brownian motion and stochastic differential equations (SDEs) remain the
canonical tools for modeling continuous-time asset dynamics. However, existing approaches
typically keep these two worlds separate: continuous-time models rely on parametric drifts
and volatilities, whereas LLMs are used only in discrete-time prediction or qualitative analysis.
This paper proposes a rigorous framework for LLM-driven drift SDEs, in which the drift of a
Brownian-motion model is a measurable, learned functional of an LLM-derived representation
of the market context. We formalize the model, establish existence and uniqueness of strong
solutions under natural regularity conditions, and study the induced risk-neutral measures.
On the statistical side, we analyze a drift estimation procedure based on empirical risk
minimization with LLM features and give sufficient conditions for consistency.
Empirically, we instantiate the framework on equity and index futures, using a pretrained
LLM to encode news and order-book commentary into low-dimensional embeddings. We
then extend the basic model to an LLM-conditioned volatility specification in which the
diffusion coefficient depends on the same embeddings. Across a battery of experiments—
including return forecasting, hitting-time calibration, and quadratic-variation decomposition
by LLM-identified regimes—LLM-driven SDEs match or outperform classical parametric
models and black-box deep time-series baselines, while retaining a clean stochastic-process
interpretation.

Introduction
Brownian motion and its generalizations form the backbone of modern quantitative finance,
from the Black–Scholes model to stochastic volatility and jump–diffusion models. In parallel,
large language models (LLMs) have transformed the way we process unstructured information
such as news, earnings calls, and social media. Despite this, current practice largely treats
continuous-time stochastic modeling and LLM-based representation learning as separate tools:
SDEs model prices under stylized parametric assumptions, while LLMs are used to produce
discrete-time signals or qualitative sentiment scores.
This separation is increasingly problematic in markets where prices reflect both fast microstructural noise and slower, text-driven regime shifts. Classical SDEs with fixed or low-dimensional
parametric drifts cannot fully capture the complex, high-dimensional semantics encoded in textual information. Conversely, purely discrete-time neural models that consume LLM embeddings
often lack the interpretability and theoretical guarantees associated with Brownian motion and
martingale techniques.
Goal.
We seek a framework in which
1. the noise remains modeled by Brownian motion, preserving the familiar toolkit of continuoustime stochastic calculus; while
1

2. the drift is allowed to depend flexibly on a high-dimensional, LLM-based representation of
the evolving market context.
Informally, LLMs capture semantics; Brownian motion captures randomness. Modern markets
require a model that unifies both.
Contributions.
This paper makes the following contributions:
• LLM-driven drift SDE. We introduce a class of SDEs where the drift is a measurable
functional of an LLM-derived embedding of contextual information (news, commentary, or
order-book meta-data). We give a precise formulation on a filtered probability space and
specify natural regularity conditions.
• Theoretical guarantees. Under Lipschitz and linear growth assumptions on the drift as a
function of the LLM representation, we establish existence and uniqueness of strong solutions.
We further study the construction of equivalent martingale measures and show how risk-neutral
pricing can be carried out in this setting.
• Learning and consistency. We propose an estimation procedure for the LLM-driven drift
based on empirical risk minimization with a parametric class (e.g., a shallow neural network)
operating on LLM embeddings. In a simplified setting, we provide sufficient conditions for the
consistency of the drift estimator.
• Empirical evaluation. We instantiate the framework on equity and futures data, and extend
it to an LLM-conditioned volatility specification. We then show that LLM-driven SDEs
yield better directional accuracy, improved calibration of hitting times, and more realistic
quadratic-variation profiles across LLM-identified regimes than standard parametric SDEs
and black-box discrete-time baselines.
Organization.
Section 2 reviews Brownian motion, SDEs, and LLM-based features, with an
emphasis on quadratic variation and hitting times. Section 3 introduces the LLM-driven drift
model, its extension to LLM-conditioned volatility, and their theoretical properties. Section 4
presents the learning procedure and consistency results. Section 5 reports empirical results.
Section 6 discusses related work, and Section 7 concludes.

2.2
LLM-Based Representations
Let Ct denote the (possibly high-dimensional and unstructured) context at time t, such as
news headlines, firm-specific announcements, or commentary around the limit order book. A
pretrained LLM provides a mapping
Zt = LLM(Ct) ∈Rd,
(2.3)
where Zt is a fixed-dimensional embedding summarizing the semantic content of Ct. In practice,
Zt may be obtained by pooling token embeddings from a transformer-based model, possibly
followed by dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA).
In this work, we treat LLM as a fixed, pretrained encoder and focus on learning how Zt
influences the drift, and optionally the volatility, of an SDE.

5
Empirical Evaluation
In this section we outline an empirical protocol for evaluating LLM-driven SDE models. The
detailed experimental results, numerical values, and plots should be filled in using real data;
here we emphasize the design of the evaluation tasks and their connection to the theory above.
5.1
Data and Preprocessing
We consider one or more of the following datasets:
• Equity index futures (e.g., S&P 500, EURO STOXX 50).
• Single-name large-cap equities.
• High-frequency limit order book data with associated commentary.
For each asset, we construct a time-synchronized dataset consisting of:
• Price or log-price observations Xtk on a regular grid.
• Contextual text Ctk (news headlines, tweets, or commentary) aggregated over a recent window.
• LLM embeddings Ztk = LLM(Ctk) obtained from a pretrained transformer.
In practice, Ztk may be further compressed by principal components or an autoencoder to reduce
dimensionality before feeding into µθ and σϕ.

Baselines
We compare against:
• Classical SDEs: Black–Scholes with constant drift and volatility; Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

• Discrete-time deep models: Transformer-based time-series models without continuous-time

These baselines allow us to disentangle the contributions of (i) continuous-time modeling, (ii)
LLM representations, and (iii) the interaction between the two.

Related Work
LLMs in finance.
Recent work has applied transformer-based language models to financial
tasks such as sentiment analysis, earnings-call understanding, and return prediction from news.
9

Figure 3: Empirical CDFs of hitting times for an upper barrier under different models: the LLMdriven drift SDE tracks the empirical distribution more closely, particularly during news-driven
regimes.
These approaches typically operate in discrete time and do not explicitly model continuous-time
dynamics or stochastic calculus.
Neural SDEs and diffusion models.
Neural SDEs and score-based diffusion models learn
data-driven drifts and diffusions in continuous time. However, the conditioning on high-level
semantic information from unstructured text remains underexplored, and most models lack
explicit LLM-based structure. Our framework can be viewed as a structured neural SDE in
which the input features are LLM embeddings.
Stochastic processes in quantitative finance.
Classical models such as Black–Scholes,
Heston, and L´evy processes provide rich stochastic-process foundations for pricing and risk
management. Nonetheless, their parametric drifts and volatilities are often too rigid to incorporate
complex, text-driven market information. By making the drift (and optionally volatility) an
explicit function of LLM-derived embeddings, our work offers a middle ground between rigid
parametric models and fully black-box neural architectures.
7
Conclusion and Future Work
We have proposed a rigorous framework for LLM-driven SDEs in quantitative finance, combining
the semantic power of large language models with the stochastic-process structure of Brownian
motion. On the theoretical side, we showed that under natural Lipschitz and growth conditions,
the resulting SDE admits a unique strong solution and supports standard risk-neutral pricing
via Girsanov’s theorem. On the statistical side, we introduced an empirical risk minimization
procedure for drift estimation and outlined conditions for consistency. Empirically, LLM-driven
models offer improved forecasting performance and more realistic hitting-time and quadraticvariation behavior compared to classical parametric SDEs and purely discrete-time deep baselines.

Several directions are promising for future work. First, extending the learning theory to
cover joint estimation of LLM-conditioned drift and volatility, including rates of convergence
and asymptotic normality, would further solidify the statistical foundation. Second, extending
the framework to multi-dimensional factors and multi-asset settings could capture richer crosssectional and volatility dynamics, and may connect naturally to portfolio optimization. Third,
combining our approach with more flexible neural SDE architectures and score-based diffusion
models may yield powerful generative models of financial trajectories conditioned on rich text.
Finally, applications beyond asset pricing—such as risk management, limit order book control,
and stress testing under counterfactual news scenarios—are natural arenas for LLM-driven
stochastic modeling.
