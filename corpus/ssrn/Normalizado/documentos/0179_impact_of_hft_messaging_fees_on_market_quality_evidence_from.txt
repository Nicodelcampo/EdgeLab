Impact of ASIC’s cost recovery regulation on high frequency 
trading and market quality in Australia 
N. Robert Enemuwe 
Banking & Finance, University of New South Wales 
 
Abstract 
This paper makes use of the natural experiment created by the introduction of ASIC’s cost 
recovery regulation on January 1, 2012, to examine the impact of the regulation on high 
frequency trading (HFT) and market quality in Australia. We employ the order-to-trade ratio 
(OTT) as a proxy for HFT trades and examine the relationship between high OTT trading 
activities and liquidity based market quality metrics. We perform a 2SLS IV fixed effect 
regression to address the endogeneity issue between liquidity measures, volatility and share 
prices. Overall, we find a reduction in OTT, relative quoted spreads and relative effective 
spreads, and an increase in quoted dollar depth after the introduction of the regulation.

1. Introduction 
Over the past several years, the global equity marketplace has experienced significant 
growth in high-speed, low-latency and technologically driven trading activity commonly 
known as high frequency trading (“HFT”). The increase in market competition, market 
fragmentation, globalization of trading and the advancement of trading technologies have all 
contributed to the growth of HFT.  
The increase in HFT is also evidence in Australia where HFT now accounts for 
approximately 27% of total turnover in the lit market (ASIC, 2015a). 
According to the Investment Industry Regulatory Organization of Canada (IIROC, 2011), 
HFT has polarized market participants and most market participants are either supportive of 
the benefits of HFT or critical of its harmful effects. Proponents of HFT say it results in 
enhanced liquidity, reduced spreads and greater market efficiency. Critics of HFT say it 
exacerbates market volatility by withdrawing liquidity when most needed, takes unfair 
advantage of “real” investors, contributes confusion and cost through excessive message 
traffic, and undermines investor confidence. 
Furthermore, the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO, 2011), 
stated that institutional investors and inventory traders are concerned about HFT traders 
preying on their large orders, negatively impacting their transaction prices and trading costs. 
IOSCO added that regulators are concerned with the heightened possibility of spoofing, 
layering, quote stuffing and other potentially manipulative activities, and participants are also 
concerned about the increased messaging rates which leads to high data processing and 
storage cost. 
Regulators around the world have introduced cost recovery models, in part to curb the 
increasing incidence of high frequency trading. The question that arises is – will this make 
markets better, given that regulators are obliged by their respective mandates to ensure that

On January 1, 2012, the Australian Securities and Investment Commission (ASIC) 
imposed cost recovery fees on ASX Limited (ASX) and Chi-X Australia Pty Ltd market 
participants to cover costs relating to its market supervisory functions. The ASIC cost 
recovery regulation is meant to recover technology costs based on the volume of transactions 
and messaging traffic. The cost recovery regulation is similar to the integrated fee model 
implemented by IIROC on April 1, 2012 (See IIROC, 2012).   
Given the high number of quote submissions, corrections and cancellations generated by 
HFT firms, these firms are expected to be greatly impacted by the ASIC’s cost recovery 
regulation.  
Lepone and Sacco (2013) examine the impact of the Canadian integrated fee model on 
market quality measures. They analyze the raw measures of message traffic, trades and OTT 
in order to examine the association between message traffic, trading and liquidity. They 
found that the integrated fee model coincided with a decline in quote submission, trades, 
volume and deterioration of liquidity. A similar study is of relevance in Australia given the 
implementation of ASIC’s cost recovery regulation and the potential impact of the policy on 
high frequency trading and market quality.  
The contribution of this paper is two fold. Firstly, this paper makes use of the natural 
experiment created by the introduction of ASIC’s cost recovery regulation to examine the 
impact of the regulation on high frequency trading typically associated with large number of 
messaging traffic.  Following Lepone and Sacco (2013), we employ the OTT as a proxy for 
HFT activities and examine the relationship between high OTT trading activities and 
liquidity based market quality metrics before and after the introduction of the cost recovery 
regulation.

2. ASIC cost recovery regime 
On January 1, 2012 fees were imposed on ASX Limited and Chi-X Australia Pty Ltd cash 
equity market participants for the performance by ASIC of its market supervision functions 
under Part 7.2A of the Corporations Act 2001 (ASIC, 2015b).  
The cost recovery framework in place between January 1, 2012 and June 30, 2013 was 
developed by Treasury and ASIC in consultation with industry in the second half of 2011. 
Between late 2012 and May 2013 Treasury consulted on amendments to the regime, which 
took effect on July 1, 2013. These arrangements were updated in June 2015 to reflect ASIC's 
2015-16 budgeted expenditure (ASIC, 2015b). 
In line with the Government’s cost recovery principle that costs should be recovered from 
those entities that have created the need for regulation, ASIC’s additional expenditure in 
relation to the cash equities markets (for ASX-listed equity securities) are recovered from 
ASX, Chi-X Australia and their market participants. 
Market participants are charged wholly variable fees based on their share of the total 
number of transactions and messages for securities executed on or reported to the ASX and 
Chi-X cash equities markets by all market participants during the quarter (as recognized by 
ASIC's real-time market surveillance system) (ASIC, 2015b).

3. Data and summary statistics 
The data for the study are sourced from AusEquities and Thomson Reuters Tick History 
(TRTH) via the Securities Industry Research Centre of Asia-Pacific (SIRCA). Our dataset 
consists of both trade data and order book data for the top 200 stocks listed on the ASX and 
Chi-X Australia from November 10, 2011 to March 30, 2012.  
The S&P/ASX 200 list of stocks represents the most liquid securities in Australia and is 
recognized as the investable benchmark for the Australian equity market. The dataset starts 
after the commencement of trading on Chi-X Australia. Chi-X Australia began full trading 
activities in S&P/ASX 200 Securities on November 9, 2011. 
The order book data represents order submissions, corrections, and cancellations at the 
best bid or ask and the data comprises the stock code, bid price, ask price, bid size, ask size, 
transaction date and timestamp in milliseconds. The trade data comprises the trade price, 
trade size, trade direction (i.e. buy/sell), transaction date and timestamp in milliseconds. 
Additional transaction data from the time and sales database include the stocks daily 
turnover, number of shares outstanding and closing prices. We calculate the stock market 
capitalization as the number of shares outstanding multiplied by the stocks closing prices.  
We delete stocks that have been delisted and new additions to the S&P/ASX 200 index 
between November 10, 2011 and March 30, 2012. This is due to re-balancing of the index. 
We were therefore left with a total of 162 of the most liquid stocks listed on the S&P/ASX 
200 index.  
Quotes prior to 10:15am and after 4:00pm are removed from our dataset in order to avoid 
the opening and closing auction process. We further eliminate all anomalous data possibly 
caused by human and system errors, such as negative spreads and we winsorize each of the 
variables at the 1% and 99% in order to remove outliers.

4. Methodology 
4.1. OTT and messaging traffic measures 
Following Lepone and Sacco (2013), we calculate the OTT as a proxy for the level of 
automated trading taking place across stocks and days. This proxy is commonly used by 
market participants and in academic studies such as in Hendershott et al. (2011), Tabb et al. 
(2009) and ASIC (2013). The daily OTT is calculated as the raw electronic message traffic 
(quote submissions, corrections and cancellations) divided by the number of trades in a day 
for each stock.

Based on the documented association between high OTT and liquidity provision (See 
Hendershott et al. (2011); Lepone and Sacco (2013)), the liquidity measures (Lit), i.e. relative

stock volatility is calculated using the realized volatility and is defined as the sum of squared 
intraday returns for the day.  The market cap for each stock i on day t is calculated as the 
number of shares outstanding multiplied by the stock closing price. 
An obvious concern is that the Lit spread measures, volatility and share price may be 
endogenous. It is easy to imagine HFT activity affecting both contemporaneous spreads and 
price volatility (Benos and Sagade, 2012). For this reason, we instrument these variables 
using their lagged values. These variables are strongly autoregressive rendering their lagged 
values good predictors of their current values. According to (Benos and Sagade (2012), we 
therefore estimate equation (8) via a two-stage least squares (2SLS) fixed effect regression 
for the period before and after the policy enactment.

6. Conclusion 
On January 1, 2012, ASIC imposed cost recovery fees on ASX Limited and Chi-X 
Australia market participants to cover the costs relating to it’s market supervisory functions. 
The cost recovery regime is meant to recover technology costs based on the volume of 
transactions and messaging traffic.  
This paper makes use of the natural experiment created by the introduction of ASIC’s 
cost recovery regulation to examine the impact of the regulation on market quality for HFT 
trading on the ASX and Chi-X Australia.   
This study employs the OTT as a proxy for HFT trades in order to examine the 
relationship between high OTT trading strategies and market quality metrics before and after 
the policy enactment.  
We also perform a 2SLS instrument variable fixed effect panel data regression to address 
the endogeneity issue between liquidity measures, volatility and share price. 
Overall, this study finds a 6.1% reduction in OTT, a 2.3 basis point reduction in relative 
quoted spread, a 0.4 basis point reduction in relative effective spread, and a 17.65% increase 
in quoted dollar depth after the introduction of ASIC’s cost recovery regulation. The results 
are all statistically significant.  
We also examine the documented association between OTT and liquidity measures, and 
finds a positive association between OTT and relative quoted spread, a negative association 
between OTT and relative effective spread and a positive association between OTT and 
quoted dollar depth before and after the policy enactment.  
The impact of high OTT trading activities on relative quoted spread and relative effective 
spread remains relatively the same after the policy implementation, but the impact on quoted 
dollar depth increased after the policy enactment.
