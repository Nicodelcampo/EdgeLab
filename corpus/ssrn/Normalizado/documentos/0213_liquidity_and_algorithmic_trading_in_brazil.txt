Liquidity and Algorithmic Trading in Brazil
Henrique Pinto Ramos1,, Marcelo S. Perlin1
Abstract
This paper provides evidence of the eﬀect of algorithmic trading (AT) in the liquidity
of the Brazilian equity market. A wide debate on the literature asserts that AT may be
both beneﬁcial and harmful to market quality. The results of our econometric estimates for
a sample of 47 stocks through mid-2017 to mid-2018 show that when a larger horizon is
analyzed (20 minutes), the lagged eﬀect of AT reduces market quality. However, the lagged
eﬀect of one-minute AT activity reduces both spreads and price impact. Still, the eﬀect of
AT on market quality is sensitive to the horizon which is being analyzed.
JEL: G11, C58
Keywords:
Liquidity, Algorithmic trading, Spreads, Market microstructure.
1. Introduction
Liquidity is a fundamental variable because the entire notion of a ﬁnancial market is that
it is a place (physical or virtual) where agents can easily trade ﬁnancial assets. Negotiating
illiquid assets can increase both explicit and implicit transaction costs, therefore reducing
proﬁts. However, this variable is safely assumed to be systematically increasing in capital
markets. Financial integration between diﬀerent countries, new regulations and technology
improvements have made access to ﬁnancial markets easier (Evans and Hnatkovska, 2014).
One channel through liquidity is increasing is the advent of algorithmic trading (AT) and
high-frequency traders (HFT). These traders can easily detect order imbalances or transitory
arbitrage opportunities, therefore acting as voluntary market makers (Holden et al., 2014).
As O’Hara (2015) describes, “markets are diﬀerent now, transformed by technology and
high-frequency trading”. The image of a broker sending orders in behalf of his clients has
been replaced by computers matching orders in frequencies not distinguishable by human
eyes. Regarding this, Hendershott et al. (2011) provide ﬁrst evidence of AT/HFT increasing
liquidity in the transaction costs and depth dimensions in the US market. Also, other studies
show AT/HFT increasing market quality (Benos and Sagade, 2016; Brogaard et al., 2014).
Although access to information and computing power has become facilitated, the overall
impact of AT/HFT is still uncertain. On one hand, AT/HFT has been related to facilitating
price discovery (Benos and Sagade, 2016; Brogaard et al., 2014) and increasing liquidity

(Hendershott et al., 2011; Hendershott and Riordan, 2013). On the other hand, models
where HFT may potentially harm other investors are present in the literature (Budish et al.,
2015). Biais et al. (2015) and Foucault et al. (2017) provide theory that fast traders may
cause negative externalities originated from adverse selection costs to non-HFT traders1.
Cartea et al. (2019) provide evidence that ultra-fast traders reduce intraday market
quality. Since high frequency traders can receive information and learn about order ﬂow
incredibly fast, these agents may act as informed traders in the spirit of Glosten and Milgrom
(1985).
Empirically, Hirschey (2018) provides evidence of anticipatory trading of HFT,
potentially increasing non-HFT trading costs. Thus, informed traders should dry liquidity
from the market, since they are expected to trade aggressively in order to anticipate price
movements.
However, HFT are also documented to act as voluntary market makers by
placing limit orders and hence, adding liquidity (Hendershott et al., 2011). In short, fast
traders may act both as demanding and as supplying liquidity, which could be respectively
negative and positive to the market. One diﬃculty lies in the fact that even in detailed
databases, there is no way to ascertain which trading strategy a HFT is engaging to.
Most of the previous research in the subject is centered in North America and Europe,
where detailed data are available. Studies regarding AT/HFT in emerging markets have
been partially made only by Boehmer et al. (2018), who investigate the eﬀects of co-location
on market liquidity of 42 trading venues. Although ﬁnancial volume in emerging markets
is much lower than in developed economies, returns can be signiﬁcantly high and liquidity
relatively low (Lesmond, 2005). Additionally, emerging markets may present more price
ineﬃciencies (Hull and McGroarty, 2014) and may be more dependent from foreign capital
ﬂows. This may draw attention of HFT searching for transitory arbitrage opportunities.
On the other hand, many of the studies presented before allege that the proﬁtability of
HFT comes from market fragmentation, since HFT should engage in low-latency arbitrage
strategies by taking advantage of spread diﬀerences between trading venues2. This is not
present in the Brazilian market, so one could think whether HFT are present in this market
and in what extent they contribute for liquidity.
The present research provides ﬁrst evidence of the eﬀect of algorithmic trading (AT)
activity in the Brazilian equities market. Using one year of data through mid-2017 to mid2018, we show through VAR estimates that the eﬀect of 20 minutes of AT activity increases
realized spreads and the high-frequency Amihud (2002) measure. Our panel data approach
using 2SLS yields similar results. Although the debate on the eﬀect of AT on liquidity is
still at large, the evidence provided in this study supports the idea of AT reducing market
quality in a longer horizon (20 minutes). However, in the short run (1 minute) our results
support the idea of AT activity increasing market quality.
The Brazilian case is an interesting market to be studied since there is only one trading
venue for equities. Additionally, research on emerging markets may be hampered by the lack

2. Data and methodology
Until 2008 the Brazilian ﬁnancial market was concentrated in two main exchanges:
Bovespa (S˜ao Paulo Stock Exchange), which traded mainly equities, and BM&F (Brazilian
Mercantile and Futures Exchange), which negotiated commodities, futures and derivatives.
In 2008, Bovespa and BM&F merged on BM&FBovespa. Later, on 2017, BM&FBovespa
merged with the clearing house CETIP, forming the B3 company, one of the largest exchanges
in Latin America. According to B3’s website, at March 2019 there were 430 companies listed
on the stock market. Before the merge with CETIP, BM&FBovespa’s 2016 Annual Report
shows an average daily trading value of R$ 7.4 bn on equities and equities derivatives with
an average market capitalization of R$ 2.2 tn3. Most of the daily traded volume in this
market comes from two types of investors: foreign (52%) and local institutional (24%). A
small share of the market is held by retail investors (17%) and the remaining percentage
refers to private companies and ﬁnancial institutions (BM&FBovespa, 2016).
The B3 exchange provides open access to trade and order data.
For each day and
market (equities, odd lot equities, options and futures), there are two main categories of
ﬁles hosted at the B3 FTP: the ﬁrst category reports all trades within a speciﬁc market
at the nanosecond time stamp4. The second category of ﬁles provides the limit order book
(LOB) for the given day for a given side of the LOB. For example, there is one ﬁle for
the buy-side order book and one ﬁle for the sell-side order book. These ﬁles report order
changes, submissions, cancels and trades. Every order is reported within an unique code for
identiﬁcation. The ﬁles are downloaded and organized through the GetHFData package of
Perlin and Ramos (2017).
Even with increasing computer power and tools to handle high-frequency data, recent
related studies use samples with small time frames in research about HFT activity5. Our
sample spans one year of trading data from 2017-07-24 to 2018-07-30 (250 trading sessions)
for 47 stocks traded at the B3 exchange. A stock is included in the estimations if:

3. Results
Table 3 reports the VAR estimates of equations 4 and 5 using RealizedSpread as the
liquidity variable.
The table describes the mean sum of coeﬃcients, the mean ﬁrst lag
coeﬃcient and the mean p-value for the Granger Causality test across the 47 stocks. Columns
indicate the dependent variable of the VAR system. The ﬁrst rows of the table evidence the
autoregressive behavior of RealizedSpread at the ﬁrst column. The sum of 20 coeﬃcients
has an average of 0.73 across stocks, while the coeﬃcient for the ﬁrst lag has a mean of 0.302,
evidencing that liquidity is persistent (Acharya and Pedersen, 2005).
The eﬀect of lagged AT on RealizedSpread is positive for 20 lags (sum of coeﬃcients 0.053) and signiﬁcant at the 1% level. Therefore, an increase in AT activity raise
RealizedSpreads up to 20 minutes. This may be related to aggressive trading strategies
conducted by agents using algorithms. Mean Granger causality tests shows that lagged AT
contains information on the prediction of RealizedSpreads. However, the mean coeﬃcient
on the ﬁrst lag eﬀect of AT on RealizedSpreads is negative (−0.027). This suggests that
AT activity at the past one minute can reduce spreads but overall AT activity (in the past
20 minutes) increase RealizedSpreads.
The second column of Table 3 reports the results of equation 5, in which the dependent variable is AT. The mean sum of coeﬃcients of RealizedSpread is positive (0.030),
with mean Granger causality p-value smaller than the usual 0.01 value. As many studies
concerns about the endogeneity between these variables (Hendershott et al., 2011; Mestel
et al., 2018), we conﬁrm this in the Brazilian market. The eﬀect of mean ﬁrst lag coeﬃcient
of RealizedSpreads on AT is negative (−0.069), suggesting that when spreads are low, AT
increase in the forward minute. The last rows of the second column of Table 3 reports the
eﬀect of lagged AT on current values. Both the mean sum of coeﬃcients and the mean ﬁrst
lag coeﬃcient are positive (0.770 and 0.227, respectively).
Table 5 reports the VAR estimates of equations 4 and 5 using Amihud as the liquidity
variable. The ﬁrst column and the ﬁrst rows evidence the autoregressive behavior of Amihud,
with the mean sum of coeﬃcients of 0.841 for 20 lags and mean coeﬃcient of 0.256 for the
ﬁrst lag. The mean sum of coeﬃcients on the eﬀect of lagged AT on Amihud is positive
(0.037), presenting a mean Granger-causality p-value lower than the usual 0.01 cutoﬀ. This
provides evidence that algorithmic trading increases price impact over the period of 20
minutes. Similar to the results of Table 3, the mean coeﬃcient on eﬀect of the ﬁrst lag of
AT on Amihud is negative. This suggests that the short-term (1 minute) eﬀect of AT is
negative, therefore AT activity contributes to reduce price impact in the 1-minute horizon.

4. Concluding remarks
The present research provides ﬁrst evidence of the eﬀect of algorithmic trading (AT)
activity in the Brazilian equities market. Using one year of data through mid-2017 to mid10
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3413130

2018, we show through VAR estimates that the eﬀect of 20 minutes of AT activity increases
realized spreads and the high-frequency Amihud (2002) measure. Our panel data approach
using 2SLS yields similar results. Although the debate on the eﬀect of AT on liquidity is
still at large, the evidence provided in this study supports the idea of AT reducing market
quality in a longer horizon (20 minutes). However, in the short run (1 minute) our results
support the idea of AT activity increasing market quality.
Further research may explore whether diﬀerent brokerage ﬁrms aﬀect diﬀerently market
quality. In this sense, brokerage ﬁrms who oﬀer co-location services to its clients may present
higher impact on market quality.
