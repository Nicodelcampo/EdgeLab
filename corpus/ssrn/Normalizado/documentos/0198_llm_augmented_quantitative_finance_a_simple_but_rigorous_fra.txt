LLM-Augmented Quantitative Finance:
A Simple but Rigorous Framework for Market Understanding and
Forecasting
Yifan Guo∗
November 29, 2025
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate strong abilities in pattern extraction, semantic
understanding, and cross-modal reasoning. However, their integration into quantitative finance
remains largely exploratory. This paper proposes a simple, modular, and mathematically explicit framework that combines LLM-based market reasoning with traditional quantitative models. We formalize an LLM feature pipeline, define a hybrid predictor, and prove several basic
properties: (i) in-sample risk reduction when augmenting linear models with LLM features, (ii)
conditions under which LLM sentiment is incrementally informative in the sense of conditional
expectation, and (iii) a simple mean–variance interpretation of LLM-based regime features. We
also provide placeholders for empirical figures (to be generated in Python) and discuss practical
considerations for deploying LLMs in trading and risk-management pipelines.
1
Introduction
Quantitative finance has historically relied on numerical signals: returns, volatility, order-flow
imbalance, liquidity indicators, and macroeconomic factors. The rapid progress of Large Language
Models (LLMs) introduces a powerful new component—semantic signals derived from unstructured
text such as financial news, earnings call transcripts, regulatory filings, and social media streams.
Despite the success of LLMs in natural language processing, a simple and transparent framework
for combining LLM outputs with standard quant models is still under development. Practitioners
often face questions such as:
• How should LLM-derived sentiment or textual embeddings enter a forecasting model?
• Under what conditions can LLM signals be provably informative?
• How can we interpret the interaction between semantic and numerical features?
In this paper, we propose a minimal but extensible framework where LLMs act as a textual
feature engine feeding downstream predictive models. We emphasize three aspects:
1. a clean mathematical setup;
2. simple theoretical properties with short proofs; and
3. an empirical blueprint with explicit figure/file names (for later implementation in Python).

1.1
Contributions
This paper makes three simple but rigorous contributions:
1. We formalize an LLM-based sentiment and embedding pipeline that converts raw text into
structured signals suitable for quantitative models.
2. We propose and analyze a hybrid forecasting model that combines LLM features with traditional
numerical predictors. We prove that, in linear regression, adding LLM features cannot worsen
in-sample fit and give conditions for genuine incremental predictive power.
3. We interpret LLM-based regime features in a mean–variance setting, showing how textual
regimes can induce regime-specific optimal portfolios.

Proof. This is the standard Hilbert-space projection property of conditional expectations. The

Proposition 1 gives a clean theoretical justification: if LLM sentiment captures information
about Y that is not already in X, then the optimal predictor using (X, S) has strictly smaller mean
squared error than the optimal predictor using X alone.

6
Discussion
6.1
Interpretability
Treating LLM outputs as features rather than direct predictors has interpretability benefits. We
can examine:
• The empirical correlation between St and yt+1;
• Which regimes Ct tend to precede large drawdowns or rallies;
• Representative documents associated with extreme St or specific regimes.
These analyses can be illustrated by further figures, for example:
• Scatter plot of St vs. yt+1 (filename: sentiment return scatter.png);
• Bar plot of average returns by regime (filename: regime return bar.png).
6.2
Robustness and Overfitting
Text data is noisy and non-stationary. To mitigate overfitting:
• Use regularization (ridge, LASSO) or simple models;
• Reduce embedding dimensionality via PCA before forecasting;
• Use rolling or expanding windows and realistic train/test splits.
6.3
Practical Considerations
In real deployments, additional issues arise:
• Latency: LLM inference may be slow; pre-computation and caching of embeddings and sentiment scores are useful.
• Costs: Commercial LLM APIs can be expensive at scale, motivating smaller, fine-tuned models.
• Data alignment: Timestamps must be handled carefully to avoid look-ahead bias and to match
text to the correct trading day.
• Regulation: The use of alternative data and AI-based decision systems can be subject to
regulatory requirements.
7
Conclusion
We presented a simple but rigorous framework for integrating LLMs into quantitative finance workflows. LLMs function as semantic feature generators, producing sentiment, embeddings, and textual
regimes that can be merged with standard numerical factors. We provided a clean mathematical
setup, basic theoretical guarantees for linear models, and a mean–variance interpretation of regimes.
Although the framework is intentionally minimal, it can be extended in many directions:
cross-sectional forecasting across many assets, intraday applications using high-frequency news,
or reinforcement-learning-based policies that incorporate LLM features. Our main message is that
LLM + quant does not need to begin with a complex end-to-end model; a transparent feature-based
approach already provides a solid foundation.
