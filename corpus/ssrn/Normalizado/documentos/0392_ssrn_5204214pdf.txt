Abstract
21
Forest canopy gaps affect the timing and amount of snowmelt, because canopies act as key controls 
22
over snowpack dynamics. Overlying canopy can decrease snowpack by intercepting snowfall, but it 
23
can also reduce ablation rates via shading. Changes in forest structure and canopy gaps may 
24
therefore affect the amount, timing, and duration of snowmelt and potentially forest response to 
25
water limitations. We test how the higher energy-input edges of gaps (‘warm edges’) differ from the 
26
lower energy-input edges of gaps (‘cool edges’) with respect to snow depth, snowmelt timing, and 
27
tree growth in a snow-dominated forest in the Western US. We use multiple dates of LiDAR-based 
28
measurement to assess springtime snow depths in warm and cool gap edges in Sagehen Creek 
29
Basin, CA. Then we use paired tree sampling and ring width chronologies to ascertain moisture 
30
sensitivity of trees adjacent to warm and cool edges. Pre-ablation snow depths in cool gap edges 
31
exceeded those in warm gap edges by 9% to 18%, with effect size depending on elevation and 
32
aspect. Snowpack also persisted longer in cool edges than in warm edges. Growth variations in 
33
warm-edge-adjacent trees were more correlated with interannual variations in snow depth those of 
34
cool edge trees, although neither had strong correlations. These findings suggest that forest 
35
structures that maximize cool edge area may benefit snow depth and persistence, and may lead to 
36
cool-edge trees being less sensitive to interannual hydroclimatic variability than warm edge trees, 
37
despite this effect being small relative to other controls over growth.  
38
Key Words
39
Forest-snow interactions, canopy gaps, remote sensing, tree rings

1. Introduction
47
Forest canopies are primary controls on snowpack mass and energy dynamics (Varhola et al. 2010), 
48
and consequently affecting the timing and magnitude of soil water recharge and stream discharge 
49
(Barnett et al. 2005; Goeking and Tarboton 2020). Understanding those controls is important 
50
because forests, mountainous terrain, and snow-dominated climates are often collocated in the 
51
Western US and those regions tend to disproportionately supply the region’s drinking water (Li et 
52
al. 2017; Liu et al. 2021). In those regions, snowmelt is also a key water resource to trees (Trujillo et 
53
al. 2012; Tague and Peng 2013; Knowles et al. 2018; Martin et al. 2018). Much research has focused 
54
on understanding how forest disturbances – natural, or induced for management objectives – and 
55
their effects on stand and canopy structure can directly alter water use by forests (Bréda et al. 
56
1995). However, indirect effects of forest disturbances can also influence snowpack dynamics and 
57
thus also the provisioning of water to trees and streams (Biederman et al. 2014; Frank et al. 2019; 
58
Goeking and Tarboton 2020; Knighton et al. 2020). Small-scale forest-disturbance effects on 
59
snowpack dynamics are particularly underexplored, despite management activities often 
60
disturbing canopies at 1-to-10-m2 scales, such as occurs with creating canopy gaps. This study 
61
investigates the effects of canopy gaps on micro-scale snowpack variations to better understand 
62
controls over microenvironments and water resources in forests.  
63
Canopy structures are changing in the Western US due to forest disturbances (Weed et al. 2013; 
64
Allen et al. 2015; Westerling 2016; Young et al. 2017a) and silvicultural treatments (Churchill et al. 
65
2013; Prichard et al. 2021; Stephens et al. 2021). Anthropogenetic impacts, especially warming 
66
temperatures and aridification, are driving larger and more severe natural disturbances including 
67
fire (Pechony and Shindell 2010; Westerling 2016), insect outbreaks (Weed et al. 2013; Hart et al. 
68
2014; Kolb et al. 2016), and drought mortality (Allen et al. 2015; Millar and Stephenson 2015; 
69
Young et al. 2017b). Severe disturbances often replace stands, especially when forests are dense 
70
and homogeneous due to historical forest management practices (Hagmann et al. 2021; Restaino et 
71
al. 2019; Young et al. 2017). However, moderate disturbances can also create patchy mosaics with 
72
gaps, which can enhance resilience by facilitating increased species and age diversity (Lydersen et 
73
al. 2013; Curtis et al. 2017; Stephens et al. 2021). Gap creation is also a management tool to 
74
artificially gain those desired effects and others, such as fire-risk mitigation (Prichard et al. 2021) 
75
and wildlife habitat (North et al. 2009; Przepióra et al. 2020). The effects of forest gaps are often 
76
discussed in terms of dichotomous conditions represented by ‘in-gap’ versus ‘under-canopy’ 
77
positions (Muscolo et al. 2014). However, within-gap heterogeneity is also noteworthy because

80
The effects of gaps and heterogeneity within gaps on solar radiation can significantly affect 
81
snowpack dynamics. Cool gap edges (i.e., north facing in the northern hemisphere) are more shaded 
82
and receive less longwave radiation from adjacent trees than warm gap edges (i.e., south facing in 
83
the northern hemisphere; (Lawler and Link 2011; Musselman et al. 2015; Musselman and Pomeroy 
84
2017), leading to cool gap edges showing slower snowpack ablation (the combination of melt and 
85
sublimation) (Currier and Lundquist 2018; Lawler and Link 2011; Mazzotti et al. 2020; Moeser et 
86
al. 2020). Snow scour and deposition by wind can also create substantial differences in snow depth 
87
between leeward and windward gap edges, though wind-related snow depth heterogeneity can 
88
occur on scales that range from 0.5 to 12 times the length of North-South radiation effects (Brandle 
89
and Finch 1991; Hiemstra et al. 2006; Currier and Lundquist 2018). Recent observational and 
90
modeling studies suggest the potential for consistent spatial patterns in snowpack across forest 
91
gaps in temperate conifer forests (Currier and Lundquist 2018; Moeser et al. 2020). Despite this 
92
progress in process-level understanding of gap effects on snow, the high spatial resolution required 
93
to measure within-gap variations in snow depth (Broxton et al. 2021) typically involves sacrificing 
94
temporal resolution (Harpold et al. 2014), and thus the temporal evolution of within-gap snow 
95
heterogeneity is not commonly systematically studied or censused across whole watersheds. 
96
Given the potential influences of gaps position on snow depths, we posit that those depth 
97
differences would likely involve longer persistence of snow on cool edges and thus snowmelt 
98
recharging soil moisture later into the growing season in cool edges. Because snowmelt is a primary 
99
source of water in these forests, shallower snow and earlier snowmelt can imply decreased 
100
moisture availability to trees in mid- and late- growing season (Harpold, 2016). That reduced 
101
availability can translate to water limitations and reduced functional capacity of  trees (Cooper et al. 
102
2020), which in unsurprising because tree productivity being water-limited is common throughout 
103
the western US (Restaino et al. 2016). The prevalence of water scarcity limiting tree growth is 
104
especially likely in the Central Sierra Nevada where little rain falls in summer and trees presumably 
105
(Bales et al. 2011) rely on storages that recharge from spring snowmelt. Thus, deeper and late106
melting snow on cool gap edges is expected to create more favorable growing conditions and less 
107
drought sensitivity compared to the micro-environment experienced by trees on warm edges. 
108
Tree rings reflecting inter-annual growth variations are a tool to test sensitivity to variations in 
109
moisture inputs. Dendroclimatology largely relies on moisture scarcity limiting growth, yielding

113

114

116

117

118
widths to be more sensitive to variations in climate than did those that survived and that 
119
vulnerability to beetles was associated with drought stress (Csank et al. 2016). This prior research 
120
suggests that warm-edge trees may show stronger correlations between ring widths and drought 
121
proxies (such as PDSI or snow depths) than do trees adjacent to cool edges if snow persists longer 
122
in cool edges and thereby mitigates water deficits experienced by trees for more of the growing 
123
season.  
124
This study quantifies the effect of gap edges on snow depth during the ablation season and explores 
125
how this effect may feedback into tree growth, in a small, snow dominated watershed in the Central 
126
Sierra Nevada. We use seasonal and annual tree ring widths and unique, high resolution, 
127
multitemporal snow observations to test the following hypotheses:
128
1.
Snow is consistently deeper on cool edges than warm edges across varied elevation and 
129
aspect. 
130
2.
Snow depth differences across gaps evolve over the ablation season with timing that is 
131
dependent on elevation and aspect.
132
3.
Trees on cool gap edges are less sensitive to annual variations in moisture availability than 
133
warm edge trees.

2.1 Study Site
136
Our study domain is an approximately 71 km2 area centered on Sagehen Creek Basin (SCB), a small 
137
headwaters catchment in the Central Sierra Nevada mountains, about 12 km north of Truckee, CA. 
138
Elevations in the study domain range from 1900 to 2710 meters above sea level (masl), and the 
139
climate is characterized by cool, snowy winters and warm, dry summers. The study domain 
140
includes two Snow Telemetry (SNOTEL) sites: site 539 at 2127 masl, and site 541 at 2541 masl.

2.2 Characterizing Forest Structure
165
We characterized forest structure metrics and features using LiDAR data collected by the National 
166
Center for Airborne Laser Mapping (NCALM) in 2014 (Guo 2014). LiDAR is an active remote 
167
sensing technique in which light pulses are emitted from an aircraft. Return time and intensity of 
168
each pulse measures distance to the surface of the ground, canopy, snow, or other object between 
169
the aircraft and the ground. Canopy height was determined at 1 meter (1-m) resolution for the 
170
study domain using LiDAR-derived models of ground elevation and upper canopy surface (Piske et 
171
al. 2022).

Our analysis delimits the study domain into areas of canopy and gaps at a 1-m resolution. Pixels 
173
containing canopy heights ≥3 m above the ground are classified as canopy, and all other pixels are 
174
classified as gaps, excluding very small canopy openings of <9 m2 total area and a 1-m buffer 
175
around canopy areas to minimize uncertainty associated with low canopy fringe density. We use a 3 
176
m minimum canopy height because shorter vegetation is often buried by snow in winter and other 
177
work has used this cutoff to effectively classify the effects of canopy on snow in SCB (Piske et al. 
178
2022). In addition, non-canopy pixels within 2 m of the canopy (including gap and buffer pixels) 
179
were classified as “fringe” pixels, which represent outlying branches and lower portions of tree 
180
canopies that most directly influence snow energy balance through longwave emission (Musselman 
181
and Pomeroy 2017).
182
Within gaps, we define edges as areas within De meters from the canopy edge, as shown in Eq 1.

De = htm / tan (Angs)

Where htm is a local mean (moving 10 m window) tree height and Angs is the daytime mean sun 
185
elevation on the vernal equinox. We apply a rayshading function (Morgan-Wall 2021) to a digital 
186
surface model (DSM, includes terrain and canopy) of surface heights in the study domain in hourly 
187
timesteps for the date of the vernal equinox. For each timestep, rayshading returns the intensity of 
188
sunlight “proportional to the dot product of the [sun] direction and the surface normal at that 
189
point”, and does not account for diffuse shortwave radiation (Morgan-Wall 2021). Sunlight 
190
intensities are then averaged across all daytime timesteps. The daily mean intensity of sunlight for a 
191
given 1-m pixel on the equinox is defined by the value Lumf. To define portions of edges that are 
192
generally sunny or shady throughout the day, we create a cutoff value for Lumf defined as Lumct = 
193
0.35. We determined the value of Lumct such that the exterior boundaries of gaps in flat areas were 
194
approximately equally classified as sunny or shady. Critically, the optimal value of Lumct will vary 
195
strongly by location, particularly by latitude. Additionally, if these methods are used to explore gap 
196
edge effects on snow for locations with very late or early snowmelt, it may be more effective to use 
197
an earlier or later date to establish Angs and Lumf.

2.3 Classifying Terrain 
210
To assess how the gap effects vary across the diverse terrain of the study domain, tree and snow 
211
data are grouped into terrain types. We use Slope-Northness Index (SNI = cos(aspect) * slope) as a 
212
simple representation of the effects of topography on energy loading, reflecting the degree to which 
213
something is steep and north facing, ranging from -1 (vertical south facing) to 1 (vertical north 
214
facing). The SNI for north facing and south facing 45-degree slopes are 0.785 and -0.785 
215
respectively, and flat surfaces and west or east facing slopes have an SNI of 0. SNI and elevation 
216
variables were derived from a 1-m digital elevation model (DEM) from 2014 snow-off LiDAR data 
217
(Guo 2014; Hijmans et al. 2023). 1-m resolution SNI and elevation were aggregated to 100-m 
218
resolution (100-m by 100-m grid cells), and 100-m data were scaled and centered before being 
219
divided into four categories using a K means clustering algorithm. The number of clusters (4) was 
220
chosen using the common “elbow method”, which resulted in clusters that generally represent high 
221
elevation north facing (high NF), high elevation south facing (high SF), low elevation north facing 
222
(low NF), and low elevation south facing (low SF) terrain types. Other numbers of clusters were 
223
also tested but provided less common-sense organization of terrain types.  
224
2.4 Measuring Snow Depth
225
Snow depth for the study domain was derived from LiDAR data for four dates spanning 2008-2016. 
226
Snow surface elevations from snow-on LiDAR flights (occurring on February 10th 2008, March 26th 
227
2016, April 17th 2016, and May 18th 2016) were differenced from ground elevations from snow-free 
228
LiDAR flights (occurring in summer 2014) to determine the height of the snow surface above the 
229
ground. Data from flights in 2008 and 2014 were collected by NCALM (Dozier and Painter 2004; 
230
Huntington 2008; Guo 2014), and 2016 flight data were collected by the Airborne Snow 
231
Observatory. For each flight, point clouds were vertically cross referenced and filtered for 
232
vegetation interference (see Piske et al. 2022 for details on LiDAR data processing).

2.4 Tree Ring Sampling and Measurement
240
Tree ring data were obtained using a paired tree sampling design, with each pair containing one 
241
tree adjacent to a cool gap edge and one tree adjacent to a warm gap edge. Paired trees were within 
242
30-110 m of each other, were the same species, had similar trunk diameter at breast height (no 
243
greater than 20% difference in DBH), and had similar levels of competition. Competition was 
244
categorically estimated (low, medium, and high) based on the cumulative DBH of living tree trunks 
245
within 7 m of the sampled tree’s trunk. Cores were collected from 37 pairs of trees including 9 pairs 
246
of lodgepole pines, 9 pairs of Jeffrey pines, and 19 pairs of fir trees (mixed red and white firs).
247
Whenever possible, cores were collected from the gap-facing side of each tree, resulting in coring 
248
aspects ranging from NE-NW for cool side trees and SE-SW for warm side trees. Cores were 
249
collected using a 5 mm diameter increment borer, then mounted and sanded until cell walls were 
250
clearly visible. The width of whole rings (WR) and annual earlywood (EW) and latewood (LW) were 
251
measured using MeasureJ2X software (Voortech Consulting). Rings were cross dated visually 
252
(Stokes and Smiley 1968), then confirmed using the dplR package for R (Bunn 2008). For each core, 
253
WR, EW, and LW width were detrended and normalized to width indices using “Friedman’s Super 
254
Smoother”, a high frequency variable span smoother (Friedman 1984) in the dplR package to 
255
eliminate the biological growth signal in ring width and highlight high frequency (annual) variation 
256
in moisture availability (Fritts 2001; Bunn 2008).
257
We measure correlation between ring width indices and climate variables for the years 2000 – 
258
2020, because the presence of gaps cannot be confirmed for earlier years. The existence of gaps 
259
selected for tree sampling are confirmed using 2007 LiDAR data and 1998 – 1999 satellite imagery. 
260
Additionally, 2020 – 2022 were dry years with some drought mortality in SCB. Several of our 
261
sampled trees showed signs of growth restriction in 2021 followed by zero growth or death in 
262
2022, so rings from 2021 and 2022 are not used for any trees in our analysis.

2.6 Moisture Availability Data
264
Annual variation in moisture availability is represented in our analysis by two primary variables, 
265
snow water equivalent on April 1st (1 Apr SWE) and climatic water deficit (CWD), and two 
266
secondary variables, annual precipitation (P) and annual mean of daily maximum temperatures 
267
(Tmax). 1 Apr SWE is an operationally important value for water management (Lynn et al. 2020). It 
268
is generally representative of annual peak SWE (Musselman et al. 2021) and a useful proxy for 
269
gauging the quantity and timing of moisture influx from snowmelt (Harpold et al. 2017; Mote et al. 
270
2018; Siirila-Woodburn et al. 2021). P and Tmax are less specific indicators of moisture availability, 
271
but are regularly used and measured variables that provide important climate context. Annual P, 
272
Tmax, and 1 Apr SWE data are from the Independence Camp snow telemetry site (SNOTEL 539), 
273
which sits in the northern portion of our study area at 2128 masl, between the mean (2163 masl) 
274
and median (2010 masl) elevations of sampled trees. 
275
CWD is calculated as potential evapotranspiration minus actual evapotranspiration, so greater 
276
values of CWD indicate lower moisture availability relative to evaporative demand. It is particularly 
277
effective for predicting forest moisture stress and mortality related to water limitation (Bigler et al. 
278
2007; Lutz et al. 2010; Restaino et al. 2016). CWD data were sampled at the location of SNOTEL 539 
279
from the gridded dataset TerraClimate (Abatzoglou et al. 2018). Monthly CWD values are summed 
280
for each water year. For the location and years observed, CWD is always zero November – April, so 
281
CWD effectively captures moisture availability during the summer growing season, while 1 Apr 
282
SWE represents moisture supplied by winter and spring snowpack dynamics.

2.7 Analysis of Gap Effects
284
We use two approaches to estimate gap edge effect on tree ring width response to variations in 
285
moisture availability: a dendrochronology approach, and a paired tree approach. For the 
286
dendrochronology approach, we separated all sampled trees by warm or cool edge, then created 
287
WR, EW, and LW width index chronologies. Initial mean chronologies were created by averaging 
288
ring width indices for each year across all trees (separately for each edge type). We then find the 
289
correlation of each tree’s annual ring width indices to the mean chronology and removing the least 
290
correlated trees to a threshold of minimum r = 0.7. Ring width indices were averaged again cross 
291
remaining trees to create a final mean chronology. We confirmed that sample size was sufficient for 
292
each chronology for the express population signal (EPS) to exceed 0.85 (Fritts 2001). For each ring 
293
width index and climate variable, we found the Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) for the final

mean chronology ring width index and the climate variable using a linear regression model, from 
295
which slope, p value, and correlation coefficient standard error (SE) were also derived.
296
In the paired tree approach, we use r to represent response of each individual tree to variations in 
297
moisture availability. For the years 2000 - 2020 in each tree, we find the correlation between each 
298

301

3. Results
305
3.1 Gap Effects on Snow Depth
306
Despite considerable spatial and temporal variation, snow depth was greater on cool edges than 
307
warm edges in 77% of 100m grid cells during the 2016 ablation period (March – May), and 97% of

4. Discussion
366
In SCB, gaps in forest canopy create cool and warm edge environments that influence snowpack 
367
throughout the snow ablation period. As hypothesized, we found that snow was consistently deeper 
368
on cool edges versus warm edges of gaps during the snow ablation period in March-May 2016 (Fig. 
369
2). At their seasonal maximum, snow depth differences across gaps ranged from 9% of pre-ablation 
370
depths on low steeply south facing slopes to 18% of pre-ablation depths on high steeply north 
371
facing slopes. Snow depth differences and absolute snow depths were greatest at high elevations 
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5204214
Preprint not peer reviewed

17
372
and on steeply north facing slopes, which is consistent with standing knowledge on snow 
373
distributions across terrain (Elder et al. 1991; Tennant et al. 2017). 
374
In February, before the onset of widespread ablation, differences between cool and warm edge 
375
snow depth varied relatively evenly about 0. This is as expected because excluding wind effects, 
376
snow accumulation is largely even across gaps because the largest effect on snow accumulation at a 
377
sub-100 m scale is canopy interception (Gray and McKay 1981; Hedstrom and Pomeroy 1998; 
378
Pomeroy et al. 2002; Varhola et al. 2010). After ablation onset in March, differences in snow depth 
379
across gaps increased, consistent with evidence that snow on warm gap edges melts and sublimates 
380
faster than snow on cool gap edges (Broxton et al. 2015; Mazzotti et al. 2020; Musselman and 
381
Pomeroy 2017). Snow depth differences then decreased as snow ablation progressed and snow 
382
depth approached zero, as seen in low elevation areas for the latest (May 18th) measurement date 
383
(Fig. 2b). Snow depth differences in higher or more north facing terrain types peaked later than in 
384
lower or more south facing terrain types (Fig. 2b). Throughout the study area, snow ablated more 
385
rapidly in warm edges than cool edges. By May 2016, snow depths were depleted to less than 1 cm 
386
in 57% of warm edges, but only 36% of cool edges in the study area (Fig. 3). Reduced snow 
387
persistence and higher ablation rates are likely to influence magnitude and timing of streamflow 
388
(Clow 2010), soil moisture (Harpold et al. 2015), and growing season (Knowles et al. 2018) in the 
389
Western US.

403
Our finding of deeper, more persistent snow on cool gaps edges versus warm gap edges supports 
404
model- and observation-based studies showing decreasing snow depth (Mazzotti et al. 2019; 
405
Moeser et al. 2015) and snow ablation rates (Musselman et al. 2015; Musselman and Pomeroy 
406
2017) as gap size increases. Under our definition, larger gaps have smaller fractional coverage by 
407
cool edges relative to warm edges (Fig. 5). Additionally, the patterns of snow depth we observe on 
408
warm and cool edges are consistent in direction, but smaller in magnitude than those of Currier and 
409
Lundquist (2018), who found 11-38% differences in snow depths for sites in throughout the 
410
Western US. We define cool and warm edge environments differently than Currier and Lundquist 
411
(2018), who assign edge types exclusively based on pixel distance from the nearest north facing or 
412
south facing canopy surface, with experimental cutoffs at 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 times local canopy height. 
413
Our method assigns edge type to a given pixel based on its distance to nearest canopy, mean direct 
414
sunlight intensity, and distance from more intensely sunlit canopy fringes. While this approach 
415
aims to better acknowledge the radiative factors that drive snow ablation (Musselman et al. 2015; 
416
Musselman and Pomeroy 2017) it relies on somewhat arbitrary cutoff values, including the 
417
reference date for illumination and fraction of hours illuminated. Future comparison of 
418
methodologies for defining edge types would benefit any management efforts to optimize snow419
related benefits of forest treatments, particularly those that create or expand forest gaps. 
420
We found that forest gaps had smaller, less consistent effects on tree response to variations in 
421
annual climate compared to their effects on snow depth. In general, whole ring and earlywood 
422
width index chronologies from warm edge trees was more positively correlated with 1 Apr SWE 
423
and precipitation, and more negatively correlated with CWD and maximum temperatures than 
424
those of cool edge trees (Fig. 3). If these correlations are representative of true relationships 
425
between climate and growth, they would indicate that warm edge trees were more sensitive to 
426
variations in moisture than cool edge trees. However, most of these correlations lacked statistical 
427
significance at the 90% confidence level. Similarly, analysis of all sampled warm-cool tree pairs 
428
suggests that on average, warm edge trees are more positively correlated with 1 Apr SWE than cool 
429
edge trees. However, standard errors of correlation coefficients were high, limiting our confidence 
430
in the size of the observed gap effect. 
431
With a few exceptions, even warm edge trees sampled in SCB displayed relatively low moisture 
432
sensitivities compared to highly drought sensitive trees observed throughout the Western US 
433
(Williams et al. 2013). This is somewhat expected because SCB is not especially arid relative to the 
434
typical ranges of our sampled tree species, and our tree sampling design prioritized intra-pair 
435
consistency and inter-pair diversity over indicators of drought stress. In identifying gaps for

sampling, we found very few well defined gaps in locations traditionally associated with high 
437
drought sensitivity, such as steep south facing slopes on the low end of species’ typical elevation 
438
ranges (Fritts 2001), because in SCB these areas are characterized by more open and fragmented 
439
forest canopy. Previous work near SCB that sampled trees more likely to be moisture sensitive 
440
found relatively low correlations between ring width indices and 1 Apr SWE in the same year 
441
(Lepley et al. 2020). We suggest that finding any gap effect on climate response, albeit small relative 
442

443
limited snowy conifer forests. It would also be especially valuable to understand whether the cold444
edge effects may have greater influences on regeneration and seedling water status, as many of the 
445
lauded benefits of gap creation in forests relate to facilitating recruitment (Yamamoto 2000). Given 
446
that seedlings have shallower roots and thus are more sensitive to dessication of shallow soils, the 
447
long-persisting snow may have more benefits to seedlings than to the mature trees that were the 
448
focus of this study.
449
Exploratory investigation suggested that ecological factors may have larger, more robust effects on 
450
tree response to variation in snowpack than snow depth or position relative to gap edges (Supp. 
451
Table 2). In a multiple linear regression model containing terrain, ecological, and snow depth 
452
variables, tree species (effect estimate =  0.98, SE = 0.27, p < 0.01) and competition (effect estimate 
453
=  -0.63, SE = 0.31, p = 0.05) had notable effects on correlation of ring width index with 1 Apr SWE, 
454
with effect sizes strongly exceeding that of tree-adjacent snow depth (slope =  -0.28, SE = 0.29, p = 
455
0.34). In this study, we were unable to thoroughly interrogate interactions of terrain and ecological 
456
variables because subgroup sample sizes were too small or uneven for robust analysis. Collection of 
457
additional tree cores using these results as a preliminary framework could address lingering 
458
uncertainties regarding the influence of terrain and ecological variables on gap effects and tree 
459
response to moisture availability. Assessment of gap effects in other ecosystems is warranted as 
460
our analysis is limited in scope to a single small basin in the Central Sierra Nevada, and its 
461
snowpack, forests’ drought response, and ecohydrological patterns are likely distinct from other 
462
areas in the Western US (Mote et al. 2018; Restaino et al. 2016; Varhola et al. 2010).
463
In Central Sierra Nevada forests, recommendations for management of forest structures have 
464
recently adopted a preference for structural heterogeneity across scales primarily citing balanced 
465
benefits for wildlife and resistance and resilience to fire (North et al. 2009). These 
466
recommendations guided the development of the Sagehen Project, a plan for forest restoration in 
467
SCB. Forest treatments in the Sagehen Project introduce and expand gaps directly as “early seral 
468
openings” (ESOs) and indirectly through variable density thinning and prescribed burning (USDA

482
Guarín and Taylor 2005; Csank et al. 2016; De Frenne et al. 2021; Keen et al. 2022), further 
483
fostering structural heterogeneity in the recovering forest.

5. Conclusion
485
In this study we demonstrate that in a snow dominated Central Sierra Nevada basin, forest gaps 
486
foster predictable spatial and temporal patterns of snowpack with potential impacts on trees’ 
487
response to moisture availability. Our findings are consistent with and build upon work showing 
488
the importance of forest structure for driving heterogeneity in the distribution, ablation, and late 
489
season persistence of snow (Broxton et al. 2015; Currier and Lundquist 2018; Mazzotti et al. 2019) 
490
and for water use and drought resilience in snow-dominated forests (Stephens et al. 2010; 
491
Hessburg et al. 2016). We also present a novel, spatially resolved demonstration of gap edge effects 
492
as a control on the timing of snow ablation, and we suggest that changes in forest structure that 
493
alter gap size and distribution may have implications for snowpack depth and persistence at larger 
494
scales. Recent forest management efforts in the Western US have aimed to increase structural 
495
heterogeneity in forests to benefit wildlife and reduce the severity of future wildfires (North et al. 
496
2009; Stephens et al. 2021). In SCB, these efforts involve the introduction and preservation of forest 
497
gaps (North et al. 2009; USDA Forest Service 2013), and our work suggests an opportunity to tailor 
498
forest treatments to also benefit snowpack and potentially forest drought response. Strategically 
499
considering the size and directionality of gaps may allow managers to maximize cool edge 
500
distribution, fostering forest patches with deeper snow and lessened sensitivities to summer 
501
drought. The findings presented here are specific to SCB, but may be transferrable to seasonally

dry, snow dominated forests in northern mid-latitude regions. There is high confidence that water
