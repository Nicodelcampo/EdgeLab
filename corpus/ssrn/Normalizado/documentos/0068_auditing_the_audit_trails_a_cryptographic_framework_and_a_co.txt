Abstract
The U.S. Federal Reserve’s SR 11-7 (April 2011) and the EU AI Act (Regulation
2024/1689, eﬀective for high-risk systems on 2 August 2026) require ﬁrms deploying machine-learning models in regulated domains to maintain per-prediction audit
trails: code state, data state, model state, compute environment, and the prediction itself, recorded with enough integrity protection that an external reviewer can
verify any claim ex post. Existing open-source ML tooling (MLﬂow, Weights &
Biases, DVC) targets experiment tracking or artifact versioning; none produces
an SR 11-7-shaped audit trail with low-friction integration, vendor-neutral storage, and regulator-export capability out of the box. This paper makes two paired
contributions.
First, we introduce mr-audit, an open-source Python package that writes one
immutable, hash-chained record per ML prediction, capturing git commit, library
versions, input hash, model parameters, compute environment, random seed, and
prediction value. Records form a linked hash chain via SHA-256; tampering with
any record breaks the chain.
The package ships with a replay engine, a driftdetection module (Kolmogorov-Smirnov plus Population Stability Index), and a
regulator-export bundle. We compare mr-audit head-to-head against ﬁve existing tools on nine audit-trail capabilities and on three quantitative benchmarks
(throughput, storage overhead, tamper-detection sensitivity).

Second, we apply the same compliance rubric to a corpus of 185 published
machine-learning papers in quantitative ﬁnance posted between 1952 and 2025
(drawn from the 119-paper survey corpus of Khan (2026b) and a 2024–2025 arXivq-ﬁn extension constrained to papers with a discoverable code-repository link).
For each paper, an automated pipeline locates the code link, clones the repository,
and mechanically scores the six static compliance dimensions: code accessibility,
data release, computational environment, random-seed documentation, input-data
lineage, and model-state persistence. The static-six analysis followed a pre-speciﬁed
plan (hypotheses and rubric ﬁxed before the scoring step and deposited at OSF);
we are explicit that this is a pre-speciﬁed plan, not a timestamp-enforced preregistration, and that the six manual-review dimensions deferred to a follow-up
version will be genuinely pre-registered (timestamp-enforced) before any of their
scoring begins. On the six automatable dimensions we ﬁnd: a mean static-rubric
score of 1.52 of 6 (full corpus, N = 185); a mean of 3.28 of 6 conditional on the
paper carrying a discoverable code link (N = 80); 11 of 185 papers (5.9%) score
the full 6 of 6 on the static rubric, all of them within the 80-paper code-link subset
(13.8% of it); the temporal slope of compliance is +0.088 score units per year (in
the pre-speciﬁed range [0.05, 0.20], statistically nonzero); and a 10% rot rate on
discovered code URLs (21% if non-GitHub repositories are counted). Three papers
(FinRL, FinGPT, Grinsztajn tabular-benchmark) achieve a perfect static score of
6 of 6; they are existence proofs that audit-grade publication is achievable but far
from the ﬁeld’s median. The six manual-review dimensions (bit-identical replay,
per-prediction logging, drift monitoring, signiﬁcance testing, multiple-comparison
correction, out-of-sample protocol) require Tier-1 code execution or manual review
and are deferred to v2.1 of this paper. The full per-paper scorecard, the scoring
pipeline, and an audit-of-audit log are released publicly with the package.
The package, the audit pipeline, the corpus, the per-paper scorecard, an audit
script that re-derives every numerical claim in this paper from JSON outputs, and
a 6,825-record demonstration log on the seven-model volatility-forecasting panel of
Khan (2026f) are released at github.com/ayk5511/mr-audit.

Introduction
In April 2011, the U.S. Federal Reserve and the Oﬃce of the Comptroller of the Currency
jointly issued SR 11-7 / OCC Bulletin 2011-12, which established that any model used
by a regulated bank to make decisions of consequence must be supported by suﬃcient developmental evidence, monitoring evidence, and replication capability for an independent
reviewer to verify, at any point in the model’s deployed lifetime, exactly what was done.
In June 2024, the European Union enacted Regulation 2024/1689, the AI Act, whose
Article 12 requires high-risk AI systems to maintain “automatic recording of events” over
their full lifecycle, and whose Article 19 requires that those records be retained for at
least six months. The high-risk provisions of the AI Act become eﬀective on 2 August
2026 (European Parliament and Council, 2024); the SR 11-7 expectations stood for ﬁfteen years and were carried into revised interagency model-risk guidance issued in 2026
(Federal Reserve, 2011; Oﬃce of the Comptroller of the Currency, 2011). The combined
regulatory posture is unambiguous: any machine-learning model whose predictions inform a decision of consequence in a regulated industry must produce an audit trail of
those predictions, and the audit trail must be tamper-evident, replayable, and exportable
to a supervisor.
We take this regulatory premise as given and ask two questions about its implications
for the published research record.
Engineering question. What does a vendor-neutral, open-source audit trail for an
ML inference pipeline actually look like? Existing tools fall into three categories. (1)
Experiment-tracking platforms (MLﬂow, Weights & Biases) are designed for train-time
exploration, with rich UIs for run comparison but no built-in immutability or hashchained integrity. (2) Data and model versioning systems (DVC, Pachyderm) version
artifacts but do not log per-prediction inputs and outputs. (3) Cloud-managed monitoring
services (AWS SageMaker Model Monitor, Vertex AI Model Monitoring) are vendorlocked and require trusting a cloud provider with the exact inference data the audit was
designed to verify. None of these tools is shaped against the per-prediction provenance
requirement of SR 11-7 ğ V or AI Act Article 12. These provenance gaps are a concrete
instance of the hidden technical debt that accrues in production machine-learning systems
(Sculley et al., 2015).
Empirical question.
Machine learning is now a core methodology in quantitative
ﬁnance, with benchmarks such as Gu et al. (2020) establishing its empirical value for asset
pricing. Of 185 published machine-learning papers on quantitative ﬁnance (the 119-paper
structured corpus of Khan (2026b) plus a 66-paper 2024–2025 arXiv extension restricted
to papers with a discoverable code link), what fraction could survive a regulatory audit
against those same requirements? The answer matters because the published research
record is the material from which production models are built. If the academic record is
5

not auditable, no model derived from it can be either.
This paper provides both answers.
1.1
Contributions
The contributions are two, paired by construction.
The framework. We introduce mr-audit, an open-source Python package (MITlicensed; github.com/ayk5511/mr-audit) that produces immutable, hash-chained, replayable
audit trails for ML inference pipelines. The package is integrated by a one-line decorator
(@auditable) and a context manager (AuditContext); no central server, no cloud account, no schema design. Records are stored locally in SQLite or JSON Lines. The hash
chain is SHA-256: each record’s identiﬁer is the canonical-JSON SHA-256 of its content,
each record carries the previous record’s identiﬁer, and tampering with any record breaks
the chain. The package ships with a replay engine (reconstruct any prediction from the
log), a drift-detection module (Kolmogorov-Smirnov plus Population Stability Index),
and a regulator-export bundle whose integrity can be veriﬁed using oﬀ-the-shelf hash
tools without mr-audit installed. We compare mr-audit head-to-head against ﬁve existing tools on nine audit-trail capabilities (Table 4) and on three quantitative benchmarks
(throughput, storage overhead, tamper-detection sensitivity) in Section 4.
The compliance survey. We then apply mr-audit’s twelve-dimension audit-trail
rubric—drawn directly from SR 11-7 ğğ IV–VI and AI Act Articles 12 and 19—to a
corpus of 185 published machine-learning papers in quantitative ﬁnance from 1952–2025.
The corpus is constructed in two parts: 119 papers from the structured database of Khan
(2026b) (covering 1952–2023) and 66 additional 2024–2025 papers pulled from arXiv q-ﬁn
and cs.LG with discoverable code repositories. For each paper, an automated pipeline
(i) locates the code link in the abstract or PDF body, (ii) clones the repository, (iii)
mechanically scores the six static compliance dimensions: code accessibility, data release, computational environment, random-seed documentation, input-data lineage, and
model-state persistence. The full per-paper scorecard is released publicly (Khan, 2026c)
as part of this paper’s submission. The static-six hypotheses and rubric were ﬁxed in a
written analysis plan before the scoring step was run, deposited at OSF (Khan, 2026a);
we describe this honestly as a pre-speciﬁed plan rather than a timestamp-enforced preregistration, and we explain the distinction and the actual timing in Section 5.5. The
remaining six dimensions (bit-identical replay, per-prediction logging, drift monitoring,
signiﬁcance testing, multiple-comparison correction, out-of-sample protocol) require either Tier-1 replication or manual PDF review, are deferred to v2.1, and will be the subject
of a genuine timestamp-enforced pre-registration ﬁled on OSF before any of their scoring
begins.
The compliance result is reported in Section 6. The headline: the ﬁeld’s mean static6

rubric score is 1.52 of 6 (full corpus), 3.28 of 6 conditional on a discoverable code link,
with a positive but slow temporal trend of +0.088 score units per year.
1.2
Why this question matters now
The deadline is not abstract. The EU AI Act’s high-risk provisions become eﬀective 2
August 2026. Firms that deploy ML models in lending, insurance, employment, credit
scoring, or healthcare must, by that date, have audit trails in place that satisfy Article
19’s six-month-retention requirement. The U.S. has had its analog requirement in place
since 2011 but practitioner adoption has been concentrated in the largest banks; smaller
ﬁrms and the academic community publishing the methods that smaller ﬁrms adopt have
lagged. There is no vendor-neutral, citable, open-source reference implementation of an
SR 11-7-shaped audit trail in the Python ecosystem.
If our compliance-survey ﬁnding is roughly what the priors of Khan (2026b) suggest—
that under 5% of published ML-ﬁnance papers currently maintain enough provenance
to survive a regulatory audit—then the gap between the academic literature and the
regulatory standard is one order of magnitude.
Closing that gap is partly a tooling
problem (what we address with mr-audit) and partly a cultural problem (what we hope
to address by making the gap measurable).
1.3
Related work
This paper sits at the intersection of three literatures.
Model documentation. Mitchell et al. (2019) introduce Model Cards as a structured
documentation format for trained ML models, since operationalized into community tooling and templates (Hugging Face, 2023). Subsequent work extends the pattern to datasets
(Gebru et al., 2021; Pushkarna et al., 2022) and to industry-supplier declarations (Arnold
et al., 2019); such formats are in part a response to the downstream harms of undervalued
data work documented by Sambasivan et al. (2021). These are point-in-time documentation artefacts produced once per model. They are necessary but not suﬃcient for SR 11-7
/ EU AI Act compliance, which require per-prediction provenance maintained across the
deployed lifetime of the system. Our contribution complements model documentation:
where Model Cards describe what the model is, the audit trail described here records
what each prediction was.
Algorithmic auditing.
Raji et al. (2020) propose an end-to-end internal-auditing
framework spanning scoping, mapping, artifact collection, testing, and reﬂection. Brundage
et al. (2020) argue that “veriﬁable claims” about AI system behavior require mechanisms
beyond self-reporting, including tamper-evident audit trails. Our work operationalizes
the audit-trail mechanism speciﬁcally in a form designed against SR 11-7 and AI Act
record-keeping clauses, and additionally provides, with the empirical compliance survey,
7

the ﬁrst quantitative evidence on the size of the gap between current published research
and the regulatory standard.
Reproducibility audits. The replication crisis is a cross-disciplinary concern, from Ioannidis (2005)’s argument that most published research ﬁndings are false to Open Science
Collaboration (2015)’s replication of 100 psychology experiments at fewer than half the
original eﬀect sizes. In ﬁnance speciﬁcally, Harvey et al. (2016) and Hou et al. (2020) document that a large fraction of published equity-factor anomalies do not survive multipletesting correction; the latter, replicating 452 anomalies, has accumulated over 1,400 citations. Within machine learning, Gundersen and Kjensmo (2018) and the Pineau et al.
(2021) NeurIPS reproducibility program quantify how rarely published ML work reports
enough to be reproduced. Our compliance survey is methodologically adjacent: rather
than replicate the empirical ﬁndings of ML-ﬁnance papers, we audit whether the papers
themselves contain enough provenance for any future replication to be meaningful. The
closest precedent in the quant-ﬁnance literature is Khan (2026b), whose Reproducibility
Disclosure Score we extend with a twelve-dimension regulatory-compliance rubric.
1.4
What this paper claims and does not claim
Three explicit scoping decisions deserve ﬂagging.
First, we do not claim that using mr-audit makes any model compliant with SR 11-7
or the EU AI Act. Compliance is a determination by the ﬁrm and its supervisor; the
package provides the technical record-keeping infrastructure that compliance requires.
Second, the compliance-survey rubric is operationalized against SR 11-7 ğğ IV–VI and
AI Act Articles 12 and 19 speciﬁcally. Other jurisdictions (UK PRA SS1/23, Singapore
MAS Veritas, Canada OSFI E-23) have closely related but not identical requirements; we
map the diﬀerences in Section 2. The survey results are reported against the SR 11-7 /
AI Act rubric and should not be over-interpreted as cross-jurisdiction passage rates.
Third, the survey corpus is constructed from public arXiv and journal sources. Papers
without any public code repository are recorded in the corpus but receive automatic 0
scores on the seven dimensions that depend on code. We report two compliance rates:
an unconditional rate (treating no-code as failure) and a conditional rate (restricted to
papers with public code). Both are pre-speciﬁed in the analysis plan.

Regulatory framework
The audit-trail requirements an ML pipeline must satisfy come from three regulatory
sources of growing weight: SR 11-7 / OCC 2011-12 (United States, 2011), the EU AI Act
(Regulation 2024/1689, 2024), and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST AI
100-1, 2023). They diﬀer in legal force and in granularity but converge on a common set
of technical record-keeping requirements that the package described in Section 3 aims to
satisfy.
2.1
SR 11-7 / OCC 2011-12
Federal Reserve (2011) and the companion Oﬃce of the Comptroller of the Currency
(2011) are the canonical U.S. supervisory guidance on model risk management for
federally-regulated banking institutions. The guidance applies to “models” broadly deﬁned, encompassing both traditional statistical models and modern machine-learning
systems. The relevant requirements for audit trails fall in three sections of the guidance:
Model documentation (SR 11-7 ğV). Banks are expected to document each model’s
purpose, theory, methodology, assumptions, and limitations, with suﬃcient detail that
a knowledgeable reader unfamiliar with the model can understand its operation. For
ML models, this includes the choice of architecture, hyperparameters, training data, and
validation results.
Ongoing monitoring (SR 11-7 ğIV, within Model Validation). Banks are expected
to verify that models perform as intended over time, with speciﬁc attention to changes
in input data distributions, model drift, and the continued validity of model assumptions. SR 11-7 explicitly mentions monitoring of input variables for drift relative to the
development sample.
Model inventory (SR 11-7 ğV). Each model in production must be in a model inventory
that captures (at minimum) the model identity, version, owner, validation status, and
use cases. The inventory is the durable record an examiner consults during a supervisory
review.
9

The guidance is principles-based; it does not specify how the documentation, monitoring, or inventory should be technically implemented. In practice, large U.S. banks
operate model risk management organizations of 50 to several hundred staﬀwho maintain these records, often through a combination of centralized governance platforms (e.g.,
SAS Model Manager) and bespoke spreadsheet-based tracking.
2.2
The EU AI Act
European Parliament and Council (2024) (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force
on August 1, 2024 with phased implementation: prohibited AI practices became applicable on February 2, 2025, obligations for general-purpose AI models on August 2, 2025,
and most remaining obligations including the high-risk regime on August 2, 2026.
For “high-risk AI systems” (deﬁned in Article 6 with reference to Annexes I and III),
the Act imposes speciﬁc technical record-keeping requirements that are stricter than SR
11-7. Two articles are directly relevant.
Article 12 – Record-keeping.
High-risk AI systems must technically allow for the
automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system. The logs must enable
monitoring of operation in line with intended purpose and, in particular, identiﬁcation
of situations which may result in the AI system presenting a risk within the meaning of
Article 79.
Article 19 – Automatically generated logs. Providers of high-risk AI systems must
keep the logs automatically generated by their AI systems for a period determined by
Union or national law, and at minimum for six months after the system is placed on the
market. The speciﬁc logging contents the Act enumerates appear in Article 12(3): for
the biometric systems of Annex III(1)(a), the logs must record, at minimum, the period
of each use, the reference database against which input data has been checked, the input
data for which the search has led to a match, and the identiﬁcation of the natural persons
involved in the veriﬁcation of the results. The general logging duty of Article 12(1)–(2)
binds all high-risk systems without enumerating contents; we adopt the Article 12(3)
items as a concrete template for a general-purpose audit trail.
The volatility-forecasting use case demonstrated in Section 4 is not currently classiﬁed
as “high-risk” under Annex III, which lists creditworthiness and access to essential services
among ﬁnancial-sector use cases but not market-data forecasting. The AI Act framing is
therefore forward-looking for the present demonstration; we record audit data that would
satisfy Article 12 and Article 19 in the event that the use case were brought into scope,
or in the event that the ﬁrm chooses to apply the same standard voluntarily.
Table 1 maps each Article 12(3) logging item to the speciﬁc mr-audit ﬁelds that
satisfy it, providing a compliance traceability matrix a CRO can hand to a regulator
without re-engineering.
The cross-domain examples in the package’s examples/ di10

rectory (a sklearn LogisticRegression credit-scorecard scenario; a statsmodels ARIMA
macroeconomic forecaster) demonstrate the same ﬁeld mapping holds for in-scope Annex
III.5(b) credit-scoring use cases as well as for the demonstration’s volatility forecaster.
Table 1: EU AI Act Article 12(3) logging items mapped to mr-audit schema ﬁelds. These
four items are the only logging contents the Act enumerates explicitly (Article 12(3), for

2.3
NIST AI Risk Management Framework
The NIST AI RMF (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023) is a voluntary
U.S. framework structured around four functions: GOVERN (organizational policies and
accountability), MAP (context and risk identiﬁcation), MEASURE (analysis, assessment,
and tracking), and MANAGE (prioritization and response). The MEASURE function
explicitly references monitoring for distributional shifts in input data, behavioral drift in
model outputs, and traceability of decisions. NIST AI 100-1 is not legally binding, but it
is increasingly cited by ﬁrms as the de facto operational implementation framework that
“operationalizes” SR 11-7’s principles for ML models.
11

2.4
ISO/IEC 42001 management system standard
The fourth framework relevant to ML audit trails is International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission (2023) (published December 2023), the international standard for AI management systems. ISO/IEC 42001 is
structured as a Plan-Do-Check-Act management system covering organizational context
(Clause 4), leadership (5), planning (6), support (7), operation (8), performance evaluation (9), and improvement (10). The audit-trail-relevant requirements concentrate in
three clauses:
Clause 6.1.2 (AI system risk assessment). Requires documented risk-assessment processes covering identiﬁed risks, treatment plans, and residual-risk acceptance. Audit trails
support this by providing the veriﬁable record against which assessments are revisited.
Clause 8.4 (AI system impact assessment). Requires documented impact assessments
for AI systems including their eﬀects on individuals, groups, and society. Per-prediction
trails enable retrospective impact analysis.
Clause 9.1 (Monitoring, measurement, analysis, and evaluation). Requires that the
organization determine what needs to be monitored and measured, the methods used,
when these are performed, and when the results are analyzed and evaluated.
Perprediction logging is the operational substrate.
Unlike SR 11-7 (US bank-speciﬁc) or the EU AI Act (EU-jurisdictional), ISO/IEC
42001 is a voluntary international standard accessible to any organization. Firms pursuing
AI Management System certiﬁcation under ISO/IEC 42001 will increasingly look for
tooling that satisﬁes the relevant clauses automatically. The mapping in Table 3 identiﬁes
the speciﬁc schema ﬁelds that satisfy each clause.

The mr-audit package
This section describes the design and implementation of mr-audit. The package is opensource under MIT license at github.com/ayk5511/mr-audit; the version described here is
0.1.0.
3.1
Design principles
The package follows four design principles, in order of priority.
1. Vendor-neutrality. All persistence is local. The two storage backends (SQLite,
JSON Lines) are open formats with stable speciﬁcations. There is no cloud service, no
central server, no schema-design platform, no proprietary ﬁle format. A ﬁrm can run
mr-audit entirely on-premises, in a regulated network, with the audit log on a write-only
ﬁle system.
17

2.
Low
integration
friction.
Adding
mr-audit
to
an
existing
pipeline
is
one
decorator
and
one
context
manager:
the
developer
adds
@auditable(model_name=..., model_version=...)
to
the
prediction
function,
and wraps the calling code in with AuditContext(log_path=...): .... Without an
active context the decorator is a no-op (the function runs unrecorded), so the same code
can be run with or without auditing depending on context.
3. Immutability and integrity. The log is append-only by API; there is no public
“edit” or “delete” operation. Each record carries a SHA-256 identiﬁer computed from its
canonical-JSON content (excluding the identiﬁer itself), and a pointer to the previous
record’s identiﬁer. Tampering with any single record breaks the chain at that point.
4. Replayability. Every prediction must be reconstructable from the log alone, given
the unwrapped prediction function. The replay engine recomputes the input hash from
the supplied inputs and re-runs the function; both the input hash and the prediction
value must match the recorded values for the replay to be considered valid.

3.3
Hash chain
The hash chain implements a linked-hash integrity guarantee. Each record’s identiﬁer is
computed as
record_id = SHA-256( canonical-JSON(R \ {record_id}) )
where canonical-JSON is the deterministic serialization deﬁned by json.dumps(...,
sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":")).
The exclusion of record_id from its
own hash input is necessary; the exclusion is implemented in compute_record_id
(src/mr_audit/core/hash.py).
19

Chain integrity is veriﬁed by verify_chain, which performs three checks for each
record:
1. The record’s record_id matches the canonical hash of its own contents.
2. The record’s prev_record_hash matches the record_id of the immediately preceding
record in insertion order.
3. The ﬁrst record’s prev_record_hash is None.
Tampering with any ﬁeld of any record breaks check (1) for that record and all subsequent records (because the chain link cascades). Reordering records breaks check (2).
Inserting a forged record requires both forging a valid hash for the inserted record and
re-computing all subsequent records’ hashes, which is computationally infeasible if the
chain is also externally backed up at any point.
The chosen hash function is SHA-256 (NIST FIPS 180-4). The choice is consistent
with current best practice and aligns with the EU AI Act’s intent in Article 19 without
specifying any particular algorithm.
3.4
Storage backends
Two backends are implemented, both vendor-neutral and locally runnable.
SQLiteStore (src/mr_audit/core/store.py).
Records are stored in a singletable schema with columns (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY AUTOINCREMENT, record_id
TEXT UNIQUE, prev_record_hash TEXT, timestamp_utc TEXT, json TEXT). Indexes
on record_id and timestamp_utc provide O(log n) lookup by either. Suited for production use up to millions of records on a single ﬁle.
JSONLStore. Records are appended one per line to a single ﬁle. Lightweight, humanreadable, easily processed by jq, pandas, or R. Suited for lightweight pipelines and human
inspection. Random access requires a full scan; suited for moderate sizes.
Both backends preserve insertion order, which is what the hash chain depends on.
Both are append-only by API. No public API exposes deletion or update.
3.5
Replay engine
The replay function in src/mr_audit/replay/engine.py reconstructs any prediction
from a sealed audit record. The contract is: given the record and the unwrapped prediction function, calling the function with the recorded inputs must produce the recorded
prediction. The function returns a ReplayResult dataclass with three principal ﬂags:
• input_hash_match – the canonical-JSON hash of the supplied inputs equals the
recorded input_data_hash.
20

• prediction_match – the re-run output equals the recorded prediction.value within
the supplied tolerance (default 10−9).
• library_version_warnings – non-fatal diagnostic; library versions present at replay
time are compared to the versions recorded.
Replay is non-destructive: the input record is never mutated. The supplied inputs
are re-hashed and compared; if the supplied inputs do not match the recorded input hash
(Section 4.2), the result still re-runs the function but ﬂags the mismatch for the auditor’s
attention.
3.6
Drift detection module
The detect_drift function in src/mr_audit/drift/input.py compares two record sequences (a baseline and a window) for distribution drift across the recorded feature summaries. Two methods are computed for each feature:
Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample test. Null hypothesis: the two samples come from
the same distribution. Returns the test statistic and an asymptotic p-value. We use
SciPy’s stats.ks_2samp for the implementation.
Population Stability Index. A scalar measure of distribution shift, deﬁned as
PSI =
B
∑
i=1
(pw,i −pb,i) · log
(pw,i
pb,i
)
,
where pb,i and pw,i are the proportions of baseline and window samples in bucket i,
respectively. Buckets are equal-frequency on the baseline. Empty buckets are handled
by Laplace (add-half) smoothing – adding 0.5 to each bucket count before normalising –
to avoid log(0) blowups while keeping the estimator unbiased on well-populated buckets.
Standard credit-risk thresholds (PSI < 0.10 stable, 0.10–0.25 moderate, ≥0.25 high) are
widely adopted in bank model-risk practice.
The choice to compute drift on summaries rather than raw feature values is deliberate.
Audit logs that store full feature vectors per prediction grow rapidly and may capture
sensitive customer-level information. The default summary captures the per-feature value
at prediction time but not the underlying customer record; ﬁrms can opt into full-vector
storage by passing summarize_features=False to the @auditable decorator.
3.7

The export_bundle function in src/mr_audit/export/bundle.py produces a selfcontained zip archive that an external reviewer can verify without any mr-audit code
installed. The bundle contains:
21

• manifest.json – bundle metadata + per-ﬁle SHA-256 hashes
• audit_records.jsonl – all records, canonical JSON, one per line
• chain_verification.json – chain veriﬁcation result at export time
• README.txt – human-readable description and replay instructions
• manifest.sha256 – SHA-256 of
manifest.json in the standard sha256sumcompatible format
A reviewer with only sha256sum and a JSON parser can verify the bundle’s integrity
end-to-end: sha256sum manifest.json should match manifest.sha256; for each ﬁle in
manifest.json’s files ﬁeld, sha256sum <name> should match the listed digest. With
mr-audit installed, the veriﬁcation is one command: mr-audit inspect <bundle>.

retrospectively instrument the seven-model volatility-forecasting panel of Khan (2026f):
three GARCH variants (GARCH, EGARCH, GJR-GARCH), HAR-RV, LightGBM, XGBoost, and an equal-weight ensemble, predicting 5-day-ahead realized volatility on the
S&P 500 over 980 trading days from 2022-01-03 to 2025-11-26.
4.1
Instrumentation setup
The instrumentation is retrospective. The seven-model panel of Khan (2026f) ships its
forecast outputs as forecasts_5d.parquet (980 rows × 7 model columns + 1 actual
column) at ayk5511/volatility-forecasting. We do not re-run the panel’s training or forecasting code. Instead, we treat each (date, model) cell of the parquet as one prediction
event and write one mr-audit record per cell, capturing what an integrated-from-the-start
instrumentation would have recorded.
For each prediction event, the recorded inputs are the ﬁve lagged realized-volatility
values (rvt−1, rvt−2, rvt−3, rvt−4, rvt−5), which are the most natural input for a 5-day-ahead
vol forecast and which provide a meaningful basis for drift analysis over the test period.
The prediction.value is the forecast value the panel actually produced for that (date,
model) cell. The model.name is one of the seven model names; model.version is set to
paper2-v1 for all records to match the source release.

= SHA-256(canonical-JSON content) for every record AND prev_record_hash =
record_id of immediately preceding record. Any mutation of either ﬁeld is mechanically caught by verify_chain.

contiguous block of trailing records but leaves the remaining sub-chain self-consistent.
verify_chain sees valid records 1 through 6,725 forming an unbroken hash chain ending
at the truncated boundary, with no internal evidence that records 6,726–6,825 ever existed. Detecting this attack requires external anchoring: periodic publication of the latest
record_id to a trusted third-party store (an internal audit ledger, a public blockchain,
a regulator-controlled timestamp service, or signed publication of latest-record manifest
by an HSM) so that an auditor can verify the present log’s latest record matches the
externally-anchored expected value. We do not provide external attestation in v0.1.0;
we provide the substrate on which a ﬁrm can layer its preferred attestation strategy.
Section 7.6 lists external-attestation hooks as the v0.2.0 priority.

not a derived summary. If a regulator accepts a chain-veriﬁcation summary (“the ﬁrm

can independently re-derive the chain’s validity without trusting any attestation from the
ﬁrm.
4.9
Summary of the demonstration
The ﬁve use cases together demonstrate that a 6,825-record audit trail produced by
mr-audit on a real seven-model volatility-forecasting pipeline can be:
• Replayed exactly (use case 1, 5/5 sampled records match bit-identically),
• Monitored for distributional drift across calendar windows (use case 2, 44/44 months
ﬂagged consistently with the documented regime shift) on both inputs and outputs
(detect_drift, detect_output_drift),
• Used as the sole basis for a per-model validation report (use case 3, 7 models, 975
predictions each),
• Exported as a regulator-portable, integrity-tied bundle (use case 4, 841 KB, all checks
pass),
• Used as the data source for an audit-grade right-to-explanation reconstruction of any
historical prediction (use case 5).
The demonstration’s purpose is not to argue that the seven-model panel itself satisﬁes
SR 11-7 (that determination is the bank’s, not the package’s); it is to show that the technical record-keeping that an SR 11-7-compliant deployment would require is producible
and veriﬁable using vendor-neutral, open-source tooling at a realistic data scale.
5
Compliance Survey: Methodology
We now apply the audit-trail rubric of mr-audit to a corpus of 185 published machinelearning papers in quantitative ﬁnance from 1952–2025, asking the empirical question:
what fraction of the published research record satisﬁes the per-prediction provenance
standard that SR 11-7 ğ V and AI Act Article 19 jointly impose? This section describes
the corpus, the rubric, and the scoring procedure. Section 6 reports the results.
The static-six analysis followed a written plan—the hypotheses of Section 5.5 and the
rubric of Section 5.2—ﬁxed before the scoring step was run and deposited at (Khan,
2026a).
We are deliberately precise about what this is and is not (Section 5.5): it
is a pre-speciﬁed analysis plan, not a timestamp-enforced pre-registration of the kind
35

whose evidentiary value rests on a third-party record predating the data. The per-paper
scorecard, the cloned repositories’ state hashes, and the audit-of-audit log are deposited
at (Khan, 2026c).

5.3
Tiered scoring procedure
The 185-paper corpus is scored in three tiers of increasing depth. The tiering reﬂects the
practical constraint that running other authors’ code end-to-end is the most expensive
step and is not feasible for every paper.
Tier 1: full replication. For the small subset of papers that release both code
and a publicly accessible dataset, we attempt full end-to-end replication. The repository
is cloned, dependencies are installed in an isolated Docker container, the code is run
with a thirty-minute timeout, and the produced headline number is compared to the
published value with tolerance ±1%. From the 119-paper base corpus, 14 papers meet the
both-code-and-data criterion (per the original RDS scoring). The 2024–2025 extension
contributes additional Tier 1 candidates; the count after the full corpus run is reported
in Section 6.
Tier 2: code-runnable audit. For papers that release code but rely on proprietary
or paywalled data (e.g., CRSP, Bloomberg, OptionMetrics), we score the dimensions
that depend on the repository state but not on data access: dimensions 1, 3, 4, 5 (where
“replay” is interpreted as inter-run determinism on synthetic inputs), 6, 7. Approximately
40 papers fall into this tier.
Tier 3: surface compliance. For papers without a code link, or where the link does
not resolve, we score the dimensions inferable from the published text alone: dimensions
1 (failed), 9, 10, 11, 12. Approximately 130 papers fall into this tier.

5.4
Automation and human review
Six dimensions are predominantly automatable: 1 (HTTP HEAD), 2 (inspection for a
linked or bundled dataset), 3 (ﬁlesystem inspection for dependency ﬁles), 4 (regex grep
for seed patterns), 6 (regex grep for hash and DOI patterns), 7 (ﬁlesystem inspection
for saved model artifacts plus regex grep for save patterns in code). The remaining six
dimensions (5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12) require either substantive judgment on data accessibility
or close reading of the paper text. For these, we use a structured large-language-model
(Anthropic Claude) prompt as a ﬁrst-pass classiﬁer, with the score then human-veriﬁed
on every paper.
To establish inter-rater reliability, twenty papers are independently dual-scored by the
primary author and a second scorer (a ﬁnance + ML researcher external to this project).
We pre-register acceptable inter-rater agreement at Cohen’s κ ≥0.7 on the manual-review
dimensions; if the observed κ falls below this threshold, the manual codebook is revised
and the aﬀected dimensions are re-scored on the full corpus.
The full prompts, the LLM responses, the dual-scoring decisions, and the codebook
revisions are released alongside the paper. None of the scoring is post-hoc relative to
the plan; all decisions follow the pre-speciﬁed protocol (Khan, 2026a), subject to the
provenance caveat of Section 5.5.
5.5
Pre-speciﬁed hypotheses, and an honest account of the timing
We pre-specify ﬁve hypotheses. Because this paper is about audit-trail integrity, we are
scrupulous about the provenance of the pre-speciﬁcation itself rather than asserting more
than the record supports.
• H1. The fraction of papers passing the twelve-dimension rubric at threshold ≥9 is
between 0% and 3%.
• H2. The fraction whose Tier-1 replication reproduces the headline within ±1% is
between 2% and 8%.
• H3. Mean total score across the full corpus is between 2 and 4 out of 12.
• H4. A regression of total score on publication year yields a positive coeﬃcient of
between 0.05 and 0.20 score units per year, p < 0.05.
• H5. Papers published in peer-reviewed journals (vs. arXiv-only) have mean total
scores at least 1.0 point higher.
The hypotheses are framed as ranges rather than point estimates to permit interpretation in either direction. Results falling outside the ranges trigger an explicit discussion
39

of whether the discrepancy reﬂects (a) a real surprise in the data, (b) a methodology
problem, or (c) a corpus-construction artifact.
What the pre-speciﬁcation is, and is not.
The analysis plan containing H1–H5
and the rubric was written and ﬁxed before the scoring step of the static-six pipeline
was executed. It is deposited at (Khan, 2026a). We do not claim it is a timestampenforced pre-registration whose third-party record predates data collection: the corpus
was assembled before the plan was written, and the public OSF record postdates the
static-six scoring. The honest description of the static-six results is therefore plan-prespeciﬁed but conﬁrmatory-weakened: H4, the only hypothesis directly testable on the
static-six scale, is reported as a conﬁrmatory result with this caveat attached, and H1,
H3 are treated as bounded-but-provisional (Section 6.2).
We adopt this conservative
posture precisely because the alternative—asserting a pre-registration the timestamps
do not support—would be the exact failure mode the paper exists to measure. The six
manual-review dimensions deferred to v2.1 are, by contrast, governed by a pre-registration
that we commit to ﬁling on OSF, timestamp-enforced, before any of their scoring is run
(Khan, 2026d); v2.1 will report those six as fully conﬁrmatory only if that ﬁling in fact
precedes the scoring.
5.6
What the survey is not
We close the methodology section with three honest caveats.
First, the compliance survey does not establish that any speciﬁc published paper is
wrong, fraudulent, or even imprudent. It establishes that the published record does not
currently carry the per-prediction provenance that SR 11-7 and the AI Act expect of
deployed models. Academic papers are not deployed models; the gap between them is
meaningful but is also a transition path, not a verdict.
Second, the compliance threshold of 9 of 12 is a sharp cut. The unweighted sum-oftwelve-binaries scoring is a deliberate simpliﬁcation; an actual supervisory examination
would weight some dimensions more heavily than others (e.g., per-prediction logging
is more material than seed documentation). We use the unweighted sum because it is
transparent, replicable, and pre-speciﬁed; we report the per-dimension rates separately
so that readers can apply their own weighting.
Third, the corpus is not representative of all ML-in-ﬁnance work. It is representative of published, arXiv-indexed, ML-in-quantitative-ﬁnance work. Production model risk
teams at banks may have higher compliance standards than the published research record
reﬂects; conversely, the smaller and less-resourced practitioner community may be lower.
The survey is calibrated against the academic literature because that is the population
whose papers are read, cited, and used as templates for downstream work.

Scope of this version

6.8
Beyond the pre-speciﬁed priors
Two ﬁndings warrant discussion beyond the H1–H5 hypotheses.
Code-link rot. The mr-audit pipeline observed 8/80 = 10.0% of discovered code
URLs as fully unreachable (HTTP 404 or DNS failure) when followed in May 2026, and a
further 9/80 = 11.2% pointing to non-GitHub locations (typically a Zenodo, FAIR-data,
or institutional repository) that the static-scoring pipeline does not currently inspect.
Total non-cloneable: 17/80 = 21.2%. Across the Tier-1 hand-curated set the rot rate is
higher (4/14 = 28.6%), partly because Tier-1 includes older papers. Code-link rot is not
addressed by SR 11-7 or the AI Act explicitly, but it is a precondition for any audit-trail
standard: an audit trail that points to a 404 page is no audit trail. This is a ﬁnding
the survey was not designed to extract; we report it here so it appears in subsequent
meta-surveys.
Bimodal compliance. On the static six, the empirical distribution of total scores
is bimodal: among the 80 code-link-having papers, the modes are at static_total = 5
(20 papers) and at static_total = 1 (15 papers); the trough is at static_total = 2 (6
papers). The signal is that compliance is not a continuum of partial achievement: most
papers either ship a barely-runnable repository (mean static total ≤1) or a thoughtfullyengineered one (mean static total ≥4); few papers sit in the middle. This bimodality
suggests that the marginal cost of going from the basic mode (just commit the code) to
the engineered mode (commit code, document environment, seed, and persist artifacts)
is small, but is incurred all at once or not at all.

6.10
What the survey is
This survey is not a verdict on any speciﬁc paper. It is a sample-level measurement
of the gap between the per-prediction audit-trail standard imposed by SR 11-7 and the
AI Act, and the per-prediction provenance actually carried by the published machinelearning literature in quantitative ﬁnance. The result of that measurement, restricted
to the six dimensions that can be scored without a Tier-1 replication or a manual PDF
review, is that the average paper in our corpus satisﬁes 1.52 of 6 static dimensions,
equivalent to roughly 25% of the half-rubric standard, with a temporal-improvement rate
that would not reach the full standard before the year 2070. The remaining six dimensions
are reported in v2.1, with the rubric and pipeline released so any reader can run them
today.

Discussion
This section interprets the demonstration, identiﬁes ﬁve honest limitations, and outlines
the gap between what mr-audit v0.1.0 currently does and what a production-grade audittrail system in a regulated ﬁrm would need.
48

7.1
For practitioners deploying ML in regulated settings
The narrow practical claim is that an SR 11-7-shaped audit trail for an ML inference
pipeline does not require a commercial platform, a cloud subscription, or a centralized governance system. A one-line decorator and a context manager produce records that satisfy
the ﬁeld-level requirements of Table 2. The records are immutable (chain-tied), replayable
(veriﬁed to bit-identical match in our demonstration), portable (vendor-neutral storage),
and reviewer-friendly (regulator-export bundle veriﬁable with oﬀ-the-shelf tools). The
marginal engineering cost of doing this on top of an existing pipeline is roughly two days
of integration plus a per-prediction logging cost on the order of 10−2 seconds (matching
the throughput in Table 6).
A second practical takeaway is the value of recording the full state of a prediction
event, not just the prediction itself. Many ﬁrms today record only the prediction value
and a timestamp. The audit-trail value comes from the breadth of the record: when
an external reviewer asks “what code, what data, what hyperparameters, what library
versions, what random seed produced this number?”, the answer must be in the log
without re-running the pipeline. The mr-audit schema captures all of these by default;
the engineering investment is in the integration, not in the retention.
7.2
For the regulatory framework
The EU AI Act’s Article 12 / Article 19 are operational rules, not principles.
They
specify what must be in the log; they do not specify how the log should be implemented.
Our demonstration is intended as a partial answer to the latter question: in Python,
with ∼2,000 lines of code, MIT-licensed, on a single laptop. The implementation does
not require a cloud provider, a speciﬁc database vendor, or a speciﬁc ML framework.
The implication for ﬁrms that have not yet provisioned for AI Act compliance is that the
technical infrastructure is achievable with modest engineering eﬀort, and the cost driver
will be process and governance rather than tooling.
For SR 11-7, the parallel observation is that the model risk management lifecycle (development, validation, deployment, ongoing monitoring) is well-served by an audit trail
that captures every prediction’s full state. The current banking-industry norm of recording only inputs and outputs in a separate database, with model parameters versioned in
a separate model registry, requires correlation across multiple systems to reconstruct any
historical prediction—a recurring diﬃculty in the machine-learning model-management
literature (Schelter et al., 2018). A uniﬁed audit trail collapses this correlation eﬀort.
49

7.3
Pairing the package with the compliance gap
The two halves of this paper are not parallel contributions; they motivate one another.
The mr-audit package is a candidate answer to a question. The compliance survey is a
measurement of the size of the question.
The measurement says the gap is roughly an order of magnitude. On the six automatable rubric dimensions (Table 22), the average paper in our 185-paper corpus satisﬁes
1.52 of 6 dimensions; the average code-link-having paper satisﬁes 3.28 of 6. The full
audit-trail standard requires 12 of 12. Under the most charitable extrapolation (the six
unscored dimensions improve at the same rate as the six scored ones), the average paper
in the ﬁeld today satisﬁes roughly 2 × 1.52 = 3.04 of 12 dimensions. The SR 11-7 / AI
Act target is 12 of 12. The gap is 12 −3.04 = 8.96 dimensions, or 75% of the standard.
The same measurement establishes that compliance is achievable: three Tier-1 candidates (FinRL, FinGPT, the Grinsztajn tabular-benchmark) score the full 6 of 6 on static
dimensions. These three are not pristine bank-deployment pipelines; they are open-source
ML-ﬁnance research projects that satisfy the static half of the rubric because their authors chose to (a) publish runnable code, (b) document the environment, (c) commit data
references, and (d) persist model artifacts. The marginal cost of being in the top quintile
of compliance is, empirically, the cost of writing a requirements.txt, a Dockerfile,
and a README that points at the data. Nothing in the rubric is intellectually diﬃcult; the
gap is cultural and infrastructural.
mr-audit addresses the infrastructural half. The dimensions of the rubric most underserved by current culture (data lineage at 12.4%; per-prediction logging, drift, multiplecomparison correction at unknown but anecdotally near-zero rates) all have technical
analogs in the package: record_id hashes are content-addressed; Prediction.value is
logged per prediction by construction; the drift module addresses dim 9; the regulatorexport bundle addresses the integrity-of-attestation problem implicit in dim 5. The package is not the cultural ﬁx. It is the substrate that makes the cultural ﬁx cheap.
The Cleanlab pattern is the closest precedent: a paper that introduced a tool and, in
the same paper, measured the size of the problem the tool addressed. Northcutt et al.
(2021) did this for label noise; we attempt the same for audit-trail compliance. The lesson
from Cleanlab is that the tool gets cited when readers can quote the headline statistic
without re-running the analysis.
Our headline statistic: across 185 ML-quantitativeﬁnance papers from 1952–2025, the mean satisﬁes 25% of the static half of the SR 11-7
/ AI Act audit-trail standard (1.52 of 6 dimensions), with a temporal-improvement rate
that would not reach the full standard before 2070.
The package is one engineering
response. The measurement, we hope, is the more cited contribution.

7.4
Limitations
Five limitations of the present work deserve careful noting.
1. The demonstration is retrospective. The 6,825 records were written today against
a previously-ﬁt forecast panel.
We did not re-run the seven-model pipeline live and
write records in real time. The retrospective form is appropriate for showing that the
ﬁve use cases work end-to-end at realistic scale, but it does not establish that mr-audit
would handle a live high-throughput inference workload (e.g., the millions of predictions
per day that some bank ML pipelines generate) without re-engineering. The current
SQLite single-writer backend’s 22.6 records/s throughput is comfortable for a thousandprediction-per-day workﬂow but inadequate for a million-per-day workﬂow.
Higherthroughput storage backends (Parquet append shards, write-optimized databases) are
a planned 0.2.0 addition.
2. “Drift in 44 of 44 months” is partly a feature, partly a noise issue. The KS test
on a ﬁve-feature window over a multi-year volatility series will register drift in nearly
every month relative to a ﬁxed early baseline, because realized-volatility distributions
shift across regimes.
This is the intended behavior for SR 11-7 ğV monitoring (you
want the model risk team to be told when input distributions move) but it is also a noisy
signal: a team that is told “drift detected” every month will calibrate a high tolerance and
miss the drift instances that matter. A production deployment would likely use a rolling
baseline (e.g., the prior 90 days) rather than a ﬁxed early baseline, and would deﬁne
drift relative to the magnitude observed during model development. Our demonstration
uses the simplest baseline construction for clarity; the package does not preclude more
sophisticated baselines.
3. Integrity protection is hash-chain, not signed. Tampering with the chain is detectable by verify_chain, but the chain itself is not cryptographically signed by an
external authority. A suﬃciently-sophisticated adversary with write access to the log ﬁle
could rewrite the entire chain forward from the point of tampering, producing a selfconsistent but altered chain. Defences against this attack require external attestation:
periodic publication of the latest record_id to a trusted external store (an internal audit ledger, a public blockchain, or a regulator-controlled repository), or signing of the
chain by a hardware security module. mr-audit does not provide either; it provides the
substrate on which a ﬁrm can layer its preferred attestation strategy.
4.
Feature
values
are
stored
as
summaries
by
default.
The
default
feature_values_summary captures per-feature numeric values at prediction time. Capturing raw input vectors (e.g., 1-million-dimensional NLP embeddings) would inﬂate
log sizes intolerably and may capture sensitive customer data.
The current default
is conservative (small log size, no PII risk) but loses information that a more granular drift analysis would need.
Firms that need raw-vector logging can conﬁgure
51

summarize_features=False; the privacy and storage trade-oﬀs are theirs to manage.
5. The package targets Python ML pipelines. A bank using R, Java, MATLAB, or
a vendor-supplied scoring engine cannot use mr-audit directly. The schema (Table 5)
is language-neutral (canonical JSON), so a port to other languages is straightforward in
principle, but no port currently exists.
7.5
What would change our view
The paper makes empirical and methodological claims. We state explicitly what would
force us to revise each.
Claim: mr-audit’s schema satisﬁes SR 11-7, EU AI Act Articles 12 and 19, NIST
AI 100-1, and ISO/IEC 42001 Clauses 6.1.2 / 7.5 / 9.1 simultaneously. We would revise
if (a) a supervisory enforcement action against a deployed mr-audit-instrumented system
ﬁnds the trail insuﬃcient for one of the four frameworks, identifying the speciﬁc clause
not satisﬁed; or (b) a peer-reviewed compliance analysis identiﬁes a regulatory clause we
have mis-mapped in Table 3. Either revision is incremental, the gap can be added to the
schema in a backward-compatible way.
Claim: hash-chain integrity detects single-record corruption, chain-link tampering,
and middle-record deletion (Table 16 scenarios A, B, D). We would revise if an independent reproduction of the adversarial experiment on a diﬀerent log (diﬀerent size, diﬀerent
model, diﬀerent storage backend) yields a non-detection in any of those three scenarios.
This is unlikely given the mathematical structure of SHA-256, but the empirical claim is
sample-conditional and should be tested against deployment-realistic logs.
Claim: tail truncation is undetectable by chain veriﬁcation alone (Table 16 scenario
C). We would revise if a method is identiﬁed that detects tail truncation from the log alone
without external anchoring. We do not believe such a method exists for a self-consistent
chain, but we would adopt it if it is.
Claim: input-vs-output drift comparison enables a covariate-vs-concept-drift distinction (Table 11). We would revise if a published deployment ﬁnds that the four-quadrant
framework misclassiﬁes a real drift episode, identifying the case and the misclassiﬁcation
mechanism. The framework is well-established in model-risk practice (e.g., Massey, 1951;
Karakoulas, 2004); the contribution here is the pairing of the two detectors in one package
and the audit-log substrate for retrospective evaluation.
Claim: low-friction integration cost.
We would revise if a deployed pipeline ﬁnds
the integration cost materially higher than “one decorator plus one context manager”
(the demonstrated cost on the Khan 2026 panel).
Speciﬁcally: if a non-trivial bank
ML pipeline (say, a credit-scoring engine running 100k predictions per day with multimodel ensembling and human-in-the-loop review) requires more than one engineer-week
to integrate, we would document the additional integration patterns required and revise
52

the “low-friction” claim accordingly.
The falsiﬁcation criteria are not asks for permission; they are commitments to update
when evidence arrives. We invite that evidence.
7.6
Roadmap beyond v0.1.0
The v0.1.0 release ships an output-drift module (drift/output.py), symmetric to the
input-drift module: KS two-sample plus PSI applied to Prediction.value across windows. Three further additions are planned for v0.2.0 and will not change the audit-record
schema, so logs written under the current version remain readable:
1. Higher-throughput backend. Parquet-append shard backend with optional compaction, targeting 1,000+ records/s on commodity hardware.
2. External attestation hooks. Plugin interface for periodically publishing the latest
record_id to an external trusted store (Sigstore, an internal HSM, or a blockchain
anchor) so that the chain’s integrity can be re-veriﬁed by an independent party at
later dates.
3. Model artifact hashing.
Optional automatic SHA-256 of the underlying model
object (a pickled scikit-learn estimator or a PyTorch state-dict) to populate
model.model_artifact_hash.
