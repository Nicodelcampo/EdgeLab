Electronic Limit Order Book and Order Submission Choice Around 
Macroeconomic News  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Grigori Erenburg 
Dennis Lasser** 
 
 
 
 
Abstract 
 
We investigate the dynamics of the displayed part of the Island ECN limit order book for the 
equity index-linked securities market around macroeconomic news releases. Our results indicate 
that the quality of the electronic market, measured by spread and depth, deteriorates during the 
releases. Furthermore, they show that traders appear to switch to more aggressive strategies as 
early as three minutes before an announcement and are most aggressive within the first minute 
after the announcement. Also, there is a noticeable difference in the response of the limit order 
book to the macroeconomic news during pre-market hours relative to regular trading hours.

1. Introduction 
As electronic trading systems continue to gain market share worldwide, an understanding 
of their market structure becomes important. One of the controversial issues in this area is 
whether electronic markets can sustain a supply of liquidity in the absence of market makers 
with affirmative obligations. In the absence of designated intermediaries, limit orders are the 
only source of liquidity. But a limit order is subject to adverse selection risk due to its option-like 
properties. As a result, during periods of high uncertainty driven by the arrival of a large amount 
of new information, liquidity in electronic markets can dry up if traders switch from limit to 
market orders.  
Before the mergers with Instinet Electronic Communication Network (ECN) and Nasdaq, 
Island Electronic Communication Network (Island ECN) was one of the largest electronic 
markets in the U.S. At their discretion, traders in the Island ECN provided all the liquidity in the 
electronic book through limit orders.  
In this paper, we examine the effect of scheduled macroeconomic news releases on the 
dynamics of the displayed electronic limit order book by exploring the unique market structure 
of the Island ECN. To our knowledge, this is the first study that analyzes the dynamics of a 
purely electronic market before and after the macroeconomic announcements in the U.S. The 
detailed data on Island ECN enables us to analyze the dynamics of quoted spread, depth at and 
away from the best quotes, and the order submission behavior of the traders.  Furthermore, the 
extended trading hours of the Island ECN allow us to study market reaction under different 
trading regimes. 
We concentrate our analysis on the impact of macro news on an index-linked security, 
the Nasdaq-100 Index Trust Shares. This work contrasts with other studies that investigate the

state of the limit order book during public news releases in equity markets and focus primarily on 
the effect of firm-specific news on the market. For example, Ranaldo (2008) studies the reaction 
of the limit order book to firm-specific news on the Paris Bourse. Kavajecz (1999) investigates 
the state of the NYSE limit order book for the cross-section of stocks during earnings 
announcements and one type of macroeconomic announcement. The nature of adverse selection 
in the market for an index-linked “basket” security around macroeconomic announcements is 
different from that in the market for a single stock around firm-specific news. The major source 
of adverse selection during a firm-specific public announcement is the asymmetric information 
that arises due to the presence of private knowledge about the firm’s cash flows (see Green 
(2004)). In the market for a basket security, the losses incurred by the uninformed liquidity 
traders when trading with informed traders should be lower than in the market for an individual 
security (see Subrahmanyam (1991)). Further, during rapid, large price movements due to the 
arrival of new public information, limit order submitters risk being “picked-off” even in the 
absence of privately informed investors. Studies by Biais, Glosten, and Spatt (2005), Copeland 
and Galai (1983), Foucault, Roël, and Sandås (2003), and Fahlenbrach and Sandås (2003)) 
discuss this type of risk.  
We note that our analysis relates to the displayed part of the Island’s limit order book.  
Certain data limitations make it difficult to draw any inferences about the hidden depth in the 
book. Yet we believe that the analysis of the displayed part of the limit order book is important 
because the displayed order book is what traders actually see when they make their order 
submission decisions. 
The paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we formalize the hypotheses. Section 3 
describes the data and the sample. In section 4 we report our results. Section 5 concludes.

2. Hypotheses  
Theoretical and empirical studies of prescheduled anticipated public announcements 
indicate that market dynamics change both before and after the announcement (see, for example, 
Kim and Verrecchia (1991), Graham, Koski, and Loewenstein (2006)). In particular, 
macroeconomic news releases are accompanied by rise in price volatility (Ederington and Lee 
(1993), Erenburg, Kurov, and Lasser (2006)), fast price changes, and increase in spreads 
(Fleming and Remolona (1999), Frino and Hill (2001)).  
To test the effect of macro news on limit order book in the market for Nasdaq-100 Index 
Trust Shares we formalize two hypotheses:  
H1: The displayed spread increases around the time of macroeconomic news releases.  
H2: The displayed depth decreases around the time of macroeconomic news releases.  
The dynamics of limit order book, including changes in spreads and depth, are affected 
by traders’ order submission behavior, which in turn is determined by price dynamics and the 
state of the limit order book. Current studies establish a connection between traders’ order 
choice, price dynamics, and a state of the limit order book. For example, Bae, Jang, and Park 
(2003) find that traders in the NYSE place more limit orders when they expect high transitory 
volatility and when the spread is large. Biais, Hillion, and Spatt (1995) find that traders in the 
Paris Bourse submit more limit orders when order book is thin and bid-ask spread is large. 
Goldstein and Kavajecz (2004) document dramatic changes in limit order submission behavior of 
NYSE traders during extreme market movements.  
The basic dilemma for traders is the choice between submitting limit orders rather than 
market orders. In reality, traders have a broader choice of submission strategies varying in degree

of aggressiveness with which traders pursue the execution of an order. For example, a limit order 
submitted with a limit price that improves on the current best bid or offer price is more 
aggressive than a limit order submitted away from the best bid-ask price, because it has much 
shorter waiting time and higher chances of being executed. According to Foucault’s (1999) 
dynamic equilibrium model, higher volatility forces market participants to submit less aggressive 
orders. The spread and the transaction costs for market orders increase. Thus, traders prefer to 
submit limit orders. Therefore, higher volatility results in a higher proportion of limit orders.  
The releases of macroeconomic news accompanied by fast changes in price and price 
volatility will affect traders’ submission behavior. Hence, we state our third hypothesis: 
H3: Traders follow less aggressive submission strategies around the time of macroeconomic 
news releases.  
Studies that investigate the performance of an electronic limit order book often explore 
its side-by-side operation with officially designated human market makers. In some 
environments traditional markets with officially designated market makers are more resistant to 
informational shock. For example, Goldstein and Kavajecz (2004) show that during the extreme 
movements of the U.S. market in October 1997, the costs of providing liquidity through the 
electronic limit order book became so high that traders preferred to trade on the NYSE floor 
rather than through the SuperDot electronic limit order book.   
 In other environments, the existence of privileged traders such as market makers can 
increase the adverse selection costs in the market during the public news releases and discourage 
regular traders from trading right after news releases. Barclay, Hendershott, and McCormick 
(2003) show that Nasdaq traders are more likely to route orders to an ECN when there are high 
levels of information asymmetry, trading activity, and return volatility.

Island ESN operated under two trading regimes. During the regular market hours, the

H4: The reaction of the limit order book to the macroeconomic news shocks is most pronounced 
during the pre-market hours when the electronic market is the primary trading venue and the 
overall market is thin. 
 
3. Sample and data 
3.1. Island ECN 
We obtain order data from the Island ECN for the period from January 1, 2002 to 
September 22, 2002. The data after September 22nd, 2002 was unavailable for us since, in 
response to a regulatory constraint, Island stopped displaying its limit order book for selected 
securities starting from September 23rd, 2002. The sample covers a total of 181 trading days.  
The Island ECN was an electronic matching system that accepted, matched, and 
cancelled orders. In 2002, Island was the most actively traded ECN with considerable market 
share in Nasdaq traded securities. According to the Island’s press release on May 15, 2002, the 
Island ECN had 11.2% of the Nasdaq share volume and 21.2% of the Nasdaq trade volume. 
Island also traded Amex and NYSE listed securities, as well as single stock futures. The ECN 
started operating in 1997 and was one of the first equity markets to provide a display of the limit

3.2. Procedure of constructing limit order book 
 Island’s historical order data are available in text files formatted according to Island’s 
proprietary ITCH data feed specification and are identical to the real-time data supplied to 
traders subscribing to Island. ITCH daily files comprise Add Orders, Modify Orders and Trade 
type messages that provide information about all submitted limit orders. An Add Order message 
describes a new order that has been accepted by the system. This message contains a time stamp, 
information about the buy-sell direction of the order, the number of shares added to the book, the 
stock symbol, and the unique reference number for this order.  Modify Order messages include 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=962255

 
 
 
7
Order Executed and Order Cancel messages and signal any changes in orders that were 
previously added to the book. An Order Executed message is issued if either a partial or a 
complete order is executed. It contains a time stamp, the reference number of the order that was 
executed (previously reported in the Add Order message), and the number of shares executed. 
Similarly, an Order Cancel message contains a time stamp, the reference number of the order and 
the number of shares being cancelled. An Add Order message is never sent for non-displayed 
orders. To indicate the execution of non-displayed orders, Island’s trading system generates a 
Trade message. If a hidden order is executed in multiple parts, then the system issues several 
Trade messages. Each Trade message contains a time stamp, the reference number of the 
executed order, the buy-sell direction of the executed order, the incremental number of shares 
executed, the stock symbol, and the price.2   
As noted above, the investor who submits the order can imitate a market order by 
submitting a limit order with a price that ensures its crossing with another order in the book. 
However, in the presence of hidden orders, the investor cannot always correctly determine what 
order submission price will make a limit order and what order price will make a market order.  
Thus, in our analysis we include only “obvious market orders,” i.e., those orders that are 
identified by an Order Executed Message. 
The ITCH database allows us to completely reconstruct Island’s displayed limit order 
book at any point in time during the trading session. Using ITCH data files, we can track each 
displayed order and its modifications from the moment it is submitted through the time when it is 
either executed or cancelled.

3.3. Nasdaq-100 Trust Shares 
We focus our analysis on the market for the Nasdaq-100 Trust Shares. Unofficially, 
Nasdaq-100 Trust Shares are called “Cubes” due to their ticker symbol (QQQ).3 This equity

3.4. Macroeconomic announcements 
The time of the news releases varies across types of macroeconomic announcements, but 
it is the same for a specific type of announcement. We obtain the dates and times of news 
releases from the database on macroeconomic announcements and forecasts collected by Money 
Market Services (MMS) International, a subsidiary of Informa Financial Group. All of the 
announcements in our sample are issued on a monthly basis. Since the most important news is 
released at either 8:30 a.m. or 10:00 a.m., we concentrate our analysis on market dynamics 
around these times.

In contrast to human-intermediated markets with designated market makers such as the

4.3. Book’s depth 
In addition to the quoted spreads, another important liquidity dimension is the level of 
depth at and away from the inside quotes. Kavajecz (1999) shows in his study that both NYSE 
specialists and limit order traders reduce depths during four announcements made by the Federal 
Open Market Committee concerning the changes in the Fed Funds rate. However, the NYSE is a 
market system that operates differently from a pure electronic limit order book. The presence of 
specialists who might be more informed than other traders during times of high information 
uncertainty can adversely affect the traders’ willingness to provide liquidity at these times.  
To evaluate the changes in displayed depth, we follow Goldstein and Kavajecz (2004). 
We measure the cumulative displayed depth in the limit order book at three levels: at the quotes 
(best bid and best offer within the book), within one cent away from the quotes, and in the rest of 
the limit order book. This measure of depth controls for changes in the inside spread in contrast 
to another commonly used measure, the cumulative depth relative to the mid-quote. For example, 
when the spreads widen, our measure of depth will be higher than the measure of depth relative 
to the midquote. 
Figure 3 shows the cumulative displayed depth around 8:30 a.m. and 10:00 a.m. on 
announcement days. Depth decreases considerably within four minutes before 10:00 a.m. 
announcements and rises to the original levels within ten minutes after the announcements. The 
changes in depth level around 8:30 a.m. announcements are not as dramatic, even though the

4.5. Pre-market hours vs. regular trading hours 
The results in our previous sections indicate a substantial difference in the reaction of the 
limit order book to the news released at 8:30 a.m. relative to 10:00 a.m. Our last hypothesis, 
regarding the differences in book reaction to the news under different trading sessions, is 
supported by the results for the spreads in Table 2, but not by the results for depth in Table 3.

pre-market hours, but the depth dynamics is more pronounced during the news released during 
market hours. However, we note that the types of announcements released during 8:30 a.m. and

4.6. Hidden orders 
Electronic markets allow participants to submit “iceberg,” or partially displayed limit 
orders. For these orders, the traders see price and only a portion of the order volume. Island ECN 
allowed the subscribers to submit “non-displayed” orders that were completely hidden from 
traders and other market participants. The available data only includes information about the 
executions of non-displayed orders from the book and does not contain any information about 
hidden orders that were never executed. The data also do not identify what type of incoming 
order triggered the transaction. As a result, we lack information on hidden order submissions and 
cannot observe the complete order submission choice. However, we can provide an analysis of 
the executed non-marketable orders. As opposed to the marketable limit orders designed to 
replicate market orders and priced to execute immediately, non-marketable orders entered the 
book before being executed. A trade message from ITCH files identifies what type of nonmarketable order has been executed – displayed or non-displayed. The following statistics are 
reported for the non-marketable limit orders. 
For the Nasdaq 100 Tracking Stock, the average proportion of executed non-marketable 
hidden orders in all executions is 50% and 70% around 8:30 a.m. and 10:00 a.m., respectfully. 
Although we believe that this study provides an important contribution to understanding the 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=962255

 
 
 
20
performance of electronic markets, it is important to discuss the role of hidden orders in Island 
ECN. 
A major advantage of the hidden order is that it eliminates the risk of exposure in the 
presence of high information asymmetry. In the presence of high information asymmetry, traders 
prefer to submit more of their limit orders in the non-displayed format to avoid the premature 
exposure of their intent. However, non-displayed orders have lower priority than regular 
displayed orders and have to be priced more aggressively. As a result, traders have to weigh the 
advantage of non-displaying their order with the necessity to price these orders more 
aggressively during announcements. Furthermore, the risk that traders face during 
macroeconomic announcements is mostly related to the quick change in the market valuation, 
and this type of risk is similar for the submitters of both hidden limit orders and regular visible 
limit orders. 
For single stocks, according to Hasbrouck and Saar (2004), executed hidden orders 
account for about 12% of all order executions and executed shares. During regular trading hours 
in the market for the Nasdaq 100 Index Tracking Stock (QQQ) executed hidden orders make up 
to 80% of the volume (number of shares), and up to 70% of the number of all trades.  
The high proportion of hidden orders in the market for the Nasdaq 100 Index Tracking 
Stock seems to contradict the properties of the basket security described by Subrahmanyam 
(1991). He argues that most of the trading in basket securities is generated by liquidity traders 
rather than by informed traders. Consequently, if the only use of a hidden order is to decrease the 
premature exposure of an informed order, it is not clear why the proportion of hidden orders in 
the Nasdaq-100 Index Tracking Stock is so much higher than the proportion of hidden orders in 
the market for individual stocks.  
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=962255

 
 
 
21
 It seems that the amount of trading activity is at least as important in explaining the 
usage of hidden orders as the proportion of informed trading. For example, the hidden order 
activity is also high for other types of actively traded market index linked securities such as S&P 
Depositary Receipts (SPY) tracking S&P 500 market index or Diamonds Trust Series 1 (DIA) 
security that tracks DJIA index. Furthermore, in the market for Cubes, the proportion of executed 
hidden orders is extremely high only during regular market hours when trading activity is also 
high. Before 8:00 a.m. and after 4:15 p.m. executed limit orders make only 10-20% of the 
volume. 
The Island’s rebate structure provides an incentive for the traders to submit hidden 
orders. As described previously, incoming orders that are executed immediately are charged 0.19 
cents per share, but orders added to the book receive a rebate of 0.11 cents per share at execution 
as a compensation for providing liquidity. Traders who want fast execution but prefer to avoid 
charges for immediate execution try to submit hidden orders very close to the opposite side of 
the book, so that the order will be executed once the new aggressive order is submitted from the 
opposite side. This way, the submitter of a hidden order obtains fairly fast execution and earns 
the rebate, while a submitter of a visible order bears the risk of accidentally hitting the hidden 
order from the other side and paying a fee.8 
Based on the discussion above, it does not appear that the unavailability of the data on 
hidden orders can bias the results of our study in any particular direction. But, it is clear that 
hidden orders on Island ECN play a very large role in the market for actively traded equity-index 
linked securities.

One may argue that the dataset used in the study has several potential drawbacks. We do 
not believe that these data limitations undermine the validity of our results or conclusions. 
However, we discuss these limitations below and leave it up to reader to make a final judgment. 
One concern may be related to the short sample period. Our sample period covers less than nine 
months, which limits the number of announcements to less than ten for each type of 
macroeconomic announcement. However, the reader should note that our analysis includes 16 
different types of announcements (six made at 10:00 a.m. and ten made at 8:30 a.m.). Except for 
the preliminary analysis, where we estimate the individual effect of each type of announcement 
on price volatility, in the rest of the paper we test the average effect of any macroeconomic news 
released at 8:30 a.m. and at 10:00 a.m. Thus, we have a sufficiently large number of days when 
at least one announcement has been made and when no major announcement has been made.9  
Another concern may be that the market experienced a sharp downfall during the sample 
period, and hence our results may not be generalized for the expanding or neutral markets. 
However, we do not analyze the direction of the impact of market news on the market. Instead, 
we make a comparison between the market dynamics on the days when the news is released and 
the days when no news is released.  
The lack of data on non-displayed orders submissions limits the scope of our analysis.  
However, as discussed in the previous section, it does not appear that this would bias our 
conclusions in any particular direction. Traders in the electronic market infer all the information 
from the displayed part of the limit order book. Therefore, by investigating the dynamics of the 
displayed part of the limit order book, we gain insight into the traders’ decision-making process.

5. Conclusion  
We investigate the dynamics of the Island ECN electronic limit order book during the 
releases of macroeconomic news in the market for the Nasdaq 100 equity-index tracking stock. 
The results of the analysis demonstrate deterioration in limit order book quality as measured by 
spreads and full depth in response to a macroeconomic announcement.  
The reaction of the limit order book to the announcements differs across trading sessions. 
During the pre-market hours, the increase in displayed spreads captures most of the reaction to 
the macroeconomic news. But during regular market hours, we find a decrease in visible depth 
and an increase in the limit order book spreads in reaction to a news release. 
 The results also suggest an increase in the aggressiveness of displayed orders submission 
during the macroeconomic news releases. The major change in traders’ behavior is observed in 
the first minutes after the announcement. We observe the peak in order aggressiveness in the first 
minute after the announcement. The effect from announcement lasts for another four to five 
minutes. Overall, our results indicate that around macroeconomic news releases, traders prefer to 
cancel fewer orders and submit relatively more market and aggressively priced limit orders. The 
changes in order submission dynamics around macroeconomic announcements are similar during 
different trading periods. 
Our results show that while the quality of the limit order book deteriorates during the 
macroeconomic news shocks, electronic markets are not only a viable competitor to the 
traditional human-mediated markets, but can stand on their own in the absence of traditional 
markets.
