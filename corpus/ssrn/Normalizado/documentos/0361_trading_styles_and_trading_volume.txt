Trading under pressure: Environmental compliance and trading 
behavior in the EU Emissions Trading Scheme 
ABSTRACT 
Regulated firms under the EU Emissions Trading Scheme (EU ETS) face annually a 
strict deadline for buying any lacking emissions allowances and attain environmental 
compliance. By employing a unique dataset that covers all firm-level carbon allowance 
transactions during Phase I and II of the scheme, in this article we investigate how this 
annual compliance pressure impacts their trading behavior. We find that firms in both 
EU ETS phases engaged more often in emissions allowances trading as the compliance 
deadline was approaching. Specifically, the frequency of corporate transactions was 
increased by 0.023 in Phase I and 0.098 in Phase II, respectively. Also, while in both 
phases they were generally following a “contrarian-type” of trading strategy, they were 
increasingly engaging in “momentum-type” trades towards the compliance deadline in 
Phase II with the odds ratio of “momentum-type” trades was decreased by 0.0003. We 
explain these findings based on the specific circumstances surrounding each EU ETS 
phase under scrutiny, discuss how this trading behavior may potentially jeopardize the 
scheme in achieving its objective and provide relevant policy recommendations.

Carbon trading; Compliance pressure; Contrarian-type; Emission allowance;

1. Introduction 
Extensive evidence suggests that when under pressure from “stress-inducing” events 
like those related to specific weather or environmental changes[1-3], investors’ trading 
behavior would be affected, particularly in terms of the frequency at which they trade 
and the trading strategy they follow. In this sense, compliance, as one of the most 
notable stress-inducing events in the cap-and-trade carbon markets[4,5], will 
significantly influence firm-level trading behavior[6,7]. Yet, how annual compliance 
affect the participants’ trading behavior of the European Union Emissions Trading 
Scheme (EU ETS) remains unknown, as the related studies on the impact of compliance 
on trading behavior are generally rendered from a macro perspective[6,7]. In this article, 
we empirically examine how compliance pressure affects firm-level trading behavior 
in the EU ETS, which is the oldest carbon market in the world [8], and prior to China 
adopting a national cap-and-trade approach to reduce their CO2 emissions, the 
largest[9]. By doing so, this work offers a theoretical basis for carbon market 
regulations and potential policy adjustments, thereby facilitating the achievement of the 
objective of cap-and-trade carbon markets across the globe, that is, to reduce 
greenhouse (GHG) emissions (mainly carbon dioxide, CO2) at the lowest possible cost 
[10-12]. 
 
Rather than adopting a command-and-control approach to motivate these investments 
[13], in 2005 Europe opted to establish the EU ETS, a cap-and-trade system to reduce 
CO2 emission through a market-based mechanism [14]. The EU ETS’s participants are 
provided with an annual cap on their GHG emissions; this comes in the form of carbon 
emissions allowances, with each providing the right to emit one ton of CO2 [11]. In 
contrast to “traditional” markets, in which speculators, for example, buy and sell

securities in their attempt to maximize their returns, participants in the EU ETS engage 
in emissions allowances trading to attain environmental compliance. To achieve this, 
each firm has until the end of March each year to submit to the relevant authorities an 
externally verified report for the volume of their CO2 emissions over the previous 
calendar year, and until the end of April, to surrender the corresponding number of 
allowances for cancellation. Failure to do so results in a penalty of €100 per ton of CO2 
[13], as well as the obligation to surrender the lacking allowances in the next 
compliance year [15]. This makes the EU ETS a “periodically-stressed” market; the 
firms lacking allowances face annually a strict deadline to buy the necessary allowances 
they require to become compliant, while the firms with surplus allowances have to 
decide either to sell these allowances within the compliance deadline, or from 2008 
onwards (Phase II), to bank them for future compliance. 
 
Reducing GHG emissions and tackling climate change requires, among others, firms 
operating in emissions-intensive sectors, such as energy, aviation, and shipping to name 
a few, to invest into cleaner technologies for the manufacturing of their products or the 
delivery of their services. The mechanism of the EU ETS facilitates such investments. 
If a firm decides to invest into cleaner technologies, and therefore emits less CO2 than 
its annual cap, the surplus allowances can be sold to partly finance the firm’s investment 
on cleaner technologies. In contrast, if a firm wishes to emit more CO2 than its annual 
cap, it needs to purchase additional allowances to avoid the strict penalties. In this 
manner, the necessary investments into cleaner technologies are initiated by those firms 
facing comparably lower emissions abatement costs, providing flexibility to those firms 
facing higher abatement costs to postpone such investments, with the expectation that 
the cost of cleaner technologies they will need to adopt will be reduced in the future.

Through this market mechanism, economists argue, the reduction of GHG emissions 
will be achieved at the lowest possible cost[12]. 
 
Motivated by the discussion on the influence of “stress-inducing events” on trading 
behavior[1-3], we empirically investigate the impact of compliance pressure on the 
firm-level trading behavior of the EU ETS’s participating firms by focusing on two 
dimensions, that is, trading frequency and trading strategy. Following Boolell-Gunesh 
et al. [16], here we define the trading frequency to be the time interval between a 
transaction and its immediate previous one from the same firm. For the trading strategy, 
motivated by the financial literature[17-19], we consider two trading strategies, i.e., 
“momentum-type” and “contrarian-type”. A “momentum-type” strategy is that a firm 
buys allowances at a price higher than its immediate previous purchase or sells 
allowances at a price lower than its immediate previous sale. A “contrarian-type” 
strategy is that a firm purchases allowances at a price lower than its immediate previous 
purchase or sells allowances at a price higher than its immediate previous sale. 
 
Our analysis is based on a unique firm-level transaction dataset that covers carbon 
allowances trading between all non-financial institutions those hold Operator Holding 
Accounts (OHAs), which allows us to filter out transactions by speculators who are not 
subject to compliance pressures and to include only those transactions conducted by the 
entities under the jurisdiction of the EU ETS. Our unique dataset spans from 2005 to 
2012, that is, the complete Phase I and II of the EU ETS. Here we do not include the 
firm-level transactions of Phase III (i.e., 2013 to 2020) and Phase IV (2021 onwards), 
because the transaction data of Phase III is not available for downloading till 2024. In 
addition, several notable events like the Great Lockdown (including oil price collapse,

Methodologically, the impact of compliance pressure on the trading behavior of firms 
falling under the provisions of the cap-and-trade scheme is empirically examined using 
the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model and the Generalized Linear Model (GLM). 
Since trading frequency is a variable with continuous values, we adopt the OLS model 
is employed to examine the influence of compliance on trading frequency [21]. The 
effect of compliance pressure on trading strategy is examined via the GLM model, 
which is particularly applicable for categorical variables [22]. 
 
The remainder of this article proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides a literature review. 
Section 3 introduces the econometric models. Section 4 describes the data. Section 5 
presents the findings. Section 6 concludes and offers implications. 
2. Literature review 
2.1. Compliance and carbon trading 
Annual compliance is perhaps one of the most important events in carbon markets; it 
can be highly costly to the participating firms of these markets[4,5]. Compliance not 
only promotes the fulfillment of the carbon markets’ primary objective, that is, to 
reduce GHG emissions at the lowest possible cost via market mechanism [10,11], but 
also affects a wide range of activities like energy usage [23], stock investment [24], and 
even foreign direct investment[25,26].

Aside from imparting influences on the aforementioned activities, compliance may also 
affect the carbon allowances trading in carbon markets. Theoretically, radical changes 
in asset prices are usually accompanied with dramatic fluctuations in trading volumes 
[27]. Accordingly, compliance, which is commonly believed to be the bane of causing 
periodically structural breaks in carbon prices [28], would affect trading behavior of the 
participants of such cap-and-trade carbon markets. The present evidence suggests that 
in the EU ETS, verified emissions announcements and allowances’ prices could 
promote the volume of the transactions aimed at fulfilling compliance obligations 
(namely compliance trading)[6,14,29]. Moreover, resulting from increased market 
activities [30] and reduction of total compliance trading [7] in Phase II of the EU ETS, 
such a pattern is more noticeable in Phase I [6]. However, the existing literature does 
not empirically examine the impact of compliance on the firm-level trading behavior. 
Since non-compliance trading is related to compliance trading [7], compliance pressure 
would affect both types of allowances trading behavior; this deserves further empirical 
exploration. 
 
In sum, despite the significance of compliance being widely recognized in the carbon 
market literature, there is no empirical evidence concerning how compliance, as one of 
the most notable stress-inducing events in the cap-and-trade carbon markets, influences 
the trading frequency of individual firms. 
2.2. Trading strategy in buying and selling 
Trading strategies could be commonly classified into momentum- and contrarian-type. 
Ever since the first documentation in stock markets [31], the trace of momentum- and 
contrarian-type trading is reported in financial markets like stock markets [32], mutual 
funds markets [33], cryptocurrency markets [34], and energy and commodity markets

[35]. The presence of such trading strategies is also identified in carbon markets [36]. 
Both trading strategies have been extensively practiced by individuals [37], institutions 
[37,38], and even algorithmic traders [39]. 
 
In spite of a rich body of trading strategy literature concentrates on identifying these 
strategies[37,38,40], estimating and explaining their profitability [40], only limited 
studies tend to examine trading behavior of buy and sell separately for uncovering the 
differentiated trading strategies in different types of trading behaviors. Through 
decomposing stock market investments into short-term investments and intermediate- 
and long-term investments, Doan et al. [41] suggest that contrarian strategies dominate 
in short-term investments but momentum strategies rule intermediate- and long-term 
investments. Based on an account-level trading dataset, Ng and Wu [42] observe that 
Chinese institutions are momentum traders and less wealthy Chinese individuals are 
contrarian traders, while the wealthiest Chinese individuals behave like institutions in 
buying but behave like less wealthy individuals in selling. Focusing on individual-level 
stock trading, Duxbury and Yao [43] suggest that Chinese individuals are momentum 
traders when buying stocks but become contrarian traders when selling stocks. Besides, 
Da Dalt et al. [44] find that Finnish citizens are more likely to be momentum traders 
when buying funds than equities. Notably, these studies imply that a given investor 
would employ the same trading strategy to a certain type of trading behavior, i.e., buy 
and sell. However, this assumption has never been tested, and the existing side evidence 
suggest that this might not the case. For instance, trading decisions are related to assets 
past returns [45], which can be highly inconsistent [32] and volatile [46], thus might 
lead to the potential radical changes in trading strategy. This urges further investigation.

4. Data description 
4.1. Data aggregation 
The data collection and processing procedure is the same one followed in our previous 
work [47], and non-emitting firms have been excluded since their trading objectives are 
different from that of emitters and the market [48]. Briefly, the complete Community 
Independent Transaction Log (CITL) for Phase I of the EU ETS and the EU Transaction 
Log (EUTL) for Phase II of the scheme were downloaded from the EU Commission’s 
website. We focus only on firms with an OHA, to ensure the dataset excluded the 
transactions from speculators that are free from compliance pressure and included only 
transactions from the firms regulated under the EU ETS. Considering the data 
availability and the normal operation of the EU ETS, the trading data from Phase III 
and beyond is not included in our work, as previously mentioned. Overall, our unique 
dataset consists of 724,790 firm-level transactions in all three types of carbon 
allowances available for trading in the EU ETS, namely, European Union Allowances 
(EUAs), Emissions Reduction Units (ERUs), and Certified Emissions Reductions 
(CERs), over the period from February 2005 to June 2012, i.e., the complete Phase I 
and II. Naturally, the total number of surrendered allowances in each phase under 
scrutiny equals the sum of the allocated, traded, and canceled allowances.

5. Empirical findings 
5.1. Impact on trading frequency 
The OLS regression results for the impact of compliance pressure on the trading 
frequency of EU ETS participants in Phase I and II of the scheme are presented in Table 
2. The observation from Table 2 indicates a significant negative relationship between 
trading frequency and time to compliance, suggesting that firms trade more frequently 
as the compliance deadline approaches. However, this relationship is influenced by the 
number of allowances a firm already holds for compliance and the prices of emissions 
allowances. Both factors demonstrate a significant negative relationship with trading 
frequency. These empirical outcomes suggest that the firms with a larger number of 
allowances relative to their annual compliance requirements feel less pressure from the 
advancing deadline, and subsequently they traded less frequently. Besides, increases in 
allowance prices compared to previous transactions also reduce trading frequency.

6. Discussion and conclusion 
The regulated firms under the EU ETS face annually a strict deadline for buying any 
lacking emissions allowances to attain their environmental compliance. By employing 
a unique firm-level transaction dataset that covers all allowances trading between nonfinancial participants of the EU ETS during Phase I and II, here we investigate how this 
annual compliance pressure impacts their trading behavior though the OLS and GLM 
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5059527
Preprint not peer reviewed

23 
models. We observe that the participating firms in both EU ETS phases engaged more 
often in carbon allowances trading as the compliance deadline was approaching. Also, 
while in both phases they generally followed a “contrarian-type” of trading strategy, 
they were increasingly engaging in “momentum-type” trades towards the compliance 
deadline in Phase II. 
6.1. Theoretical contribution 
Our work renders three major contributions. First, we find that participants of the EU 
ETS engaged more often in carbon allowances trading as the compliance deadline was 
approaching. This phenomenon was particularly the case for those with high allowances 
requirements, and less so for those that already held sufficient allowances. This finding 
extends the literature investigating allowances prices and the volatility of their 
returns[54-56] by identifying an additional source behind the volatility clustering 
observed in the EU ETS. This refers to periods of low return volatility followed by 
periods of increased volatility. In the EU ETS, the latter occur at the end of the calendar 
year and also, in the months leading to the compliance deadline [57]. While this has 
been attributed to the increased volume of allowances traded during this period [29], 
the research in traditional markets has indicated that volatility clustering can also arise 
due to increased trading frequency [58]. Thus, our finding suggests that volatility 
clustering in the EU ETS is not only because increased amounts of allowances are 
traded from December to April, but also because during this period firms traded more 
frequently. 
 
Second, we observe that participants of the EU ETS generally followed a contrariantype trading strategy, however, they were increasingly engaging in momentum-type of 
trades as the compliance deadline in Phase II was approaching. In the case of Phase I 
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5059527
Preprint not peer reviewed

24 
of the EU ETS, the fact that the firms generally followed a contrarian-type of trading 
strategy throughout the period can be explained by the generous caps provided to firms, 
along with the fact that emissions allowances issued in Phase I could not be used for 
compliance in Phase II. This led to a situation in which the demand for allowances in 
the latter part of Phase I was particularly weak and therefore, the prices of allowances 
fell to a few eurocents at the end of that period [13,15,59]. The switch in the trading 
strategy observed in the period leading to the compliance deadline in Phase II, however, 
leads to novel insights on the trading practices of participating firms in the EU ETS and 
in turn, extends the literature examining allowances prices based on trading behavior 
of the EU ETS participating firms[7,29,47]. 
 
Specifically, since buying transactions from participating firms lacking allowances near 
the compliance deadline in Phase II were taking place when there was an upward trend 
in allowances’ prices, implies that firms, given the annual compliance pressure they are 
facing, are particularly concerned about the potential of incurring increased compliance 
costs if allowances prices continue to rise. However, this may put an upward pressure 
on allowances prices that is not reflective of an increase in their fundamentals, that is, 
marginal abatement costs. Correspondingly, since sale transactions from the firms with 
surplus allowances were mainly taking place when there was a downward trend in the 
allowances’ prices indicates that firms prefer to recoup a fraction of their investments 
on cleaner technologies rather than banking these allowances for future compliance. In 
turn, this may put a downward pressure on allowances prices that is not reflective of a 
decrease in marginal abatement costs. From a firm-level perspective, these new insights 
from this work further suggest that the EU ETS participants neither do generally follow

Preprint not peer reviewed

25 
a comprehensive carbon strategy for attaining environmental compliance, nor do they 
take full advantage of the tools that policymakers have provided them with. 
 
Third, while the revisions undertaken in the operation of the EU ETS after Phase I seem 
to have promoted the overall efficiency of the market [36,60-62], the annual compliance 
pressure that firms face seems to alter their trading behavior in a manner that may still 
jeopardize this cap-and-trade carbon market in achieving its primary objective, that is, 
to reduce GHG emissions at the lowest possible cost[10-12]. In detail, a necessary 
condition for a cap-and-trade system to facilitate CO2 reductions in a cost-effective 
manner is for allowances’ prices to accurately reflect the firms’ marginal abatement 
costs [63]. The reason is that otherwise, the time at which a firm will decide to adopt 
cleaner technologies will be suboptimal; if the emissions allowances price is lower than 
marginal abatement costs, a firm may opt to incorrectly postpone such investments, 
while if the carbon emissions allowances price is higher, it may be led to undertake a 
poor investment. In both cases, the extent at which the cap-and-trade system will be 
able to achieve its objective is adversely impacted as the adoption of cleaner 
technologies will not be initiated by those facing comparably lower abatement costs. 
6.2. Policy implications 
With the annual compliance pressure EU ETS participants face impacting their trading 
behavior in a way that contributes to increased volatility in the market, and potentially 
results in allowances prices deviating from their fundamentals at the period leading to 
the compliance deadline, not only is the investment signal for the firms not clear, but 
importantly, it can be misleading. From a policy perspective therefore, actions could be 
taken to alleviate the impact of the compliance pressure on firms’ trading behavior. 
First, policymakers can introduce incentives for firms to surrender allowances before 
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5059527
Preprint not peer reviewed

26 
the compliance deadline. For example, they can offer subsidy or additional benefits to 
firms to increase the willingness of surrendering allowances earlier in the year. This 
approach encourages firms to engage in proactive trading throughout the entire calendar 
year, reducing the market volatility caused by last-minute compliance trading. Second, 
policymakers can implement measures to enhance market stability and reduce 
fluctuations in allowance prices. These measures can include introducing price ceilings 
or floors to limit extreme price movements, and implementing circuit breakers to halt 
trading during periods of excessive volatility. Third, policymakers can focus on 
increasing market transparency by providing better access to information and data 
related to allowance trading. Potential means to publish relevant information involve 
publishing real-time emissions data, enhancing reporting requirements, and ensuring 
the availability of accurate and timely market information. 
6.3. Limitations and future research 
As with any study, this work is not free from limitations. Admittedly, this article suffers 
two major limitations. First, due to limited data availability and affected operational 
continuity, this article primarily examines the trading behavior of the firms under the 
provisions of the EU ETS during Phase I and II. In this regard, our work may not reflect 
the firms’ latest trading behavior. Second, this study is based on the EU ETS and does 
not include other carbon markets. This limitation might hinder the generalization of the 
observations derived from this study. Therefore, future research can include the latest 
transactions of the EU ETS’s participants, as well as can consider other carbon markets 
like China’s national carbon market and Chicago Climate Exchange.
