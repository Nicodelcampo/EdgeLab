Effects of Market Microstructure Changes on Market Liquidity: The Case of Malaysia 
 
Muath Asmar1*, Zamri Ahmad2 
Finance Section, School of Management,  
Universiti Sains Malaysia, 11800, Penang, Malaysia 
Abstract  
Securities markets around the entire world are currently structured in numerous of ways, and they 
are making major changes in their rules and methods of trading. In the last two decades, several 
substantial changes in the Bursa Malaysia microstructure have taken place in terms of trading 
mechanism and market regulations. The current paper explores the effects of market 
microstructure changes on the liquidity of securities traded in Bursa Malaysia from 1990 to 2009. 
An intervention model is employed in the empirical testing of this paper. The results provide 
evidence that the liquidity of securities is affected by the changes in the mechanisms and 
regulation of trading. The findings of this study provide beneficial information to the regulatory 
bodies of securities exchanges, portfolio managers and individual investors. 
JEL classification: G10, G14 
Keywords: Market Microstructure; Market Liquidity; Trading Mechanisms; Market Regulation; 
Bursa Malaysia 
1. Introduction 
The primary functions of securities markets are price discovery and provision of liquidity to the 
market participants (O'Hara, 2003). Although the ultimate functions of securities markets are 
identical, the ways of achieving their goals vary across markets and over time within the same 
market. Indeed, securities markets over the entire world are being structured in several ways, and 
they make major changes in their rules and methods of trading over time. Changes in 
technologies and regulations of securities markets are the factors behind these differences in the 
securities market microstructure. These changes have brought forth a plethora of studies on what 
is commonly referred to as market microstructure research (Berkman & Comerton-Forde, 2011). 
In this area of finance, several studies (e.g., Cheng et al., 2006; Comerton-Forde, 1999; Frino et 
al., 2008) show that the differences between the microstructure of markets imply that they 
provide different levels of liquidity. However, most of these studies focus mainly on crosssectional differences in the market microstructure by comparing between two trading methods in 
a similar span of time. In addition, the majority of the microstructure studies have employed 
various measures of liquidity which primarily captured the trading cost dimension of liquidity. 
Nevertheless, liquidity of securities has several dimensions in addition to trading cost, namely 
trading quantity, trading speed and price impact. Since liquidity has several dimensions, existing 
measures inevitably demonstrate a limited ability to measure fully the liquidity of securities and 
they may be inaccurate even in the dimension they aim to capture (Liu, 2006). Moreover, most of 
the finance literature on the effect of market microstructure on the liquidity of securities has been 
conducted on quote-driven markets (Chai et al., 2010). Therefore, investigating the changes in the

2.  Literature Review 
The change in liquidity of securities over time is a widely known fact in financial economics and 
that the common component in the liquidity across securities is the cause of this change (Narayan 
& Zheng, 2010). Liquidity is an elusive, vague, and slippery concept that observing it directly is 
difficult. It has a number of aspects, and it cannot be captured in a single measure (Lee et al., 
2006). In general, liquidity describes the level at which large quantities of security can be traded 
quickly at low cost with little price discount (Liu, 2006). One important implication drawn from 
this definition is that the market is considered liquid if the stocks traded in the market can be 
easily bought or sold without transactions costs. Therefore, liquidity is often measured by a 
number of proxies of transactions costs, for example, effective spread (Roll, 1984) and the quoted 
bid-ask spread (Amihud & Mendelson, 1986).  
The differences between market types of trading mechanism imply that they provide 
different transaction costs, which consequently lead to different liquidity levels of securities. 
Several studies (e.g., Affleck-Graves et al., 1994; Chung et al., 2003; Huang & Stoll, 1996; 
Weston, 2000) have examined the effects of market microstructures on transaction costs related 
to the order-driven (auction) market and quote-driven (dealer) market. These studies suggest that 
a quote-driven market has significantly higher transaction costs than that of order-driven market. 
For example, Affleck-Graves et al. (1994) examine how differences in the trading mechanism 
affect transaction cost of securities. They compare the bid-ask spread in the auction market, as 
represented by the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and the American Stock Exchange 
(AMEX), with the competitive dealer market represented by the National Association of 
Securities Dealers Automated Quotations (NASDAQ) system. They find that the orderprocessing cost component is smaller and that the adverse selection component is greater in the 
NYSE/AMEX trading systems than in the NASDAQ system. Another study was conducted by 
Gajewski and Gresse (2007) to compare the cost of trading between the hybrid order-driven 
market of the London Stock Exchange, in which part of the order flow is executed by dealers out

of the order book, and the pure order-driven market of the Euronext, in which order flow is 
executed by a centralized electronic order book. Their results indicate that transaction costs are 
lower in the pure order-driven than in the hybrid order-driven market.  
Other studies (e.g., Blennerhassett & Bowman, 1998; Theissen, 1999; Venkataraman, 
2001) have examined the effects of trading mechanism on transaction costs of the same securities 
in different trading mechanisms. These studies have found significant differences in transaction 
costs between two different trading mechanisms. An example provided by Theissen (1999), who 
compares the transaction costs in the trading mechanism used by the Frankfurt Stock Exchange, 
in which trading is conducted on the floor and through an electronic trading system 
simultaneously. He finds that the bid-ask spreads tend to be larger on the floor than in the 
electronic trading system. Venkataraman (2001) compares the execution costs of similar stocks in 
Paris Bourse, in which an automated trading structure is implemented, with those in the NYSE, in 
which a floor-based trading structure is implemented. As different market microstructures are 
applied in these two securities exchanges, the results indicate that the execution costs are higher 
in Paris Bourse than in NYSE. Moreover, Pagano and Schwartz, (2003) analyze the effects of 
employing electronic call auctions in market closings on the market quality of Paris Bourse. 
Their results indicate that the introduction of the closing calls lowers the execution costs for 
individual participants and enhances price discovery for the broad market. 
Furthermore, market transparency is an important concern to market regulators because it 
affects the availability of information to the market participants, subsequently affecting the 
transaction and information costs. Changes in market transparency influence the information 
asymmetry, bid-ask spread, and therefore the liquidity of securities. Supporting this point of 
view, several studies provide significant evidence on the effect of transparency on transaction and 
information costs (e.g., Chung & Chuwonganant, 2009; Flood et al., 1999; Pagano & Roell, 
1996; Rakowski & Beardsley, 2008; Rakowski & Wang Beardsley, 2008). These studies have 
revealed that increasing the market transparency decreases the transaction costs. For example, 
Rakowski and Beardsley (2008) examine how information asymmetry and noninformationrelated transaction costs change while moving further along the electronic limit order books. 
They find that the asymmetric information component decreases as depth increases. The authors 
also find that inactive stocks have more information asymmetry in the inside quotes but decrease 
more rapidly along the books than active stocks. Chung and Chuwonganant (2009) have 
investigated the effect of changing pretrade transparencies on bid-ask spreads using data before 
and after the introduction of a fully integrated order display and execution system, which 
provides more transparent and deeper markets for NASDAQ securities. Their results show that 
the spreads decline significantly after the changes in pretrade transparency. They interpret their 
results as the increasing market transparency prompting market participants to submit more 
aggressive quotes and orders. Other studies reveal different results from the previous ones (e.g., 
Bloomfield & O'Hara, 1999; Madhavan et al., 2005). The results of these studies indicate that 
increasing market transparency increases transaction costs. For example, Bloomfield and O'Hara 
(1999) investigate the effects of trade and quote disclosure on market efficiency, bid-ask spreads, 
and trader welfare. Results show that increased transparency increases the informational 
efficiency of transaction prices and apparently increases the opening bid-ask spreads by reducing 
market-makers’ incentives to compete for order flow.

Above all, transaction costs and consequently the liquidity of securities constitute the 
important factors that affect the prices of assets according to the forms of market microstructures 
where securities are traded. The market structure has an immediate and direct effect on the 
provision of liquidity to market participants. The existing empirical literature has mainly focused 
on comparing the liquidity of different market microstructures. For example, Comerton-Forde 
(1999) compares the opening mechanism used by the Australian Stock Exchange, in which call 
auction is implemented, and the Jakarta Stock Exchange, in which continuous auction is 
implemented. He reports that the choice of opening mechanism has significant implications for 
liquidity and volatility in the opening of trading. His results suggest that the use of a call auction 
enhances market efficiency by increasing liquidity and lowering volatility in the opening of the 
exchange. Frino, et al. (2008) examine the effects of market microstructure change on the 
liquidity of stocks traded in Italian Bourse. Their result indicates that the stocks that switch from 
the auction market to specialist market reveal a reduction in both bid-ask spreads and quoted 
depth as a result of the switch. Chung et al. (2005) study the relation between tick sizes and 
market liquidity of Bursa Malaysia, in which a step-function tick is implemented. They find a 
high degree of quote clustering on the stocks traded in Bursa Malaysia and that quote clustering 
is negatively related to tick size. Their results indicate that larger tick sizes for higher priced 
stocks are detrimental to market liquidity. Cai et al. (2008) investigate the effect of the change in 
minimum tick size on liquidity provisions in the Tokyo Stock Exchange when stock prices cross 
the ¥1000 threshold. They find that a lower percentage of the bid-ask mid-point leads to a 
proportional reduction in trade execution costs, exceeding the proportional reduction in the bidask mid-point.  
The rules of trading securities determine how prices are formed; therefore, market 
regulations can affect their liquidity. Supporting this view, Hegde and McDermott (2003) study 
the market liquidity effects on stocks added to and deleted from the Standard and Poor’s (S&P) 
500 index. Their results reveal positive, significant, and sustained improvement in the liquidity of 
added stocks to the index; in contrast, stocks deleted from the index incur negative abnormal 
returns, and their market liquidity suffers over three months after deletion. The high degree of 
transparency in the process of trading enhances the liquidity of securities by reducing the 
opportunities for taking advantage of less informed or nonprofessional participants. Therefore, 
securities exchanges require as much trading information as possible to be disseminated 
immediately to make it available to the public (Pagano & Roell, 1996).

4. Methodology and Empirical Results 
Liquidity has several dimensions. It is often measured by a number of measures covering one or 
more of its dimensions. Thus, among the available measures of liquidity in the literature, the 
current study employed the Liu (2006) measure of liquidity, which is the standardized turnoveradjusted number of zero daily trading volumes over the prior x months (LMx). The reason for 
selecting this measure of liquidity is that it captures multiple dimensions of liquidity (i.e., trading 
quantity, price impact, trading cost, and trading speed). In addition, Liu (2006) reveals that this 
measure is highly correlated with the commonly used bid-ask spread, turnover, and return-tovolume measures. Furthermore, Chai, et al., (2010) proved that the proxies of liquidity, such as 
stock turnover, return reversal measure, illiquidity ratio, and proportional spread have better 
correlation with LMx measure of liquidity. LMx measure of liquidity is calculated as follows:

sessions, and market transparency, and to market regulations, such as the rules of listing, trading 
unit, and tick size and spread, as well as explicit transaction costs.  
 
5. Conclusion 
Changes in regulations and technology affect the securities markets. In response to these changes, 
securities exchanges have made changes in the trading mechanisms used to accomplish trading 
and have amended the rules of trading that govern the trading process. In the last two decades, 
Bursa Malaysia has implemented several changes in their market microstructure on trading 
mechanism and market regulation. The current study investigates the effects of these changes on 
the liquidity of securities. The results emerging from the use of an intervention model reveal that 
the market microstructure, in terms of trading mechanism and market regulations, is an important 
determinant of the liquidity of securities. This finding sheds light on the importance of market 
microstructure as a determinant factor affecting the liquidity of securities. Therefore, the findings 
of this study provide important information to the regulatory bodies of securities exchanges with 
regard to the market design in order to improve the liquidity of securities. Portfolio managers and 
individual investors can also benefit from the findings of this study by considering the effects of 
market microstructure changes on the liquidity of securities and consequently the risk of 
securities they manage. Further research on the effects of market microstructure on the liquidity 
of securities in other markets and on the use of other proxies of liquidity will contribute to the 
better understanding of the determents of the liquidity of securities.
