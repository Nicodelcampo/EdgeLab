Abstract
This paper develops a regime-conditioned trading framework for foreign exchange markets that
exploits intraday mean reversion toward a volume-weighted fair value benchmark under conditions of momentum exhaustion. Three dimensions are integrated: price location relative to
previous-session extremes, deviation from the Volume Weighted Average Price (VWAP), and
trend strength as measured by Wilder’s Average Directional Index (ADX). The strategy generates short (long) signals when price reaches prior-session highs (lows) while simultaneously
exhibiting large positive (negative) VWAP deviations and a turning-over ADX, which collectively indicate that directional momentum is fading. The model bridges market-microstructure
theory — specifically the role of VWAP as an institutional benchmark price — with technical
regime filtering, contributing a theoretically grounded, state-conditioned mean-reversion framework to the FX literature. Formal backtesting and performance attribution are left to future
work.
Keywords: mean reversion, VWAP, Average Directional Index, momentum exhaustion, foreign
exchange, regime conditioning, market microstructure
JEL Classification: G12, G14, G15, C58

Momentum Exhaustion and Fair Value Reversion in FX Markets
1.
Introduction
Foreign exchange markets are the largest and most liquid financial markets in the world, with daily
turnover exceeding $7.5 trillion as of April 2022 (BIS, 2022). Despite their depth, empirical studies have documented persistent short-horizon inefficiencies: intraday price dynamics in FX exhibit
discernible patterns linked to order flow, liquidity provision, and the behaviour of institutional participants (Evans and Lyons, 2002; Lyons, 2001). One of the most studied of these patterns is mean
reversion — the tendency of prices that have deviated from equilibrium to return toward it over short
time horizons (Poterba and Summers, 1988; Lo and MacKinlay, 1988).
A fundamental challenge in exploiting mean-reversion strategies is distinguishing genuine exhaustion from the early stages of a trending move. Acting against a strong trend generates severe adverse
excursion and ultimately negative expected returns (Jegadeesh, 1993). This identification problem
motivates our regime-conditioning approach: we restrict mean-reversion trades to states in which
trend strength, as measured by Wilder’s Average Directional Index (ADX), is elevated but no longer
increasing, signalling that directional momentum is decaying rather than building.
The equilibrium anchor in our framework is the Volume Weighted Average Price (VWAP). Originally developed as an execution benchmark for institutional trading desks (Berkowitz et al., 1988),
VWAP has accumulated a substantial theoretical rationale as a measure of intraday fair value —
the price at which the volume-weighted average transaction has occurred. Algorithmic execution
strategies that seek to minimise market impact routinely target VWAP, creating a gravitational pull
toward it from both above and below (Kissell and Glantz, 2003; Madhavan, 2002). This institutional
anchoring provides the economic mechanism driving the reversion dynamics our strategy seeks to
exploit.
The spatial trigger for signal generation is proximity to previous-session extremes (prior-day high
and low). These levels act as liquidity nodes: limit orders, stop orders, and option barriers tend to
cluster at psychologically and technically salient price levels, creating zones of potential exhaustion
and reversal (Osler, 2001, 2003). When price reaches these levels in a state of momentum decay, the
probability of continuation diminishes relative to reversion.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature
on mean reversion in FX markets, VWAP-based strategies, and directional-index conditioning. Section 3 sets out the theoretical framework and economic intuition. Section 4 provides the formal
model specification. Section 5 derives the composite trading signal. Section 6 discusses implementation considerations. Section 7 describes the planned empirical programme. Section 8 concludes.
Appendix A provides a full Python implementation of the strategy pipeline.
2.
Literature Review
2.1. Mean Reversion in Foreign Exchange Markets
Mean reversion in asset prices has been studied extensively since the seminal papers of Poterba and
Summers (1988) and Lo and MacKinlay (1988), who documented negative autocorrelation in stock
returns over short horizons. In FX markets, the evidence for intraday mean reversion is generally
stronger than at daily or weekly frequencies, owing to the dominant role of market-making and

liquidity provision (Lyons, 2001).
Evans and Lyons (2002) demonstrate that order flow is the primary driver of short-run exchangerate movements, with prices reverting once the informational content of order flow dissipates. Breedon
and Ranaldo (2010) show that large one-sided order flow imbalances at fixing windows generate
transient price dislocations that subsequently reverse, consistent with a temporary market-impact
mechanism. Menkhoff et al. (2012) provide evidence that technical trading rules, which typically
exploit mean reversion or trend exhaustion, generate positive returns in FX markets even after
accounting for transaction costs, particularly at intraday frequencies.
2.2. VWAP as an Institutional Benchmark and Fair Value Proxy
The Volume Weighted Average Price was formalised as an execution benchmark in the work of
Berkowitz et al. (1988), who proposed it as a measure of trading performance relative to market
conditions. Since then, it has become the dominant intraday execution benchmark for institutional
equity and FX desks worldwide (Kissell and Glantz, 2003).
Madhavan (2002) analyses the implications of VWAP-targeting behaviour for price dynamics and
shows that when a significant share of market participants target VWAP, prices exhibit a well-defined
gravitational pull toward the VWAP level throughout the trading session. This is because agents
seeking to execute at VWAP provide mean-reverting liquidity when prices deviate significantly from
it.
In the FX microstructure literature, Ito and Yamada (2012) document that VWAP deviations at
the Tokyo fix are systematically exploited by informed traders and subsequently corrected, suggesting
that VWAP carries genuine equilibrium content. Butz and Oomen (2019) extend this analysis and
formalise the relationship between VWAP, order-flow imbalance, and price impact in electronic FX
markets, providing a structural foundation for VWAP-based reversion strategies.
2.3. Directional Indicators and Regime Conditioning
Wilder’s Directional Movement System, introduced in Wilder (1978), provides a family of indicators
— the Positive Directional Indicator (+DI), Negative Directional Indicator (−DI), and the Average Directional Index (ADX) — designed to quantify the strength of a trend independently of its
direction. The ADX has been widely adopted in academic and practitioner research as a regimeconditioning tool: values above a threshold (commonly 25) are interpreted as trending regimes, while
values below suggest range-bound or mean-reverting conditions (Colby, 2003).
Katz and McCormick (2000) demonstrate empirically that conditioning mean-reversion strategies
on low-to-moderate ADX environments substantially improves performance relative to unconditional
implementations, reducing exposure to breakout losses.
Chan (2013) formalises this intuition in
a regime-switching framework, showing that the expected return to counter-trend strategies is a
decreasing function of current directional strength.
Our contribution departs from this prior work in two respects. First, we condition not on the level
of ADX alone, but on its derivative — specifically, we require that ADX is elevated but no longer
rising, which is a more precise signature of exhaustion. Second, we combine this regime filter with
a market-microstructure anchor (VWAP) and a spatial trigger (previous-day extremes), creating a

multi-dimensional signal with distinct theoretical motivation for each component.
2.4. Previous-Day Extremes as Liquidity Nodes
The role of psychologically salient price levels in FX markets has been documented by Osler (2001),
who finds significant clustering of stop-loss and take-profit orders at round numbers and prior-session
extremes. This clustering creates self-reinforcing dynamics: as price approaches a prior high, stop
orders above it and option barriers at it generate a transient increase in selling pressure once those
levels are penetrated or approached, which can catalyse reversal (Osler, 2003).
De Grauwe and Grimaldi (2006) provide a model in which heterogeneous agents — chartists
and fundamentalists — interact, producing endogenous cycles of overshooting and reversion around
fundamental value. Prior-session extremes naturally serve as coordination points for the chartist
component of this population, reinforcing their role as spatial triggers for reversals.
3.
Theoretical Framework
3.1. Economic Hypothesis
The strategy rests on the following central hypothesis:
When an FX exchange rate approaches the prior session’s extreme prices while exhibiting a
large deviation from intraday VWAP, and directional momentum is elevated but no longer
increasing, the probability of mean reversion toward VWAP in the near term exceeds the
unconditional baseline.
This hypothesis has three interlocking economic foundations.
Foundation 1 — Institutional fair-value anchoring.
A large fraction of institutional FX
volume is executed algorithmically via VWAP-targeting strategies. When price deviates significantly
above (below) VWAP, algorithmic sell (buy) orders accumulate, creating a restoring force. The larger
the deviation, the stronger the expected restoring force.
Foundation 2 — Liquidity exhaustion at session extremes.
Prior-day highs and lows represent levels at which the market previously halted. Stop-loss orders, option barriers, and psychological
resistance concentrate at these levels. When price revisits them, the pool of momentum-driven buyers (sellers) willing to pay up (sell down) is typically smaller than at the first visit, while the pool of
fade-oriented liquidity providers is larger.
Foundation 3 — Momentum decay as an exhaustion signal.
A rising ADX indicates that
a trend is gathering strength; the time to fade a trend is not when it is accelerating. However, when
ADX begins to flatten or decline from an elevated level, the marginal trend-following participant is
no longer being rewarded, reducing fresh buying (selling) pressure and increasing the susceptibility
of price to reversal.

The three conditions are individually necessary but not jointly sufficient; their simultaneous
occurrence produces a significantly more concentrated distribution of subsequent returns skewed
toward reversion.
3.2. Market Microstructure Interpretation
Let the exchange rate at time t within a session be Pt. Define the session VWAP as the volumeweighted centre of gravity of all transactions from session open to time t. A price Pt > VWAPt
implies that the marginal buyer has transacted above the average price paid by all participants in
the session. From the perspective of VWAP-benchmarked institutions, Pt represents an unfavourable
execution level relative to their benchmark, reducing their willingness to buy and increasing their
willingness to sell. The reverse holds for Pt < VWAPt.
This institutional microstructure provides the mechanism through which VWAP functions as an
equilibrium attractor, a role formalised by Madhavan (2002) and empirically confirmed by Butz and
Oomen (2019).

5.3. Target and Risk Management
The natural profit target for a short (long) position initiated at P0 ≥Ht−1 (resp. P0 ≤Lt−1) is
VWAPt∗, where t∗is the time at which the position is closed. Because VWAP is itself dynamic,
the target moves through the session. A common operationalisation is to close the position either
(i) when price crosses VWAPt from the entry side, or (ii) at session close, whichever occurs first.
Stop placement is theoretically motivated by the spatial trigger: a short entered at Ht−1 should
be stopped out on a confirmed close above Ht−1 by a margin proportional to the Average True Range,
consistent with the risk management framework of Wilder (1978).
6.
Implementation Considerations
6.1. Data and Session Definition
FX markets trade continuously, which requires an explicit session definition for VWAP computation
and for identifying prior-session extremes. Common choices are (i) the London session (07:00–17:00
GMT), (ii) the New York session (13:00–22:00 GMT), and (iii) the 24-hour rolling session reset at
midnight New York time. The session definition affects both VWAP anchoring and the relevance of
prior-session extremes as liquidity nodes; it should be chosen to align with the liquidity profile of the
target currency pair (Andersen and Bollerslev, 1998).
6.2. Parameter Calibration
The model contains four free parameters requiring empirical calibration:
(i) The VWAP-deviation threshold θ, which governs the minimum overextension required to trigger
a signal. A natural starting point is to set θ equal to one intraday standard deviation of Dt.
(ii) The ADX lookback n, standardly set at 14 (Wilder, 1978), but potentially adjusted for higherfrequency data where the effective lookback in calendar time should remain economically meaningful.

(iii) The ADX mean-window m for computing ADXt, which determines the reference period against
which current trend strength is evaluated.
(iv) The bar frequency ∆t, which governs the trade-off between signal frequency and noise. At very
short frequencies (e.g., tick-by-tick), microstructure noise dominates; at very long frequencies
(e.g., daily), the intraday anchoring properties of VWAP are lost. The range 5–60 minutes is
typical for intraday FX strategies.
6.3. Transaction Costs and Execution
Mean-reversion strategies are sensitive to transaction costs because positions are short-lived and
profit margins per trade are modest. The round-trip bid-ask spread in major currency pairs (EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD) is typically 0.1–0.5 basis points for institutional participants in
normal conditions (BIS, 2022). Net-of-cost performance evaluation is essential; signal profitability in
gross terms is not a sufficient indicator of viability.
6.4. Applicability Across Currency Pairs
The model is theoretically applicable to any exchange rate for which intraday volume data of sufficient quality is available to compute a meaningful VWAP. In practice, this restricts the universe
to the major and cross-currency pairs traded on electronic platforms (EBS, Reuters Matching, and
multilateral trading facilities), and excludes pairs dominated by voice brokering where volume data
is unreliable (King et al., 2013).
7.
Planned Empirical Programme
The theoretical framework presented here will be subjected to systematic empirical evaluation in
subsequent work. The planned programme includes:
Data.
High-frequency tick and bar data for at least six major currency pairs (EUR/USD, GBP/USD,
USD/JPY, USD/CHF, AUD/USD, USD/CAD) over a minimum of five years, sourced from a tier-1
electronic venue or reputable data vendor.
Backtesting methodology.
Walk-forward out-of-sample evaluation with rolling parameter reestimation to prevent look-ahead bias. Performance will be evaluated on standard metrics including
the Sharpe ratio (Sharpe, 1994), Calmar ratio, maximum drawdown, and the information ratio
relative to a passive carry benchmark.
Statistical significance.
Given the well-documented dangers of data-snooping in technical trading
strategy research (Sullivan et al., 1999; White, 2000), statistical significance will be assessed using
White’s Reality Check and the superior predictive ability test of Hansen (2005).
Regime decomposition.
Performance will be decomposed across market regimes (trending vs.
mean-reverting, high vs. low volatility, crisis vs. normal) to assess the conditions under which the
strategy is most effective and to identify potential structural sources of return.

8.
Conclusion
This paper has presented a theoretically grounded, multi-dimensional framework for identifying
mean-reversion opportunities in intraday FX markets.
The model integrates three conceptually
distinct but complementary components: VWAP as a microstructure-based measure of institutional
fair value, prior-session extremes as spatial triggers reflecting the clustering of contingent orders, and
ADX with its first difference as a regime filter that confines entry to states of momentum exhaustion
rather than momentum continuation.
The strategy’s theoretical contribution lies in the precise characterisation of the joint conditions
under which reversion is most probable. Each individual condition is well-grounded in prior literature;
their combination into a coherent, state-conditioned signal architecture represents an advance over
single-indicator approaches. By explicitly avoiding entry when ∆ADXt > 0, the model provides
a principled solution to the trend-versus-reversion identification problem that has long challenged
mean-reversion practitioners.
Formal empirical evaluation — incorporating backtesting, transaction cost analysis, and statistical significance testing — is deferred to the companion paper. The present work establishes the
theoretical scaffolding necessary to interpret those results within a coherent economic framework.

Implementation notes.
The default threshold θ = 0.001 (0.1%) is a starting value only and
should be calibrated to the realised intraday volatility of the target pair as discussed in Section 6.
The Wilder smoothing function uses a simple-sum seed over the first n bars, the standard convention
in Wilder (1978); an exponential moving average seed is an alternative. For large datasets, the loop
inside wilder_smooth can be replaced with a Numba-accelerated implementation for substantial
speed gains. The pipeline does not include position sizing, stop-loss execution, or transaction cost
accounting; these components are required before any live or simulated deployment.
