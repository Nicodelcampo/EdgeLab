Faculty Advisor: Dr. Joshua Spizman 
 
 
Abstract 
 
Ark Investment Management (ARK), led by CIO Cathie Wood, has risen to prominence over the 
past few years because of its remarkable performance. Because of requirements for active ETFs to 
publish daily holdings, market participants have gained unprecedented access to following the path 
to market-beating performance. ARK is celebrated for both its stock picking and active trading 
abilities. In this paper, I study how active trading affects alpha from ARK’s funds, and I create 
static synthetic portfolios to strip out the effects of active trading. I find that active trading on 
average reduces ARK’s one-month returns by 1.36 percentage points.

I. Introduction 
 
Between February 12th and March 23rd of 2020, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 
37% in response to the global emergence of COVID-19.1 Despite triggering circuit breakers at 
the New York Stock Exchange, the sell-off did not appear to cause a sustained panic. By July of 
2020, the market had already recovered to its pre-crash levels.2 The rapid rebound could be 
explained by a variety of factors that are beyond the scope of this project, but suffice it to say, 
the rebound and the subsequent bull market captured the imagination of individual investors. 
Platforms like Reddit and Twitter grew communities of engaged investors, traders, and 
speculators. The public could not get enough stock market content. Even controversial sports 
media mogul Dave Portnoy began live streaming his daily stock market moves and musings. 
During this period, he was reportedly getting nearly one million views per day.3 
 
As this stock market mania ensued, people naturally started trying to find the “smart 
money.” Unlike in previous periods of widespread excitement, this generation had access to a 
new form of information, actively managed exchange traded fund (ETF) holdings disclosures. 
Under SEC rule, actively managed ETFs must report daily holdings. Instead of relying on

II. Literature Review 
 
The body of academic work on actively managed ETFs is limited, but there are some 
resources that frame the relevance of this question. The central question related to actively 
managed ETFs is whether active strategies outperform passive strategies. This is a question that 
has been studied and debated in a variety of settings. In a 2014 article, Ellis analyzes historical 
returns and fees and determines that although active management might have once made sense, 
the improved efficiency of current markets makes active management undesirable for clients due 
to the limited outperformance and associated fees (Ellis, 2014).8 This line of thinking appears to 
have caught on with many investors. In 2019, for the first time in U.S. capital markets history, 
passive funds held more assets than active funds.9  
 
This trend toward passively managed funds is not welcomed by all in the investment 
community. There are some who are worried that the increased levels of passive money in the

contribute to better returns in the market portfolio. In this paper, I study whether that same 
principle holds true for individual equities within an actively managed ETF.

not definitively prove that active trading is inferior to buying and holding a concentrated 
portfolio, but it does provide some evidence to that effect.

3.2 Methodology 
 
To best measure ARK’s active trading ability, I analyze how much alpha active trading 
generated over one-month periods. I calculated the returns of synthetic portfolios that mirrored 
the initial holdings of each ETF for each date instance. In order to obtain these portfolio returns, 
I multiplied the one-month return (r) of each equity held by the ETF weighted by its initial 
allocation within the ETF (w) divided by the proportion of holdings accounted for (p): 
 
 
By dividing by the proportion of holdings accounted for, the synthetic return more 
accurately reflects the performance of the holdings. For example, if for a given instance only 
90% of holdings were accounted and the holdings returned an average of 10%, the synthetic 
return without the proportion of holdings adjustment would be 9%. For this research, I assume 
that any missing holdings performed as the average of the accounted for holdings by dividing the 
weighted average by the proportion of holdings accounted for. The average number of holdings 
accounted for in this data set is 97.22%.

4.3 ARK Funds Portfolio Turnover and Relative Performance 
The first two tables imply that there is a significant difference between the one-month 
returns of the ETFs and the one-month returns of the static synthetic portfolios. They do not, 
however, prove anything about whether the amount of trading influences the amount of 
underperformance, only that trading does lead to underperformance. To analyze the impact of the 
amount of trading, I calculated an additional variable, fund turnover. For the fund turnover 
variable, a value of one would indicate that every initial position in the portfolio was liquidated, 
and the proceeds were used to purchase new positions by month end. A value of zero would 
suggest that no trading occurred. If an ETF both increased and decreased its position within a 
given month, this measure would only account for the difference from the initial allocation. The 
mean value for one-month fund turnover for the data set is 11.46%.  
In this regression, I controlled for the number of holdings within the ETF and the specific 
funds. The t Stat of -4.0777 for the fund turnover variable indicates that fund turnover is a highly 
significant explanatory variable (table 4). The coefficient suggests that if an ARK fund were to 
turnover its entire portfolio, it would underperform the synthetic portfolio by 9.55 percentage 
points. For the average fund turnover of 11.46%, the ETF would underperform the synthetic 
portfolio by 1.09 percentage points over a one-month period. 
I also find that the number of unique holdings is not significant to the relative 
performance. Unsurprisingly given the earlier results (table 3), the specific funds are also 
insignificant in this regression.

4.4 ARK Funds Relative Performance Controlling for Market Conditions 
In this regression, I am testing whether market conditions had a significant impact on 
relative performance (table 5). It seemed plausible that when the market as a whole performed 
better or worse in a given one-month period, ARK might have better or worse relative 
performance against the synthetic portfolios. For instance, ARK might have great results trading 
in a bull market, but poor results trading in a bear market.  
I find, however, that market conditions do not have a significant impact on ETF relative 
performance to the synthetic portfolios. I measure market conditions by using the S&P 500 index 
price return over each one-month period. The t Stat for the S&P 500 performance is closer to 
significant than any of the other control variables, but it still falls well outside the range for 
significance. These results suggest that ARK funds do not trade better or worse due to the 
overarching market conditions. Active trading costs ARK’s investors money regardless of 
whether they are in a bull market or a bear market.  
 
V. Conclusion 
 
In this research, I provide data and statistical analysis implying that ARK funds are losing 
potential alpha by actively trading. The findings are not an indictment of ARK as a whole, but 
rather they suggest that a piece of ARK’s strategy might be best altered to achieve optimal 
returns. Not only are investors losing alpha due to the mechanics of ETFs (Pham, Marshall, 
Nguyen, and Visaltanachoti, 2021) and potentially due to less liquidity than the underlying 
securities (Rompotis, 2010), but also because of ineffective trading strategies.  
 
ARK has had remarkable returns over the past few years, but that does not mean that 
every piece of its strategy has contributed to its success. As this research shows, active trading 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985640

 
22 
has actually led to lower returns. In the last five years, individual investors following ARK’s 
daily holdings reports would have been better off buying portfolios of ARK’s holdings and 
holding them rather than following ARK into each of its daily trades or buying ARK’s ETFs.  
 
These results provide further evidence to the common perception that “you cannot time 
the market.” It is clear in the data that trading did not benefit perhaps the most well-known active 
investor in the market today. In my view, there is a troubling trend to associate picking stocks 
with active trading. Although both elements are present in actively managed ETF returns, they 
are two distinct, and in many ways unrelated, fields. In this study, I have shown that the elements 
can be separated. Although this study does not definitively prove that ARK is good at picking 
stocks and not just lucky, it does prove that there is a significant element of its returns that are 
associated with trading. While the case remains open on whether ARK can sustainably generate 
market beating returns, it is apparent that its current trading strategy is not contributing toward 
those market beating returns.  
There are a few avenues for future research. First, one could analyze additional ETFs. I 
would expect that the coefficients on fund specific dummy variables would be significant in this 
new research due to the firms having different managers and significantly different trading 
strategies leading to significantly different mean relative performance. Another avenue for future 
research would be analyzing the relative performance between static synthetic portfolios and 
actively managed ETFs over longer holdings periods. I have done some preliminary work down 
this avenue that suggests the underperformance holds over longer time periods.
