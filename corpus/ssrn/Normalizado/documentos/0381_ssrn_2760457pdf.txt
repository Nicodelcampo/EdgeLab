Brokers or Dealers? Trading Intermediation
and Market Liquidity
Abstract
Investors can execute trades through either brokers that trade on their behalf (agency intermediation) or dealers that trade with them (principal intermediation). We explain the
heterogeneity in intermediation via the trade-oﬀbetween monitoring brokers and incurring
dealer inventory costs.
Brokers do not internalize execution outcomes and thus require
monitoring. Dealers internalize outcomes but face inventory costs. Consistent with model
predictions, agency intermediation is more prevalent in transparent and liquid markets; when
intermediary capital cost increases, principal intermediation is replaced by agency intermediation. Our theory implies that improving market transparency can alleviate the pressure
of tightening capital regulations on market liquidity.
Keywords: trading intermediation; market liquidity; market transparency; capital costs.
JEL classiﬁcation: G12, G23, N22

Introduction
Institutional trading, which is the bulk of all trading, is almost all conducted via intermediaries such as brokers and dealers. Thus, intermediaries play an essential role in facilitating
market access and liquidity. However, there has been few studies about why trading intermediation diﬀers across markets and over time. Understanding these questions is important
because they are closely related to the concerns about declining market liquidity due to post2008 capital regulations (Duﬃe, 2017; Bao, O’Hara, and Zhou, 2018), the recent Treasury
market turmoil during the COVID-19 crisis (Cheng, Wessel, and Younger, 2020), and policy
discussions about increasing transparency in ﬁxed income markets (Harris, 2015).1
In this paper, we highlight the distinction between two diﬀerent forms of intermediated
trading: agency and principal intermediation. In the former, intermediaries serve as brokers,
earn commissions, and execute trades on behalf of investors without taking inventory risk.
In the latter, intermediaries serve as dealers, earn markups, and take on inventory risk.2
There is signiﬁcant variation in the use of these two forms across markets and over time.
As illustrated in Figure 1, agency intermediation is dominant in the equity and exchangetraded futures markets, while principal intermediation is more prevalent in ﬁxed income
and currency markets.
There is also variation over time within the same market.
For
instance, U.S. corporate bonds are mostly principal intermediated, but agency intermediation
increased during the 2008 ﬁnancial crisis (Section 3.1).

What determines how trading is intermediated, and how does intermediation aﬀect market liquidity? While most studies take the intermediation structure as given, in this paper,
we propose a theory in which intermediation structure endogenously depends on the trade-oﬀ
between the cost of monitoring brokers and the cost of paying dealers for holding inventory.
Unlike dealers, brokers trade on behalf of customers, so they do not suﬀer from bad trading
execution outcomes. Thus, customers need to monitor brokers, and monitoring is more effective when market is transparent – investors can observe the quotes and trades by others –
and when there is suﬃcient turnover in the underlying security to be observed. Our theory
thus predicts that agency intermediation will dominate markets with high transparency and
turnover. We also predict a substitution eﬀect: when dealer inventory cost increases, agency
intermediation will rise endogenously. We show evidence consistent with both predictions.

We also investigate how market liquidity is aﬀected by the equilibrium structure of intermediation. We deﬁne liquidity as the inverse of the expected time it takes to ﬁnish a
transaction; principal intermediation is the fastest, agency the second, and no intermediation (investors trading by themselves) the slowest. We ﬁnd that increasing dealer inventory
cost – for instance, caused by tighter capital regulation after the 2008 ﬁnancial crisis – does
reduce market liquidity, but the adverse impact is dampened by the substitution towards
agency trading. Further, our theory suggests that increasing market transparency can further
reduce the adverse impact of higher dealer capital costs.
The key moral hazard in agency intermediation comes from the fact that brokers are
only paid for executing trades, but their compensation is not tied to the price they manage
to obtain for customers. This is unlike dealers who commit upfront to the price at which
they trade with customers. Therefore, unless properly monitored, brokers have limited incentive to execute well. Harris (2015) ﬁnds that corporate bond brokers, in over 40% of
the cases, do not obtain the best available price for their customers. Moreover, brokers can
even actively misbehave to proﬁt at the expense of their customers. In addition to ample
anecdotal examples (Harris, 2002), a slew of recent studies shows that brokers leak information about proﬁtable trades, ﬁre-sales, or insider trades to other clients (Li, Mukherjee,
and Sen, 2016; McNally, Shkilko, and Smith, 2017; Barbon, Maggio, Franzoni, and Landier,
2019; Di Maggio, Franzoni, Kermani, and Sommavilla, 2019). Anand, Samadi, Sokobin, and
Venkataraman (2019) ﬁnd that some brokers send orders to self-aﬃliated trading venues,
resulting in lower ﬁll-rates and higher transaction costs.3
To alleviate the moral hazard in agency intermediation, brokers need to be monitored,
and monitoring is only eﬀective in markets with high transparency and turnover. This can be
intuitively understood through an example. Suppose you are a fund manager. At 10:00 AM
of January 28th, 2020, you decided to buy 100,000 shares of Apple stock through a broker.

This process of investors using market data to evaluate brokers is known as “transaction cost analysis” in the investment management industry. It is routinely conducted in
high-turnover and transparent markets such as equities and futures.5 When investors cannot eﬀectively monitor brokers, it will be preferable to use principal intermediation where
investors trade with dealers at agreed-upon prices. Relative to agency intermediation, the
key drawback of principal intermediation is that dealers will incur inventory costs and need
to be compensated with markups. In the end, we conclude that the agency- versus principalintermediation choice is based on the trade-oﬀbetween broker monitoring costs and dealer
inventory costs.
We develop our theory in Section 2 using a model with endogenous intermediation structure. To capture the incentive misalignment of brokers, we follow the modeling framework
in Townsend (1979) and assume that brokers can misreport actual transaction prices. To
ensure that brokers do not misreport, if investors choose agency intermediation, they need
to monitor brokers by comparing the execution prices against the prices of other trades in
the market. Investors can also choose principal intermediation where intermediaries have
aligned incentives but charge customers for incurring inventory cost.
The equilibrium choice over the form of intermediation depends on three aspects: market transparency, security turnover, and intermediary inventory cost. The inventory cost
prediction is straightforward: agency intermediation is preferred when intermediaries charge
high prices for providing balance sheets. The more interesting predictions come from market
transparency and market turnover. In an opaque market where investors do not observe
others’ trades, it is costly to monitor brokers so investors tend to resort to principal interme5Investors compare broker execution prices against benchmarks – such as the volume-weighted average price (VWAP), the time-weighted average price (TWAP), or the participation-weighted average price

Consistent with the theory’s predictions, agency intermediation is indeed more prevalent
in markets with high transparency and high turnover. In addition to trading frequently,
the U.S. equity market is also highly transparent since all trades and quotes are reported in
real-time and accessible to all investors through the consolidated tape. As predicted, agency
intermediation is the primary method in equities, and investors routinely use market data
to monitor broker performance. In contrast, the opaque ﬁxed income and currency markets
are predominantly principal intermediated. The currency market is an interesting example
because even though it enjoys very high trading volume6, market transparency is low (most
trading volume is over-the-counter and never reported), and investors ﬁnd it challenging to
compute eﬀective price benchmarks. Consistent with our theory, the currency market has
limited usage of agency intermediation.
We also present evidence that intermediary inventory cost drives the intermediation structure in the U.S. corporate bond market. Following the existing literature, we detect agency
intermediation by identifying pairs of trades in TRACE with opposite directions and happen
within one minute (Zitzewitz, 2010; Sirri, 2014). During our sample period of 2006 - 2014,
there are three major events that led to spikes in intermediary inventory costs: the subprime mortgage defaults in late 2007, the Lehman bankruptcy in September 2008, and the
European sovereign debt crisis in 2011. When these shocks occurred, the fraction of agency
intermediation spiked, as our theory predicts.

Our theory centers on the moral hazard problem of brokers. It is, then, natural to ask:
what about dealers?
In non-competitive markets, they can engage in price-gouging and
earn excessive rents (Wang, 2016; Li and Sch¨urhoﬀ, 2019). However, this reﬂects the lack
of competition and is not a moral hazard problem per se.7 Our paper focuses on the additional moral hazard issue inherent in agency intermediation that is not present in principal
intermediation, and it is a force that is orthogonal to the eﬀects of competition. Further,
diﬀerences in competition cannot explain the intermediation structure heterogeneity across
markets. Focusing on the role of competition will lead to the counterfactual prediction that
ﬁxed income and currencies markets, which have less competition among liquidity providers,
should witness more agency intermediation.
Our results bear important implications for policy makers.
First, the intermediation structure matters for market resilience to adverse shocks. As
shown by the broad credit market dysfunctions in the 2008 ﬁnancial crisis (Brunnermeier,
2009) and the Treasury market turmoil in the recent COVID-19 crisis (Duﬃe, 2020), markets
that rely on dealers experienced severe liquidity evaporation when intermediaries receive
shocks, but we do not see similarly adverse developments in agency intermediated markets.
Therefore, facilitating agency intermediation can help the market better withstand liquidity
shocks.
Second, our theory sheds light on how improving market transparency can increase liquidity and reduce transaction costs, a theme that is central to the proposed ﬁxed income
market reforms after the ﬁnancial crises. Further, improving market transparency can help
alleviate the pressure of bank capital regulations on market liquidity.8 While most existing
studies take market structure as given, we argue that incorporating the endogenous shifts
of intermediation structure is vital for understanding the overall impact on liquidity and
welfare.

A Model of Trading Intermediation
In this section, we provide a model of trading intermediation. We then use the model
to analyze how market transparency, trading turnover, and capital regulation aﬀect the
equilibrium intermediation, market liquidity, and welfare.
2.1
Model Setup
There are two types of agents, risk-neutral sellers and risk-neutral intermediaries, both
with discount rate e−ρ. A single asset is traded.
Trading times are discrete and denoted by the set T = {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}. At each time
t ∈T , N sellers receive liquidity shocks and each want to sell one unit of the asset. We
interpret N as market turnover: a larger N corresponds to a security with more naturally
occurring trading interest. Each seller realizes a private trading gain of v > 0 at the time of
successfully selling the asset. For now, we assume that v is a ﬁxed number, but later we will
consider the case when v follows a distribution.
Selling happens through intermediaries who can sell to an external market which refers
to trading venues such as exchanges, dark pools, or networks of over-the-counter trading
parties. Intermediaries execute trades with a delay of one period which can be interpreted as
the search time in the corporate bond and currency markets or the execution time of large
orders in the equity market. The true price at which the security can be sold on the market
follows an exogenous process {Pt}t∈T . The price increment (adjusted for expected growth)
Pt+1 −eρPt follows an i.i.d. normal distributions with mean 0 and volatility σ, and these
increments are independent of all other variables. As a result, the discounted price process
e−ρtPt is a martingale.

Trading Procedure.
Because all sellers are the same, we only discuss one seller who has
a trading need at time t. To avoid complexities in information structure, we assume the
seller contacts intermediaries for quote only at time t. We further make the simplifying assumption that intermediaries are fully competitive. This allows us to focus on the forces that
change the relative cost between principal and agency intermediation, and ﬁx the orthogonal
factor of competition which merely changes the distribution of proﬁts between sellers and
intermediaries.
The trading procedure is illustrated in Figure 3. The seller ﬁrst chooses among diﬀerent
intermediaries. Each intermediary then proposes both a principal contract, simply the price
at which the seller is willing to buy the asset, as well as an agency contract to be described
later. The seller decides whether to accept one of the intermediaries, and if so, chooses
between agency and principal intermediation.
The seller can costlessly contact multiple
intermediaries and thus engage them in full competition. By assumption, the intermediary
can sell the asset on the market at time t + 1 if the seller accepts the proposal.
If the
seller chooses agency intermediation, the intermediary helps the seller sell on the market
at time t + 1 and the seller holds inventory from t to t + 1. If the seller chooses principal
intermediation, the intermediary immediately buys from the seller at t, keeps inventory from
t to t + 1, and oﬄoads the position on the market at t + 1.

Agency and Principal Intermediation Contracts.
In an agency contract, the intermediary executes the sale at time t + 1, pockets the proceeds Pt+1, reports a selling price of
ˆPt+1 which does not have to be truthful, and then immediately pay the seller q(Xt+1, ˆPt+1)
for the asset. The intermediary has an incentive to report an execution price that maximizes
his own payoﬀ. Following Townsend (1979), this misreporting option is a convenient way to
capture the moral hazard problems in agency intermediation discussed in the introduction.13

crucially depends on the true price Pt+1. The monitoring cost cM > 0 captures the resources
that sellers need to expend to monitor their brokers.

In principal intermediation, intermediaries provide a quote Q at which they are willing to
buy the asset at time t. The chosen intermediary takes hold of the inventory immediately at
time t, and incurs an inventory cost cH > 0 per unit of holding time until oﬄoading the asset
at time t+1. This cost can be motivated by book-keeping costs and capital costs, where the
latter is aﬀected by capital regulations. The ineﬃciency of principal intermediation comes
entirely from this inventory cost. The full competition among intermediaries makes sure
that the equilibrium price quote Q equals the expected payoﬀfrom selling the asset plus the
inventory cost.
In principal intermediation, because the seller knows exactly the price quoted by intermediaries, there is no “misreporting problem”. The commonly discussed problem of dealer
price-gouging (excessive markups) in principal intermediation is not a moral hazard problem
per se, but rather the consequence of lacking competition. This is in contrast to agency
intermediation where the moral hazard arises from the fact that brokers do not commit to a
outcomes against the price benchmarks such as the volume-weighted average price.

2.3
The Eﬀect on Market Liquidity
We proceed to study how intermediation structure aﬀects market liquidity in equilibrium.
In general, the concept of market liquidity can involves both price impact and the speed of
transactions, and our model captures the latter. Thus, we interpret faster transaction for
sellers to reﬂect better market liquidity.
So far, we have focused on the choice between agency and principal intermediation. For
the purpose of studying liquidity, this is an incomplete picture: if intermediation friction is
too large, investors may choose to not use intermediaries and trade by themselves, which is
likely ineﬃcient, or even give up certain trading needs. To take this into account, we now
introduce “no intermediation” as an option for sellers: the seller can directly sell the asset
to the market at time t + k with delay k > 1, where k is allowed to pick up a non-integer
value for generality.17 While we call it “no intermediation”, in the extreme, if k becomes
large, this can also represent “no trading”. One such example is the corporate bond market
where investors rarely trade directly.
By assuming k > 1, we assert that intermediaries can trade more quickly (even if by a
tiny amount) than sellers. This is motivated by the very reason why intermediaries exist:
they can facilitate trading by using their expertise.

of capital regulation on market liquidity, such as improving market transparency, or bundling
bonds with similar credit and interest rate risks together to increase turnover within each security. This implication is highly relevant to the Treasury market dysfunction in March 2020
during the COVID-19 crisis. According to Duﬃe (2020), Treasury market depth dropped

Inventory cost shocks.
Motivated by the theoretical argument in Andersen, Duﬃe, and
Song (2019), we use dealer funding cost proxies to measure intermediary inventory costs.
We use two proxies, the LIBOR-OIS spread and the Markit North America Investment
Grade CDS index (CDX IG). As shown in the upper panel of Figure 6, there appears to be
three major spikes over the sample period. The ﬁrst spike occurs around the end of 2007,
when subprime mortgage default concerns become widely noticed. The second shock is in
September of 2008, when Lehman ﬁled for bankruptcy. The third, which is a smaller shock,
begins in late 2011, during the European sovereign debt crisis.
Measuring agency intermediation.
We measure agency intermediation by detecting
“riskless principal trading” in which dealers trade with a client after having arranged an
exactly oﬀsetting trade with another counterparty. Such paired trades occur almost instantaneously, so the intermediary takes no inventory risk and eﬀectively acts as a broker. Using
trading data from the Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine (TRACE), we classify two
trades as an agency pair if, following a trade in which a customer sells to a dealer, another
trade in which dealers sells to a customer occurs within the same minute in the same bond
(Zitzewitz, 2010; Ederington, Guan, and Yadav, 2014). To increase conﬁdence that we are
detecting prearranged paired trades, we also require the second trade to happen at a price
at or above the ﬁrst so that the intermediary makes non-negative proﬁts.19
Most identiﬁed pairs of agency trade happen during the same second. Because the median
corporate bond trades less than once a day (Edwards et al. (2007)), the chance of spuriously
identifying pairs is minuscule. We start the sample in 2006 due to pervasive bunched trade
reporting before 2006 causing spuriously high fractions of “matched pairs” (Appendix B.2).

Equities.
The U.S. equities market is transparent. All quotes and trades, including trades
on dark pools, are reported with accurate prices, share counts, and timestamps. These data
are made available through the consolidated tape association at a small cost to institutional
investors.23 Investors know the highest bid and lowest oﬀer available throughout the entire
market (national best bid and oﬀer). In this way, they can easily compute implementation
shortfalls and detect trade-throughs. Knowledge of the real-time trading volume allows investors to compute the commonly used volume-weighted average price benchmark (VWAP).
The equity market also enjoys high liquidity, as there are signiﬁcant turnover and frequent
updates of trades and quotes in most publicly traded stocks.
Consistent with theory predictions, most intermediated equity trading is conducted in
the agency style. Broker-dealers provide various agency services that fulﬁll diﬀerent client
needs.24 There is some principal intermediation in “upstairs” block trading, but that practice
is in decline. Investors also monitor broker execution quality by systematically comparing
realized execution prices to price benchmarks, a practice known as the “transaction cost
analysis” (TCA).25

Currencies.
Foreign exchange is a prime example of instruments with high liquidity, but

4
Summary
In this paper, we document and explain the variations in trading intermediation and
study its implication on market liquidity. Speciﬁcally, we study agency intermediation, in
which intermediaries work as brokers and earn commissions, and principal intermediation, in
which intermediaries work as dealers and take on inventory risk. Large heterogeneity exists
in the intermediation structure across markets and over time.
We show that the structure of trading intermediation is determined by the trade-oﬀ
between the cost of monitoring brokers and the cost of compensating dealer for holding
inventory. Agency intermediation is more likely chosen when the market is more transparent,
if the security is heavily traded so that computing a benchmark becomes easier, and if dealer
inventory cost is larger (potentially due to tighter capital regulation).
The empirical evidence is consistent with our predictions. Indeed, in transparent and
high-turnover markets such as equities, agency intermediation has higher usage. On the
contrary, opaque markets such as foreign exchange, and illiquid markets such as the corporate
bond market, are dominated by principal intermediation. We also present evidence for the
role of intermediary inventory cost using shocks to corporate bond dealer balance sheets.
When dealer inventory cost increases, the fraction of agency intermediation also spikes as
predicted by our theory.
Our results bear important implications for market liquidity and welfare. We ﬁnd that
improving market transparency can counteract the adverse impact of tighter capital regulation on market liquidity. Furthermore, tighter capital regulations may actually improve
the welfare for market participants if the ineﬃciency involved in principal intermediation is
suﬃciently large. In summary, while most studies take intermediation structures as given,
we argue that taking into account the endogenous change in intermediation structure is vital

B.2
Trade bunching before 2006
We ﬁnd clear evidence of bunching in trade reporting before 2006. For each year, we
look at the distribution of trades by the reported execution minute of the hour, that is, the
“mm” part of each “hh:mm”. The corporate bond market is over-the-counter and does not
have market open or closing times, so there is no reason to expect trades to be concentrated
in particular minutes of the hour. However, before 2006, there are clear spikes at “round
minutes”, such as hh:00, hh:30, etc, shown in the left panel of Figure 9. As expected, we
do see spuriously high ratio of agency trading around the bunched times in right panel of
