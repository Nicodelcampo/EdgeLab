Quantitative Trading Model with Deep Learning

Abstract
Financial markets are inherently dynamic and exhibit complex temporal patterns
that standard feature engineering often fails to capture. This paper investigates the
effectiveness of path signature transforms in representing high-frequency financial
market data for directional trading predictions. Using 5-minute interval NASDAQ
data spanning five years, six standard technical indicators are transformed into path
signatures of varying truncation orders and fed into a feedforward neural network
to predict short, neutral, and long trading signals. Performance is benchmarked
against a baseline model using raw features, assessing both predictive accuracy
and real-world trading outcomes. Results show that low-order signatures (orders
2–3) efficiently encode temporal and sequential dependencies, improving directional
prediction accuracy and model robustness, while higher-order signatures introduce
noise that can degrade generalization. Further analysis using visualization techniques confirms that signature features capture the evolving geometry of price and
volume more effectively than traditional features.
These findings highlight path
signatures as a compact, expressive, and practically valuable representation, offering a powerful tool for high-frequency financial modeling and algorithmic trading
strategies.
Keywords: Rough Path Theory, Deep Learning, Intraday Trading, Feature Engineering

1

A key obstacle in modeling financial time series data is transforming irregular, noisy
and often non-Euclidean market paths P : [0, T] →Rd. Classical approaches often assume
time series data to be stationary, i.e., Pt ∼Pt+τ where τ is lag; however, most financial
data are non-stationary and noisy, mostly relying on handcrafted features ϕ(Pt), which
may fail to capture higher-order interactions.
Recent developments in rough path theory and signature transforms S(P) address this
limitation through a mathematical tool that converts raw price movements into structured
financial fingerprints, which preserve the full story of market patterns such as volatility
clustering and directional changes [Lyons, 2014]. However, using this rich, leveraged, and
structured representation for traditional deep learning models y = fθ(x) = σ(Wx + b)
creates challenges. These models are designed for flat, Euclidean data Rn, so they are
not well-suited for irregular, noisy, and often non-Euclidean data [Bronstein et al., 2017].
Signature transforms map a multivariate time series P : [0, T] →Rd into a structured
feature space S(P) ∈Rn where higher-order interactions and time-order dependencies
are preserved. This transformation captures complex temporal patterns that handcrafted
features often miss, such as cumulative path effects and interaction terms across price
and volume dimensions [Lyons, 2014, Kidger et al., 2020b].
Although signature methods have previously been explored for time series forecasting
[Arribas et al., 2022], their application in real-time trading, particularly for generating
directional signals in noisy intraday environments, remains largely underexplored. This
study presents a comparative framework: using an identical deep learning architecture,
we train two models, one on traditional engineered features, the other on signaturetransformed inputs. The goal is to assess whether the signature-based model can generate
more reliable long/short signals, leading to stronger risk-adjusted returns and reduced
drawdowns, especially in periods of heightened market volatility.

Literature Review
2.1
Traditional Trading Strategies
Classical financial models are often based on factor and momentum strategies to capture
long-term or short-term alpha. The Fama-French three-factor model Fama and French
[1992] extends the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) by modeling excess returns Rt
as a function of market, size, and value factors:
Rt −Rf = α + β1(RM −Rf) + β2 · SMB + β3 · HML + ϵt
(6)
where RM is market return, SMB is small-minus-big (size premium), HML is highminus-low (value premium) and ϵt is residual noise. This model assumes linear dependence, constant betas, and normally distributed residuals. However, during regime shifts
or crisis periods, empirical evidence shows that factor loadings βi become time-varying
and volatility increases abruptly, violating the independently and identically distributed
(i.i.d.) noise assumption Cont [2001].
To adjust for such dynamics, some extensions use rolling regressions or state-dependent
switching models, but still lack memory of how prices evolved over time, a limitation tied
to their scalar, point-based view of returns. A complementary class of models is timeseries momentum (TSMOM), which generates trading signals based on the sign of lagged
price differences:

2.2
Machine Learning in Trading
Traditional deep learning models, such as feed-forward neural networks, learn mappings
from vectorized input features to outputs via compositions of affine transforms and nonlinearities. Given a sequence of inputs x = (xt−k, . . . , xt), the LSTM computes a hidden
state ht via recursive nonlinear transformations:
ht = LSTM(xt, ht−1)
(9)
Unrolling this recursion for k steps:
ht = LSTMt ◦LSTMt−1 ◦· · · ◦LSTMt−k+1(xt−k, ht−k−1)
(10)
The predicted next value ˆPt+1 is obtained by passing ht through a feed-forward mapping f:
ˆPt+1 = f(ht) = σ(Wht + b)
(11)
where W ∈R1×m, b ∈R and σ is an output activation (e.g., linear or sigmoid).
While effective for Euclidean-structured data, these models are limited when applied to
financial time series that exhibit complex, non-Euclidean, and path-dependent structures.
Similarly, transformer-based models have gained traction in high-frequency financial applications Lim et al. [2021], offering attention-based mechanisms to capture temporal
dependencies. However, they also assume discrete sampling and Euclidean vector spaces,
overlooking the path-dependent and algebraic structure of market trajectories.
2.3
Signature Transforms in Finance
Traditional statistical and deep learning models often rely on vectorized input representations or windowed features, which can obscure the rich temporal structure of financial time
series [Cont, 2001]. In contrast, signature transforms provide a pathwise, coordinate-free
method to encode sequential data, preserving the order and interaction of events along
the trajectory. Rooted in rough path theory [Lyons, 2014], the signature transform offers
a mathematically principled approach to representing data streams and has emerged as
a promising tool in quantitative finance.

2.4
Deep Learning in Financial Time Series Modeling
Deep learning models have become increasingly popular in financial time series analysis
due to their ability to learn complex, nonlinear patterns from high-dimensional data
Goodfellow et al. [2016]. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly Long ShortTerm Memory (LSTM) networks, are widely used to capture temporal dependencies by
maintaining a hidden state ht updated recursively:
ht = LSTM(xt, ht−1)
(14)
where xt is the input vector at time t. By unrolling over k steps, the hidden state
aggregates information from past inputs, allowing prediction of future values or classification tasks:
ˆyt = fθ(ht) = σ(Wht + b)
(15)
with W, b as learnable parameters and σ an activation function. Transformer architectures further improve temporal modeling by using attention mechanisms Vaswani et al.
[2017], Lim et al. [2021], but both approaches typically operate on vectorized, discretetime inputs and assume Euclidean structure, limiting their ability to model continuous,
path-dependent market behavior.
To overcome this, recent studies have explored combining signature transforms with
machine learning. Signature features provide a compact yet expressive representation of
a path’s evolution through iterated integrals, preserving order, interaction, and structure
[Lyons, 2014]. Empirical work supports this approach: [Kalsi et al., 2020] showed that
signature-based models outperformed traditional methods in high-frequency execution
strategies, capturing volatility bursts and price impact. [Kidger et al., 2020b] extended
this into streaming setups using Neural Controlled Differential Equations, although those
models remain more complex and less practical for direct trading signal generation.
Instead, our approach focuses on combining truncated signature features with a standard deep feedforward network:
ˆyt = fθ(S(m)(Pt)) = σ(WS(m)(Pt) + b)
(16)
where S(m)(Pt) ∈Rn is the m-th order signature of the input path segment Pt, and
the output ˆyt ∈{0, 1, 2} denotes short, neutral, or long signal classes.
This setup is simple, trainable, and scalable, while still benefiting from the structurepreserving power of signature transforms. Unlike prior work that focused on forecasting,
we directly generate actionable directional signals for trading and compare performance
against models trained on raw engineered features.

Methodology
3.1
Research Design
This study follows a quantitative and analytical research design, assuming that financial market behavior can be objectively measured and modeled. The research adopts a
deductive approach, starting with the hypothesis that signature transforms can capture
meaningful market path structures useful for trading [Lyons, 2014]. Historical OHLCV
5

data is used to construct sliding path windows, which are transformed into fixed-length
vectors using truncated signature transforms (order 2, 3 and 4).
These vectors are processed using a Deep Learning (DL) model to classify path regimes
such as uptrend, downtrend and consolidation Bronstein et al. [2021].
Based on the
regime, the strategy decides whether to enter a long-only trade. The performance of the
model is evaluated through backtesting using metrics such as Sharpe ratio and maximum
drawdown Lo [2002], Glasserman [2004].The study uses Python with the following key libraries: pandas, numpy, signatory, PyTorch, scikit-learn, matplotlib, and seaborn
for data processing, modeling, and evaluation.
3.2
Data
This research uses 5-minute OHLCV data (Open, High, Low, Close, Volume) for the
NASDAQ-100. The datasets cover the period from August 11, 2019 to August 9, 2024.
The data, taken from Kaggle Elkhirani [2024], includes about 375,000 time-stamped
records from regular US trading hours (09:30 AM to 04:00 PM EST). Also, for backtesting
and performance evaluation out of sample, data from April 8, 2025 to July 3, 2025 are
used. Each row represents a 5-minute candlestick with price and volume information.
The dataset was cleaned and prepared in Python by converting timestamps to the
correct datetime format, sorting them in order, and removing missing values, duplicates,
and data outside of market hours. This clean, time-ordered dataset is then used to create
rolling windows, apply signature transforms, and train geometric deep learning models to
spot market patterns and generate trading signals. Because the data are high-frequency
and span several years, it allows for thorough testing in different market conditions.

3.5
Trading Strategy Design
The trading signals are generated by a neural network model trained on signature features
that outputs a discrete classification
f(Xt) ∈{1, 0, 2},
(37)
corresponding to short, neutral, and long signals respectively. A trade is initiated only
when f(Xt)̸ = 0, i.e., the model signals either a long or short position. To avoid overtrading, a cooldown period of one bar (5 minutes) is enforced after each trade exit.
Position sizing is based on 0.5% of the last closed equity, ensuring consistent exposure
while accounting for compounding performance. The strategy uses a reward-to-risk ratio
of 1 : 1.4, with stop-loss and take-profit levels defined as:
PSL = P0 −R,
PTP = P0 + 1.4 R,
(38)
where P0 is the entry price, and R is the risk per trade (equal to 0.5% of capital in dollar
terms). A position is only entered if the current cash allows full execution based on this
calculated risk.
The equity curve Et evolves according to:

with initial equity normalized to 1.

These findings are consistent
with prior studies indicating that path signature features, rooted in rough path theory,
provide a mathematically principled representation of sequential data, effectively capturing the underlying structure of financial time series [Kidger et al., 2020b, Lyons, 2014].
The results support the use of signature features in intraday models, particularly when

4.7
Findings and Discussion
The analysis of signature-based feature transformations provides several compelling insights into their practical use in intraday financial modeling and algorithmic trading.
Looking at Table 4 and Figures 7, 6, and 5, a clear pattern emerges: simply achieving
higher predictive accuracy or increasing feature complexity does not automatically translate into superior trading outcomes. In fact, while higher-order signature features (Orders
3 and 4) sometimes improve standard classification metrics such as class-level discriminability, they also introduce substantial noise in the context of market microstructure.
This noise manifests as declining Sharpe ratios, larger drawdowns, and occasionally negative total returns [Kidger et al., 2020b, Lyons, 2014, Morrill et al., 2020]. For example, our fourth-order signature model produced a total return of -4.51% and a negative
Sharpe ratio of -0.59, despite achieving moderate classification accuracy. These results
suggest that pushing feature complexity too far can lead to overfitting and amplify noninformative fluctuations, especially in high-frequency trading environments where microstructure noise is pervasive.
In contrast, second-order signature features consistently delivered the strongest and
most reliable trading performance across all evaluated metrics. The second-order model
reached a final equity of 1.2214, corresponding to a 22.14% total return, with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 2.82 and a relatively modest maximum drawdown of -7.75%. This
robust performance was primarily driven by long trades, which showed higher win rates
and cumulative profits (62.3% win rate with 20.30% PnL). These observations indicate
that lower-order signatures effectively capture the critical temporal and path-dependent
structures present in intraday price movements, preserving essential market information
while avoiding the noise amplification seen in higher-order transformations. Essentially,
this demonstrates that careful selection of truncation depth is crucial: it ensures that
models retain meaningful patterns without being distracted by random, high-frequency
fluctuations.
Raw features, though sometimes producing higher classification accuracy or slightly
better macro-average F1-scores, consistently underperformed in actual trading scenarios. Specifically, raw features yielded only a 1.92% total return with a Sharpe ratio of
0.46 and a maximum drawdown of -8.71%. This stark contrast reinforces an important
18

point: standard predictive metrics alone are insufficient for evaluating trading strategies,
because they fail to capture the temporal dependencies and path-dependent behaviors
that drive profitable intraday trading [Kidger et al., 2020a, Lyons, 2014]. Supporting
this, the t-SNE visualizations in Figure 6 reveal that higher-order signature features tend
to compress the feature space, diminishing the separability of actionable trading signals.
In comparison, raw features maintain cluster separation but lack the richer structural
information encoded by signature transforms, limiting their practical utility.
Further evidence comes from the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Area
Under the Curve (AUC) analyses. While higher-order signatures occasionally show minor improvements in class separability for certain categories, these gains do not translate into better trading performance. This decoupling highlights a critical insight for
high-frequency or intraday market applications: preserving temporal dynamics and pathdependent structures is far more important than marginal gains in predictive metrics
[Lyons, 2014, Morrill et al., 2020].
Overall, these findings strongly suggest that signature-based feature transformations
offer a tangible advantage in algorithmic trading, particularly when applied with an optimal truncation depth. Second-order signatures strike the right balance, capturing meaningful market dynamics while avoiding overengineering or the noise inherent in higherorder features.
This confirms that the signature method is not only a powerful tool
for extracting features from complex multivariate time series but also provides practical
benefits for designing trading systems. These results align with prior research, which emphasizes the robustness of signature methods for sequential data while cautioning against
excessive complexity that can degrade out-of-sample performance [Kidger et al., 2020b,
Lyons, 2014, Morrill et al., 2020, Tóth et al., 2023].
5
Conclusion
This study shows that path signature features offer a clear advantage in intraday trading
over traditional raw features. Second-order signatures consistently preserved essential
temporal and path-dependent patterns, achieving better risk-adjusted returns and directional consistency without amplifying noise. Higher-order signatures (Order 3 and 4),
while sometimes improving classification metrics, often introduced excessive noise, reducing Sharpe ratios and increasing drawdowns [Kidger et al., 2020b, Lyons, 2014, Morrill
et al., 2020]. The results highlight that high predictive accuracy alone does not guarantee
profitable trading. Signature transforms capture richer sequential information, enabling
models to exploit intraday structures more effectively than raw features [Lyons, 2014,
Morrill et al., 2020, Tóth et al., 2023].
Overall, signature-based features provide a robust and interpretable framework for
modeling financial time series, balancing complexity and performance. Future research
could explore combining them with attention-based architectures or adaptive learning to
better capture evolving market dynamics.
