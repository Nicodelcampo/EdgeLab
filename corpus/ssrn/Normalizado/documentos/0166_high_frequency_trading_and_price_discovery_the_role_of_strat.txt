High-Frequency Trading and Price Discovery:
The Role of Strategic Runs⋆
Tino Cestonaroa,1
aGoethe University Frankfurt, Theodor-W.-Adorno-Platz 4, 60323 Frankfurt, Germany
Abstract
Strategic runs are an outcome of high-frequency trading strategies that lead to alternating
cycles of order submissions and cancellations within a short time interval. Examining granular limit order book data, we ﬁnd that submissions and cancellations resulting from these
runs have less permanent price impact and contribute less to price discovery than similar
trading activities not part of a strategic run. Once other market participants can recognize
that an order belongs to a strategic run, its contribution to price discovery diminishes signiﬁcantly. Our ﬁndings indicate that such high-frequency trading activities do not enhance
price discovery but rather dilute it by triggering a substantial number of orders with limited
informational value.
Keywords:
High-frequency trading, strategic runs, price discovery, price impact, limit
order book

1. Introduction
For more than a decade, high-frequency trading (HFT) has become a dominant aspect
of equity markets, profoundly impacting them in various ways. According to the European
Securities and Markets Authority (2021) (ESMA), HFT activity accounts for approximately
60% of the total volume traded in European equity markets. Despite HFT being a wellestablished practice in equity markets, its implications for market quality remain a subject
of ongoing debate in the literature. Contrary to the ﬁrst beliefs, the early empirical literature
has demonstrated that HFT can have a positive impact on market quality. Studies of Carrion
(2013) and Brogaard et al. (2014) provide evidence of the beneﬁcial impact of HFT activity
on price discovery and price eﬃciency. Moreover, the literature also ﬁnds positive liquidity
eﬀects due to HFT (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013; Brogaard and Garriott, 2019). However, these
studies are contrasted by various others demonstrating HFT’s negative impact on equity
markets. Van Kervel and Menkveld (2019) and Hirschey (2021) show that high-frequency
traders are able to detect informed order ﬂow of high-frequency traders and exploit this
using their speed advantage at the cost of slower traders. Furthermore, Shkilko and Sokolov
(2020) and Goldstein et al. (2023) provide evidence that the speed advantage of the fastest
high-frequency traders is associated with higher trading costs and the crowding out of slower
traders from the limit order book.
The recent literature demonstrating the negative eﬀects of HFT suggests that at least
some activities of these traders might not be beneﬁcial for certain aspects of market quality.
In this study, we aim to contribute to this discussion by analyzing a speciﬁc outcome of HFT
activity known as "strategic runs" and examining its impact on price discovery. Strategic
2

runs are a widespread phenomenon that accounts for a large fraction of all limit order
book activity and predominantly results from HFT strategies rather than agency trading
(Chakrabarty et al., 2023). The original deﬁnition of strategic runs (hereafter also referred
to as runs) dates back to Hasbrouck and Saar (2013), who deﬁne runs as a sequence of linked
order book events. According to the authors, a run starts with an order submission, followed
by a cancellation of that order. Within a very short time interval, the algorithm resubmits
the order on the same side and with the same order size. This sequence of order cancellations
and resubmissions on the same side and with the same quantity continues in an alternating
order until the initial order is fully executed or ﬁnally cancelled. While the algorithmic
strategy and its parameters remain unobservable, the resulting order ﬂow suggests that the
rapid submissions and cancellations aim to trigger interactions with other trading algorithms
(Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013).
Recent theoretical models on limit order markets provide only a few explanatory approaches to the phenomenon of strategic runs. Contrary to the traditional models, such as
Glosten and Milgrom (1985), that consider market orders as the central source of information
utilized by informed traders, new models highlight that both market and limit orders are
used by uninformed as well as informed investors (Goettler et al., 2009; Riccó et al., 2020;
Roşu, 2020). This implies that the limit price of a limit order may reﬂect the uninformed
investor’s belief about the true value of the asset or the true value of the asset observable by
the informed investor. While these models provide useful insights, the model of Bhattacharya
and Saar (2021) is the ﬁrst that provides an explanation for the frequent revisions, i.e. numerous cancellations followed by resubmissions, that characterize a strategic run and are a
stylized fact in limit order book markets. According to Bhattacharya and Saar (2021), infor3

mation asymmetry in a limit order book market leads to frequent revisions, as uninformed
investors adjust their beliefs in accordance with the information conveyed by the order ﬂow.
This behaviour is fostered by the tremendous advancements in trading technology and the
associated reduction in costs for monitoring, cancellation, and resubmission. The theoretical
derivation is also in line with the empirical ﬁndings of Van Kervel and Menkveld (2019) who
show that high-frequency traders learn from the informed order ﬂow of institutional investors
and frequently revise their orders accordingly.
While the model of Bhattacharya and Saar (2021) provides an explanation for the occurrence of strategic runs, it does not explain all patterns in those runs. In our data sample,
we observe that almost half of all cancellations are followed by a limit order submission with
an unchanged price limit.2 As the revision of a limit order might lead to the loss of time
priority, it is not likely that such a resubmission comes from the same algorithm updating its
bliefs. It appears more likely that such a resubmission comes from another HFT algorithm
that was triggered by the cancellation. This aligns more to the hypothesis from the initial
paper of Hasbrouck and Saar (2013) that those observations are the outcome of multiple
trading algorithms interacting with each other.
Putting both explanations together, the linked order book events within a strategic run
may result from both high-frequency traders updating their beliefs and multiple algorithms
reacting to each other’s actions. Note that both actions convey limited information to the
market. While updating beliefs only relates to processing an existing information in the
2In the original paper from Hasbrouck and Saar (2013), they ﬁnd the same pattern of unchanged price
limits in the resubmitted order, but they do not report any numbers regarding the frequency of this sequence.
4

observed order ﬂow, the attempt to trigger other algorithms rather represents a search for
information than providing new information to the market.
As a consequence, we suppose that the informativeness of the order ﬂow generated by
strategic runs is lower than non-strategic run order ﬂow, which partially consists of informed
order ﬂow.
Moreover, we conjecture that a resubmission with an unchanged price limit
provides less information than a resubmission with an updated price limit, as the former
suggests that multiple algorithms trying to trigger each other, while the latter is more related
to high-frequency traders updating their beliefs in accordance with detected informed order
ﬂow.
To shed light into the informational value of strategic run order ﬂow, we examine the
permanent price impact and the contribution to price discovery of limit order book events
associated with strategic runs. Speciﬁcally, we investigate empirically whether limit order
book events that are part of a strategic run diﬀer in terms of price impact and their contribution to price discovery from limit order book events that are not generated by a strategic
run. Due to the widespread awareness of the phenomenon of strategic runs, we suppose that
the contribution to price discovery of limit order book events declines when market participants are able to recognize that the event is part of a strategic run. Moreover, we examine
whether changes in the aggressiveness of the resubmitted orders provide new information
to the market and thus aﬀect the permanent price impact and the contribution to price
discovery of these limit order book events.
Our results show that limit order book events associated with strategic runs have less
price impact and contribute relatively less to price discovery compared to non-strategic run
events. Furthermore, we ﬁnd that the ﬁrst two events of a strategic run are more relevant for
5

price discovery than subsequent events in the run. Both results suggest that limit order book
events triggered by a strategic run are less informative than non-strategic run events. The
contribution to price discovery decreases signiﬁcantly as soon as other market participants
can recognize the strategic run. In addition, we observe that resubmissions within a strategic
run that increase its aggressiveness have a higher price impact than resubmissions that do not
alter the run’s aggressiveness. Considering a decreasing aggressiveness within a run, we ﬁnd
for two out of three event categories that the price impact is lower than for resubmissions
that do not alter the aggressiveness.
Since the price impact is higher for increases and
lower for decreases, this suggests that high-frequency traders are adjusting their orders in
line with future midpoint movements. This aligns with empirical ﬁndings of Van Kervel
and Menkveld (2019) and the theory of Bhattacharya and Saar (2021) indicating that highfrequency traders are able to convey information from observed informed order ﬂow and
update their beliefs accordingly. Thereby, these updates in terms of run’s aggressiveness
provide more informational value and contribute more to price discovery than resubmissions
that do not alter run’s aggressiveness.
Our study enriches two research streams in the existing literature. On the one hand,
our ﬁndings contribute to the mentioned literature on the impact of HFT on market quality. We provide further evidence that certain activities of high-frequency traders do not
improve price discovery, but rather dilute it by submitting a substantial number of messages
to equity markets that contain limited informational value. Speciﬁcally, revisions that do
not alter the aggressiveness of a strategic run exhibit a low signal-to-noise ratio, calling into
question the economic value of this order ﬂow. On the other hand, our evidence contributes
to the growing body of research on price discovery at the level of individual limit order
6

book events. For instance, Brogaard et al. (2019) demonstrate the relevance of non-trading
activity, such as limit order submissions and cancellations, for price discovery, while distinguishing also between HFT and non-HFT activity. Following up, Brugler and Hendershott
(2023) disentangle HFT’s contribution to price discovery regarding the source of information. Additionally, Kwan et al. (2022) show that not only do the attributes of the limit order
book event itself, such as aggressiveness and order size, determine the event’s contribution
to price discovery, but the state of the limit order book, including factors like bid-ask spread
and order book imbalance, also plays a crucial role. Adding to this literature, our study
provides evidence that, besides the state of the limit order book and the explicit attributes
of the limit order book event, implicit attributes also matter, such as the interconnection
between multiple events and the attributes of these events. In particular, we are the ﬁrst to
show that limit order book events linked through a strategic run, which essentially repeat
the same signal, tend to provide less valuable information to the market. However, these
events become more informative if they alter the aggressiveness of the strategic run.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses the data used
in this study, the identiﬁcation of strategic runs, and the applied methodology to estimate
price impact and the contribution to price discovery of a limit order book event. Afterwards,
we report the results of this study in Section 3. Section 4 concludes.
2. Data and Methodology
2.1. Data
In this study, we rely on message data from Deutsche Börse’s A7 Analytics Platform.
The data sample consists of the 10 largest German stocks in terms of trading volume traded

on XETRA. The sample period spans from January to March 2023. Market statistics such as
trading volume, relative spread, and volatility for the 10 stocks in the sample are presented in
Table A.1 in the Appendix. Furthermore, the table shows the average number of messages,
market orders, limit orders, and cancellations per trading day. The average relative spread of
2.60 to 3.67 basis points (bps) and the large number of messages suggest that these stocks are
highly liquid instruments that are frequently traded. Furthermore, according to Chakrabarty
et al. (2023), the number of messages and the number of cancellations highly correlate with
algorithmic and HFT activity. The average number of messages and cancellations per stockday indicate that the stocks in the sample are actively traded stocks where large fractions
of order ﬂow are generated by algorithmic and high-frequency traders.3
The data itself consists of messages each describing the incremental change to the limit
order book. Among other things, each message includes a template ID describing the message
type (such as order submission, order cancellation, order modiﬁcation), the associated side
of the limit order book (buy or sell), its price limit and quantity, and two timestamps, both
with nanosecond precision: a priority timestamp associated with the order, and a timestamp
for the limit order book observation. Using the priority timestamp, we can link an order
cancellation with its initial submission. However, the data does not come with trader IDs.
This means we cannot track whether two order submissions are generated by the same trader.

2.2. Strategic runs
Our deﬁnition of a strategic run aligns with the original deﬁnition proposed by Hasbrouck
and Saar (2013). In their study, the authors deﬁne a strategic run as a sequence of linked
limit order book events.
A run begins with an order submission, which is subsequently
canceled. This is followed by further submissions and subsequent cancellations, all on the
same side of the book and with the same quantity as the initial submission. The outcome
is an alternating cycle of limit order submissions and cancellations until the quantity of the
initial order is either ﬁnally executed or deﬁnitively canceled. An example of a strategic run
in our data sample is provided in Table A.2 in the Appendix. While we cannot observe the
cause of these runs, it is likely that these runs are the result of algorithmic trading strategies
4To validate our order book reconstruction, we compare it with the oﬃcial limit order book time series
data from XETRA.
9

by high-frequency traders attempting to trigger or undercut other trading algorithms in the
market (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013).
To identify strategic runs in our data sample, we follow the approach of Hasbrouck and
Saar (2013). We iterate through each cancellation in the message stream and verify whether
it is followed by a subsequent limit or market order. We consider the initial submission, the
cancellation, and the subsequent resubmission as part of a strategic run if the resubmission is
(1) on the same side, (2) submitted within 100ms after the cancellation, and (3) has (almost)
the same quantity.5 If the resubmission is a market order that is fully executed or there is
no resubmission after 100ms, the strategic run ends. When a resubmission is only partially
executed, we follow the remaining quantity using the same principle. Messages that are
already assigned to a strategic run cannot be linked to other runs. Finally, we divide our
sample in runs that consist of 3 to 9 events and runs that have 10 or more events. In line
with the original study (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013) and other studies applying the same
algorithm to more recent data samples (Chakrabarty et al., 2023), we predominantly focus
on the latter group. The probability of 10 or more events with identical attributes randomly
occurring within a short time period is very low. Hence, it is highly probable that these
events are part of a strategic run and are accurately identiﬁed by the algorithm.

2.4. Estimating price impact and contribution to price discovery
Hasbrouck (1991a) introduced a structural vectorautoregression (SVAR) model to estimate the price impact and the contribution to price discovery of trades. This model is
extended by Fleming et al. (2018), allowing to estimate the eﬀects of other trading activities such as limit order submissions and cancellations, while controlling for the diﬀerence
between buy- and sell-sided activities. The extended approach is used by previous studies
that examine the contribution of market orders, limit orders, and cancellations to price discovery at the high-frequency level (e.g., Brogaard et al., 2019; Chaboud et al., 2021; Brugler
and Hendershott, 2023). We also follow this approach by estimating a model in the same
way, but analyzing the diﬀerences between trading activities that are part of a short or long
strategic run and those that are not. Speciﬁcally, we estimate the following model for each
stock-day in our sample:

Moreover, in the model, ωt is the structural innovation vector, while matrix B0 represents
the contemporaneous eﬀects among the variables in the system. We identify the model by
allowing for contemporaneous aﬀects in the return equation only. This identiﬁcation strategy

improving, or cancellation worsening the midpoint can instantaneously shift it by consuming,
improving, or deleting the current BBO, respectively. In line with Fleming et al. (2018) and
Brogaard et al. (2019), we estimate the SVAR in event time, while using ﬁve lags. Finally,
we exclude all asset-days where at least one dummy variable has less than 10 observations.

impulse response functions (IRFs). The main purpose of IRFs is to describe how the variables
react over time to a shock in one (or more) variable(s) in the system, making them useful
for tracing the transmission of shocks up to time horizon t + h. As we are predominantly
interested how each order book event impacts the midpoint, we calculate IRFs to examine
the reaction of rt with the impulses being one unit in one dummy per IRF. We set the

time horizon h to 20 order book events after the impulse. This value is derived based on
graphical analyses of multiple asset-days and is also in line with the value used by Brogaard
et al. (2019). In the results section, we report the mean of the IRF estimates per asset-day.
We use standard errors clustered at the asset-day level as introduced by Thompson (2011)
to determine whether an estimate diﬀers signiﬁcantly from zero or whether two estimates
signiﬁcantly diﬀer from each other at the 95% and 99% level, respectively.
In addition to the IRFs, we also calculate the information shares measure proposed by
Hasbrouck (1991b) that is based on the decomposition of the variance of the eﬃcient price.
The information shares complement the permanent price impact estimates of the IRFs by
weighting the price impact with the relative occurrence of the event. The result is an estimate
in percentage points suggesting how much of the midpoint innovation is explained by the
corresponding order book event.
Again, we report the mean of the estimates across all
asset-days in the results section.

Generally, the more aggressive the event, the stronger the price impact. While market orders
have the strongest price impact, reaching up to 0.848 bps, midpoint-improving submissions
and midpoint-worsening cancellations lead to price impacts of up to 0.603 and 0.550 bps,
respectively. In contrast, activities at the BBO have a price impact of up to 0.096 bps. Limit
orders two or more ticks away from the BBO have only a marginal price impact in the long
run with estimates of up to 0.016 bps. These estimates align with previous literature but
are in the lower bound of estimates (e.g., Brogaard et al., 2019; Kwan et al., 2022). The
18

relatively low values of our price impact estimates might be attributable to the fact that our
sample contains substantially more messages per asset-day than the samples used in other
studies such as Brogaard et al. (2019), potentially reducing the informativeness and, thus,
the price impact of any single event on average.
The diﬀerences between non-strategic run activity and strategic run activity are presented
in the second-to-last column (I-III). The results show that the permanent price impact of
non-strategic run events is signiﬁcantly larger than the impact of strategic run events for
almost every event type. While the diﬀerence in terms of price impact is 0.296 bps between
market orders, it is 0.326 bps for improving limit orders. Order cancellations exhibit similar
diﬀerences as submissions. A cancellation at the BBO triggered by a strategic run has a
price impact of 0.051 bps, whereas a cancellation at the BBO not generated by a strategic
run impacts the price by 0.096 bps.
Generally, the diﬀerences in price impact between
non-strategic run and strategic run events are relatively large and economically meaningful,
especially for aggressive events. For instance, the price impact of an improving limit order is
more than twice as high if it is not part of a strategic run. Regarding events tackling deeper
levels of the limit order book, the positive diﬀerences become less signiﬁcant.
Consistently with those results, we observe a signiﬁcant and positive diﬀerence for almost
every event type when comparing non-strategic run to short-run activity (I-II). Comparing
these events, we ﬁnd for all events except for limit order submissions at the BBO that
short-run activity has less price impact than non-strategic run activity. Overall, our results
suggest that an order book event with the same level of aggressiveness has less permanent
price impact when it is part of a strategic run, which indicates that these events possess
signiﬁcantly lower informational value. This may be because some of the high-frequency
19

traders’ information and beliefs were already conveyed to the market by preceding orders
that were part of the run. Generally, the results show that the linkage between multiple
events signiﬁcantly aﬀects the price impact of individual events.
Furthermore, for some activities we estimate a negative price impact. However, given
the signed event dummies, we would expect that each event has a (signiﬁcant) positive price
impact. For instance, our results suggest that a buy order that is part of a long strategic run
and has a price limit of two or more ticks worse than the BBO, reduces the midpoint by 0.007
bps in the long run. While this result seems to be counterintuitive at ﬁrst, because the buy
order is increasing demand, it aligns with the empirical literature. Brogaard et al. (2019)
interpret this negative price impact as the capability of high-frequency traders to predict
the next midpoint move, and act accordingly to gain time priority at the associated price
levels. For instance, a high-frequency trader predicting an upward move of the midpoint
may submit a sell order one tick away from the best oﬀer (which has a negative price impact
in SR 3 to 9) to gain a high time priority at the new best oﬀer after the upward move of the
midpoint. Supporting this interpretation, we ﬁnd negative price impact prevalent only in
those events that are triggered by high-frequency traders (i.e., short and long runs). Overall,
this "counterintuitive price impact" aligns with the ambiguous informational value provided
by strategic-run generated order ﬂow.

Previous studies show that the price impact of an order book event depends not only on
its aggressiveness, but also on other event characteristics, as well as the state of the limit
order book (Kwan et al., 2022). This raises the concern that the diﬀerences in price impact
and contribution to price discovery between non-strategic and strategic run events may be
attributable to other characteristics that systematically diﬀer between these subgroups. For
instance, orders that are part of a strategic run may be systematically lower in size, and
thus have lower price impact. To analyze whether the diﬀerences between non-strategic run
and strategic run activity are not driven by other factors, we repeat the previous analysis,
but split the classes along other dimensions that are known to aﬀect the price impact of an
event. Speciﬁcally, Kwan et al. (2022) demonstrate that order size as well as order book
imbalance are key determinants of the price impact of an event. As a consequence, we use
these two attributes to split the events into further subclasses.
In particular, we apply the following approach when estimating each model asset-day
wise. First, we discretize both attributes making them an additional dimension that we can
use to further divide the events. Speciﬁcally, we calculate the tertiles across all messages of
22

the order book imbalance and the order size associated with each message. Afterwards, we
are able to split the events along four dimensions: aggressiveness, strategic run type, order
book imbalance, and order size. This leads to 243 potential classes.6 However, in an SVAR,
the number of coeﬃcients increases exponentially in the number of parameters, making it
practically non-feasible to estimate an SVAR that makes use of all classes. This limitation
of the SVAR is also highlighted by Kwan et al. (2022). To circumvent this issue, we proceed
in an iterative way when estimating our models. Compared to the previous analysis, we now
split a certain class of aggressiveness by incorporating an additional dimension. For instance,
we divide the three types of improving limit orders (no SR, SR 3 to 9, SR10+) into three
further subclasses of order size. We repeat this for each class of aggressiveness, except for
market orders, because they already have a limited number of observations in the SR 10+
class. This yields to 75 classes when controlling for order size.7 Finally, we proceed in the
same way when using the order book imbalance dimension. While this iterative approach is
computationally expensive, it avoids the over-speciﬁcation of the SVAR, and yields reliable
and comparable estimators.

controlling for order size, 80.0% of all events (20 out of 25) exhibit a signiﬁcantly larger price

3.2. Price discovery during a strategic run
The previous analyses provide evidence that events associated with strategic run activity
by high-frequency traders have a reduced price impact and contribute less to price discovery
compared to events not part of strategic runs. This ﬁnding implies that other market participants respond diﬀerently to limit order book events of the same aggressiveness depending
on whether they are generated by these HFT strategies. Speciﬁcally, we expect that market
reactions will start to adapt when market participants are able to recognize that order book
events are generated by strategic runs. As market participants may use the same algorithm
as applied in this study to detect strategic runs, they may start to presume strategic run
activity when a cancelled order is resubmitted given the conditions discussed in Section 2.2.
To test whether market reactions change when strategic run activity becomes traceable,
we divide all events that are associated with a strategic run (SR10+) into two further subclasses. The ﬁrst class consists of the ﬁrst two events that are part of a strategic run. The
ﬁrst event can be a limit or market order submission, with the market order only partially
executed. This event is followed by a cancellation of the (remaining) quantity. A priori, both
events are not attributable to a strategic run. The second class comprises the subsequent
24

events of the run. These events involve resubmissions and cancellations in an alternating
order and may be linked to a strategic run by other market participants using the algorithm
of Hasbrouck and Saar (2013). All other events occurring in the order book are assigned to
a third class and are also categorized according to their aggressiveness. In sum, we estimate
a model comprising limit order book event dummies divided into three classes of strategic
run groups: the ﬁrst two events of a strategic run, the subsequent events of a strategic run,
and events not belonging to a run (no SR) or associated with a short run (SR 3 to 9). The
other dimension is again aggressiveness which comprises 9 classes. Consequently, the SVAR
we estimate has the same number of parameters as the model presented in Table 4 and 5.8

As the previous results indicate that the ﬁrst two events of a strategic run have a higher
signal-to-noise ratio, we further examine whether this also applies to events within the run
that alter the run’s aggressiveness. As discussed in Section 1, if the resubmitted limit order
27

does not change in terms of aggressiveness compared to the order canceled shortly before, the
resubmission is more likely a response of an involved trading algorithm or another attempt
to trigger reactions from other algorithms, rather than providing new information. However,
if the resubmitted order changes in aggressiveness, it indicates that one of the involved
algorithms has revised its beliefs about the asset’s true value (Bhattacharya and Saar, 2021).
Given that high-frequency traders can predict future midpoint moves by extracting valuable
information from the historical order ﬂow (Van Kervel and Menkveld, 2019), we hypothesize
that the direction of the change in the aggressiveness is positively correlated with the future
midpoint movement. This means that we expect the highest price impact if the aggressiveness
increases, whereas the price impact should be the lowest when the aggressiveness decreases,
since high-frequency traders expect a downwards move of the midpoint.
Therefore, we
suppose that limit orders adjusting a run’s aggressiveness convey more information than limit
orders that do not. Consequently, the former should be more relevant for price discovery.
We examine this hypothesis by dividing the order book events along three dimensions.
The ﬁrst dimension is again the aggressiveness of the event, divided into 9 classes. The
second dimension is whether the order book event is associated with a strategic run or
not. As before, the requirement here is that the strategic run must consists of 10 or more
events (SR 10+). The last dimension is the change in the aggressiveness of the run. We
diﬀerentiate between the limit order submissions that are part of a strategic run by using
dummies to indicate whether the limit order leads to a decrease, an increase, or no change
in the run’s aggressiveness.

The results suggest that resubmissions that do not alter the aggressiveness contribute
2.9% to price discovery. In contrast, submissions associated with changes in aggressiveness,
contribute 2.0% and 1.1%, respectively. In terms of frequency, resubmissions that do not
alter the aggressiveness are the most common event, representing 60.2% of all resubmissions.
Meanwhile, resubmissions that increase aggressiveness account for 18.8%, and those that
decrease aggressiveness account for 21.0%. Therefore, even though updates in aggressiveness
account for only 39.8% of the limit order submissions during a strategic run, they contribute
more to price discovery. Comparing the results on an event-by-event basis, we ﬁnd that
improving limit orders that represent a change in aggressiveness account for 1.266% of price
discovery, whereas the same event contributes 2.555% if it does not alter run’s aggressiveness.
Considering limit orders at the BBO, these events contribute 0.576% or 0.219% when they
alter run’s aggressiveness. This is a substantial diﬀerence compared to orders at the BBO
that do not alter run’s aggressiveness, which only marginally contribute to price discovery
(0.052%). Additionally, both classes of deeper orders are more relevant for price discovery
if they signal a change in run’s aggressiveness. Overall, we ﬁnd that, with the exception of
improving limit orders, events that change aggressiveness contribute more to price discovery
than no-change submissions, even though they are less frequent. In summary, these results
31

suggest that limit order submissions altering the aggressiveness of a strategic run provide
greater informational value. Therefore, the contribution to price discovery depends not only
on the submission’s inclusion in a run but also on the limit price of the preceding order.
Moreover, an increase in aggressiveness is particularly associated with a future midpoint
move in the same direction as the resubmitted limit order. These ﬁndings indicate that
high-frequency traders revise their orders in response to observed (informed) order ﬂow,
making these resubmissions more informative with respect to future midpoint movements.
4. Conclusion
The role of HFT and its impact on ﬁnancial markets is likely one of the most debated
topics among market microstructure researchers over the past decade. One reason for this is
the variety of HFT activities, each of which can have distinct impacts on market quality, necessitating a nuanced understanding of HFT’s eﬀects on ﬁnancial markets. In this study, we
contribute to this discussion by examining the permanent price impact and the corresponding contribution to price discovery of limit order book events, such as order submissions and
cancellations, associated with strategic runs.
We deﬁne strategic runs, in accordance with Hasbrouck and Saar (2013), as an outcome
of HFT strategies leading to an alternating cycle of order submissions and cancellations
within a short time interval.
Each iteration of submission and cancellation consistently
targets the same side of the limit order book, uses the same order size, and can be repeated
more than 100 times. Although the underlying strategies of the involved trading algorithms
are not observable, market data patterns suggest that they result from multiple algorithms
attempting to undercut or trigger other algorithms (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013). Since these
32

limit order book events frequently repeat the same signal and search for information rather
than providing new information, we question the informational value of this order ﬂow.
Using granular data that captures each incremental message sent to the limit order book,
we estimate SVAR models to evaluate the permanent price impact and the contribution to
price discovery of speciﬁc limit order book events, distinguishing between those associated
with strategic runs and those that are not.
We ﬁnd that order submissions and cancellations submitted to the limit order book as
part of a strategic run have less price impact and contribute less to price discovery than
submissions and cancellations of the same aggressiveness that are not associated with a
strategic run.
This signiﬁcant diﬀerence between non-strategic and strategic run events
persists when controlling for order size and imbalance in the limit order book. Furthermore,
we observe that the contribution to price discovery is signiﬁcantly reduced once other market
participants can recognize the strategic run.
Based on these ﬁndings, we conclude that
limit order book events resulting from high-frequency traders’ strategic runs provide limited
informational value.
Additionally, we show that the price impact of a strategic run event depends on whether
the event leads to a change in the aggressiveness of the run. This suggests that other market
participants observe high-frequency traders’ runs and interpret changes in run’s aggressiveness as new information. Moreover, this ﬁnding aligns with the theory of Bhattacharya and
Saar (2021), which posits that the frequent cancellations and resubmissions in today’s limit
order book markets result from high-frequency traders constantly revising their orders as
they update their beliefs about the asset value based on the observed (informed) order ﬂow.
33

We view our contribution as two-fold. First, we contribute to the literature on HFT’s
impact on market quality by demonstrating the reduced signal-to-noise ratio of strategic run
events, which account for a substantial fraction of messages in limit order book markets.
Our study provides further evidence that a nuanced perspective is required when assessing
the impact of HFT on market quality. Second, we extend the growing literature on limit
order price discovery. Speciﬁcally, we show that the contribution of a limit order book event
to price discovery is determined not only by the characteristics of the event (Brogaard et al.,
2019; Brugler and Hendershott, 2023) and the state of the limit order book (Kwan et al.,
2022), but also by its relationship to other events and the attributes of these interconnected
events. In particular, we demonstrate that the price impact of an individual limit order book
event depends on whether the event is triggered by a strategic run and on whether it alters
the run’s aggressiveness.
While our study further disentangles the activities of HFT and their implications for
market quality, more research examining speciﬁc HFT activities is needed to gain a comprehensive understanding and to account for the rapidly changing market environment. This can
help regulators to introduce more nuanced regulations with respect to HFT. Furthermore,
since large fractions of limit order book activity are interconnected, not only via strategic
runs, future research should aim to understand more deeply how order ﬂow is interconnected
and how this relates to price discovery and price eﬃciency.
