Abstract
This study introduces an innovative AI-powered methodology for identifying external factors that drive informational inefficiencies across in a given
dataset. Our secondary objective is to shed light on the widely observed pairs
trading phenomenon, commonly referred to as statistical arbitrage. By zooming into the factors that drive pairs trading strategies, we aim to contribute to
the creation of a more stable market environment and foster broader benefits.
Keywords:
Factors, Information Asymmetry, Statistical Arbitrage, AI, Market
Efficiency, Pairs Trading Strategies, Risk Management, Regulatory
Frameworks

1. Introduction
The study of pairs trading originated with Gatev et al. (2006), who aimed
to evaluate a popular Wall Street strategy.
Their findings confirmed the
effectiveness of the strategy while also questioning the prevailing explanation
for its success: the notion that traders were being compensated for bringing
market prices into equilibrium, thus enforcing the Law of One Price. The
Law of One Price, as established by Deardorff (1979), suggests that no onesided arbitrage is possible. However, Gatev et al. (2006) proposed that the
observed profitability of pairs trading may be attributed to an undisclosed
factor, which is the focus of our research.
The investigation into the influence of asymmetric information on market
dynamics was initiated by Fama (1970), with subsequent contributions from
Grossman (1976), Grossman and Stiglitz (1976 and 1980), and Friedman et

al. (1983). These scholars posited that an informationally-efficient market is
characterized by prices that accurately reflect all available information. As
per this theory, if our markets were truly informationally-efficient at present,
it would be unattainable to generate profits through any trading or investment strategies.
However, contemporary studies indicate that inefficiencies continue to exist and can emerge due to various factors. Notable recent examples include
the work of DeMiguel et al. (2023), who employ machine learning techniques
to identify mutual funds exhibiting a positive alpha, indicating their potential
for outperforming the market. Additionally, Ying (2024) observes that stocks
with concentrated institutional ownership incorporate information into the
markets at a slower pace compared to their counterparts, presenting opportunities for arbitrage.
These information inefficiencies can be categorized and quantified as factors, which are time-series data that convey specific information to the market. If the returns on a particular financial instrument can be accurately
predicted in advance by any factor, it indicates that the current returns fail
to incorporate all the available information in a timely manner. As a result,
this creates opportunities for arbitrage. Consequently, numerous researchers
have endeavored to develop factors that can better explain the unexplained
aspects of market information. Prominent examples include the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) proposed by Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965),
Arbitrage Pricing Theory by Ross (1976), as well as the three-factor model introduced by Fama and French (1993), among others. Harvey and Liu (2018)
document the recent proliferation of factors in the literature on return factorization, many of which are highly correlated. Our paper also adds to this
growing body of research in identifying and systematizing such factors.
In line with previous studies conducted by DeMiguel et al. (2017), Freyberger et al. (2017), Feng et al. (2017), and Kozak, Nagel, and Santosh
(2020), our research utilizes a supervised machine learning approach to identify the number of factors at play. DeMiguel et al. (2017), Freyberger et
al. (2017), and Feng et al. (2017) employ the Least Absolute Shrinkage and
Selection Operator (LASSO) technique to ascertain the necessary predictors
for adequately assessing the cross-sectional variation of different financial instruments. Our methodology closely aligns with that of Kozak, Nagel, and
Santosh (2020), who construct a Stochastic Discount Factor using Principal
Components.
In our study, we adopt a popular strategy for capitalizing on these in2

efficiencies, commonly referred to as a long-short strategy or pairs trading.
This approach, as outlined by Vidyamurthy (2004) and Gatev et al. (2006),
involves identifying a pair of assets that exhibit consistent and statistically
significant relative value disparities.
The selection of an appropriate pair is typically conducted using cointegration techniques such as Engle and Granger (1987) or Johansen (1991).
In a similar vein, Elliott et al. (2005) introduce the stochastic spread model,
which involves modeling the price differential between the two instruments
as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and subsequently filtering the outcome.
Building on this approach, Do et al.
(2006) extend the methodology by
substituting prices with asset returns in Elliott et al. (2006)’s framework.
In addition to traditional approaches, more advanced techniques incorporating machine learning have emerged. For instance, Dunis et al. (2006,
2015) utilize neural networks to forecast spreads, demonstrating the potential of this methodology. Furthermore, Ko et al. (2023) compare traditional
statistical methods with genetic algorithms (GA) and non-dominated sorting
genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) specifically in the context of cryptocurrency
markets. Their study reveals that NSGA-II outperforms other methods, underscoring the advantages of employing sophisticated algorithms to optimize
pairs trading strategies.
Until now, most pairs trading strategies did not incorporate exogenous
factors. Recent methodologies, similar to our own approach yet rooted in
pure statistical arbitrage without exogenous factors, have demonstrated sustained positive returns. Notable examples include the work of Avellaneda
and Lee (2010) and Focardi, Fabozzi, and Mitov (2016). However, failure to
explain the profitability phenomenon may result in models that are potentially flawed and susceptible to instability. This oversight can even contribute
to market-wide contagion, as highlighted by Gao and Ladley (2022).
According to the Financial Economics literature, the observable differences between two assets are likely to originate from informational inefficiencies. For instance, Lee et al. (2024) highlight the presence of an informational
lead-lag effect among stocks within the same industries, indicating profitable
opportunities arising from information asymmetries. Bartram and Grinblatt
(2021) demonstrate that informational inefficiencies directly drive returns in
the Asia-Pacific markets. Empirical studies consistently support the information asymmetry hypothesis as the primary driver of pairs trading models,
as evidenced by Engleberg et al. (2009), Chen et al., and other relevant
research.
3

The groundbreaking works of Glosten and Milgrom (1985) and Kyle
(1985) established the link between informational asymmetries and asset
returns, a relationship that remains prevalent in the current financial landscape. Ongoing research continues to shed light on these asymmetries. For
instance, Ranaldo and Somogyi (2021) investigate market flows and reveal
the presence of informational asymmetries in foreign exchange markets. Similarly, Guo and Mota (2021) demonstrate the value of equity analysis for
predicting future stock returns. These studies collectively emphasize the enduring significance of informational asymmetries in driving market dynamics.
Informational asymmetries can be attributed to a range of factors, as evidenced by research in the field. For instance, Krishnaswami and Subramaniam (1999) demonstrate that more complex firms tend to have higher levels
of information asymmetry compared to streamlined firms that undergo spinoffs. Baik et al. (2010) discover that the geographic location of investors is
correlated with their knowledge, with local investors exhibiting greater familiarity with local companies compared to foreign investors. Moreover, Huang
et al. (2022) identify differences in investor attention to specific patterns
of information arrival as a contributing factor to the emergence of informational inefficiencies. Collectively, these studies provide insights into the
diverse sources of informational asymmetries.
Our research presents an innovative approach that bridges the information
asymmetry literature with the realm of statistical arbitrage. Specifically, we
enhance the classic co-integration model developed by Vidyamurthy (2004)
by introducing exogenous variables. To identify these factors, we propose a
novel AI methodology, which helps determine the potential drivers of informational asymmetries in statistical arbitrage, pairs trading strategies, and
other lead-lag relationships among asset prices.
To evaluate the model’s effectiveness, we conduct simulations to assess
its performance and characteristics. Furthermore, we provide an empirical
study focused on futures, where we include variables such as macroeconomic
indicators, bond yields, and market indices as factors. Through the implementation of real-world data, we showcase the practical application of our
model.
By leveraging AI in our framework, we contribute to the ongoing discussion on reducing the universe of factors. Moreover, our approach enhances
the robustness and performance of pairs trading strategies, ultimately leading
to improved market stability overall.

2. Methodology
2.1. Pairs Identification
To identify a promising pair of securities, we follow Gatev et al. (2006)
and Vidyamurthy (2004) in defining the residual returns, also known as
spread, as the simple difference between two returns.
Using a simple return formulation, Given two assets, i and j with prices pi,t and pj,t, and their
log returns ri,t = log(pi,t) −log(pi,t−1), the residual can be represented as:
ϵij = log(pi,t) −log(pi,t−1) −log(pj,t) + log(pj,t−1)
(1)
If we pick two assets with identical prices at time t-1, Pi,t−1 = pj,t−1 or simply
invest identical dollar amount into i and j at time t, then we can represent
the residual formula as:
ϵij,t = log(pi,t) −log(pj,t)
(2)
Further assuming that the residuals are random with mean 0, we can estimate
the above equation as:
log(pi,t) = α + βlog(pj,t) + ϵij,t
(3)
This specification was provided by Vidyamurthy (2004). He proposes a
pairs trading strategy where we buy $1 of asset i and short-sell β dollars of
asset j whenever α is small. When α is large, we short-sell $1 of asset i and
buy β units of asset j whenever. An example of “small” and “large” α can
be this simple rule:
• If α > 0, buy $1 of asset i, short-sell β units of asset j
• If α < 0, short-sell $1 of asset i, buy β units of asset j
A more precise formulation that would allow divergent securities prices
at time t-1 can be found similar to Rad et al. (2016) as:
log(pi,t) = α + βlog(pj,t) + γlog( pi,t−1
pj,t−1) + ϵij,t
(4)
Both Vidyamurthy (2004) and Rad et al. (2016) assume persistence of
relationships between the prices of the two securities. This persistence can
be measured as the statistical significance of the coefficient β in equation
5

(3). The statistical significance of β can be measured by its accompanying
t-ratio, where the t-ratio measures the precision of the β, and is computed
as the estimate divided by the standard deviation of the coefficient, as estimated by the regression. Whenever the t-ratio corresponds to the Z-score
probability of 90% (99%) or greater, the coefficient is assumed to be (highly)
statistically significant and persistent. As a result of the strategy assuming
a given expected relationship between two securities and essentially trading
away the deviations from that relationship, the strategy is often referred to
as mean-reverting.

2.3. Why OLS Fails
It is easy to see why OLS fails in the presence of collinearity.
Consider two correlated time series: x and z, where z is an affine transformation of x,
z = αz + βzx + ϵz
(9)
Depending on the relative magnitude of the βz = coefficient, the following
OLS regression may fail right away:
y = α + βxx + βzz + εy
(10)
If the right-hand-side variables are represented as X = [x, z] and the lefthand-side variable is Y = [y], then β = (XTY )/(XTX)−1. If {x} and {z}
variables are highly correlated, however, there is not enough variability in
the X and the necessary inverse of XTX may not exist, and β cannot be
estimated.

2.5. Identifying Dominant Factors with Eigenregressions
The idea of using eigenfactors for identifying relevant drivers has been
explored in the financial literature. However, the adoption of the technique
has been slow mostly due to the lack of interpretability of the factors. As
[30] show, however, the factor composition is readily reflected in its respective
eigenvector, making the explanatory process fully transparent.
Thus, the OLS regression y = α + βxx + βzz + εy with correlated x
and z from equation (10) can be transformed into a new regression with
independent variables (equation (20) that are orthogonal by construction
and can, therefore, be estimated using OLS;
y = α + βF + ϵ
(21)
where F is a matrix with each column i representing factor Fi from equation
(20).
The corresponding regression coefficients βi will help us identify the factors Fi that are most relevant to explaining the given variable y. Once we
identify statistically-significant factors, we can peer inside the factor construction to examine the relative weights of the original variables in each
significant factor, following [30].
Thus, to estimate the optimal drivers of a time series from a set of highly
collinear candidates, we propose to:
1. Perform an eigenvalue decomposition of the candidate variable set
2. Construct eigenfactors
3. Regress the time series of interest on the resulting eigenfactors
4. Explain the eigenfactors by the dominant coefficients in their respective
underlying eigenvectors.

5. Conclusion
Our research presents a novel AI-based methodology that effectively identifies informational inefficiencies between financial instruments utilizing exogenous factors. By leveraging Unsupervised AI techniques, we are able to
uncover previously hidden relationships and better comprehend the factors
influencing pairs trading outcomes.
Through comprehensive analysis, we
provide empirical evidence of the significance of these exogenous factors in
driving the observed statistical arbitrage.
Understanding the mechanics and determinants of pairs trading opens up
avenues for a range of potential benefits. By gaining insights into the reasons
behind successful statistical arbitrage, market participants and policymakers
can work towards creating more stable market conditions. Additionally, the
knowledge gained from this study can inform the development of new trading
strategies, risk management approaches, and regulatory frameworks.
Overall, our research adds to the existing literature by introducing a
novel approach to analyzing pairs trading, facilitating a deeper understanding of market dynamics. We believe that this investigation contributes to
the broader goal of enhancing market efficiency and fostering a more robust
financial ecosystem.
