 
 
The Impact of HFT on the Speed of Adjustment to New 
Information: Evidence from Interest Rate Derivatives 
 
Alex Frino*, Michael Garcia, Zeyang Zhou 
University of Wollongong, Wollongong, NSW, Australia 
 
 
 
 
 
Abstract 
This study examines the impact of algorithmic trading (AT) on the speed of adjustment and price 
discovery during scheduled macroeconomic announcements for interest rate derivatives. In 
February 2012, the Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) introduced co-location services for 
futures traders. This change in market structure increases AT and, in this study, we examine price 
efficiency for both exchange-traded futures and over-the-counter (OTC)-traded swaps in the pre- 
and post-AT periods. Our results demonstrate that, in the presence of AT, the speed of adjustment 
to new information has improved for both futures and swaps. In addition, we find that the price 
discovery contribution of the futures market improves in the post-AT period, with this 
improvement significant for announcement days. We conclude that AT stimulates market 
adjustment to new information and enhances the price discovery of futures on days with scheduled 
macroeconomic releases.

1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Improvements in technology have revolutionised the way in which investors can trade in financial 
markets. Algorithmic trading (AT), defined as “the use of computer algorithms to automatically 
make certain trading decisions, submit orders, and manage those orders after submissions”1, is an 
example of these technological changes. The effect of AT on market quality is important and has 
attracted interest from researchers, practitioners and policy makers. This paper examines the effect 
of AT on two dimensions of market quality, namely, liquidity and price discovery, through which 
information is incorporated into prices.  
Algorithmic trading (AT) is thought to be responsible for more than half the trading volume in the 
United States (US).2 Consequently, many exchanges now offer technological upgrades to reduce 
latency and attract more order flow. One argument put forward by the empirical literature is that 
the reduction in latency enables algorithmic traders to more rapidly adjust their prices when new 
information arrives and therefore improves price efficiency (Chaboud, Chiquoine, Hjalmarsson, 
& Vega, 2014; Scholtus, van Dijk, & Frijns 2014; Chordia, Green, & Kottimukkalur, 2018; 
Brogaard, Hendershott, & Riordan, 2014; Frino, Mollica, Monaco, & Palumbo, 2017a). Another 
argument proposed by the theoretical literature is that the latency reduction improves liquidity 
through one of two channels, either adverse selection or inventory management. The adverse 
selection channel states that fast market makers are able to avoid being adversely selected and can 
thus maintain their liquidity supply (Hoffmann, 2014). The inventory management channel, as 
addressed by Aït-Sahalia and Saglam (2013), shows that the speed advantage of fast market makers 
causes a reduction in their cost of holding inventory; therefore, they can afford to increase their 
liquidity supply. In summary, the reduction in latency may impose a positive effect on price 
discovery and market liquidity. 
Much of the literature has looked at the impact of AT on liquidity, by identifying technological 
upgrades that alter the amount of AT activity and examining the consequences on market liquidity. 
The seminal work of Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld (2011) employs the start of auto-quote 
usage on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) as an exogenous instrument and presents a causal 
link between increases in AT and improvements in liquidity. Subsequent studies employ upgrades 
in trading facilities for smaller markets and draw similar conclusions (Riordan & Storkenmaier,

Our paper is distinguished from previous studies in the following ways. Firstly, we extend the 
work of Frino et al. (2014) to study how the introduction of co-location affects market liquidity 
during macroeconomic releases. In February 2012, the Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) 
allowed futures traders to co-locate their servers at the exchange’s data centre, thus providing a 
natural experiment to test the impact of AT on liquidity. Frino et al. (2014) use this event to isolate 
the effect of latency on overall market liquidity, finding liquidity improvements following the 
technological upgrade. We extend their work by focusing on the time intervals in which liquidity 
is most desired by the market, in other words, around information releases. 
Secondly, previous research provides evidence on how AT affects liquidity and primarily tests 
whether impact occurs following co-location. Very little evidence exists on a critical aspect of 
market quality, that is, the speed of adjustment to new information. In this paper, we focus on the 
speed of adjustment which measures the resiliency of liquidity in a market, rather than simply 
measuring liquidity. 
Thirdly, we extend previous research by identifying a futures market in which co-location was 
introduced, as well as a related derivatives market where co-location was not introduced. Our study 
then examines whether spillover effects occur between the two markets, whereas most previous 
research only looks at what happens in a market in which co-location was introduced. We find that 
the speed of adjustment to new information has improved for both futures and swap markets 
following co-location. We conjecture that the reduced latency in futures has led to an improvement 
in price efficiency for over-the-counter (OTC)-traded swaps through cross-market arbitrage. In 
addition, we provide evidence on the impact of AT on the lead–lag relationship between the two 
derivatives markets. Specifically, we show the price discovery contribution of the futures market 
improves in the post-AT period, with this improvement significant for announcement days. 
Fourthly, studies that explore the role of AT in forming prices generally focus on the response of 
equities to firm-specific information releases (Frino et al, 2017a; Frino, Prodromou, Wang, 
Westerholm, & Zheng, 2017b). We extend the previous literature by examining the response of 
interest rate derivatives to macroeconomic information releases. Investors with better market-wide 
information prefer to trade in futures, while investors with stock-specific information prefer to 
trade in stocks (Frino, Walter, & West, 2000). Fleming and Remolona (1999) conjecture that

macroeconomic announcements do not normally confront market makers with the risk of trading 
with more informed investors. Consequently, the liquidity provision around these announcements, 
originating from market makers, is most likely to be driven by inventory control, rather than 
information asymmetry. For this reason, we select days on which macroeconomic announcements 
are made as our sample days and study the role of AT in price formation following these news 
releases.  
The outline of this paper is as follows. Section 2 provides institutional details, with an overview 
of the study’s data and methodology then presented in Section 3. Section 4 presents descriptive 
statistics on swaps and 90-day bank-accepted bills (90-day BABs) futures and reports the

2. 
INSTITUTIONAL DETAILS 
The Australian 90-day BABs futures contract, introduced in 1979, has become one of the most 
liquid contracts in the world, trading a daily average of 114,805 contracts in 2017, six times higher 
than the daily average turnover of the SPI Futures contract (Australian Financial Markets 
Association [AFMA], 2017). The 90-day futures contract trades at the ASX Trade24 on a quarterly 
expiration cycle (March, June, September and December) from 8:28 a.m. Australian Eastern 
Standard Time (AEST) to 4:30 p.m. AEST. During the winter period, from the second Sunday in 
March until the first Sunday in November, it trades from 5:08 p.m. AEST to 7:00 a.m. AEST. It 
also trades from 8:28 a.m. AEST to 4:30 p.m. AEST, and from 5:08 p.m. AEST to 7:30 a.m. AEST 
during the summer period, from the first Sunday in November until the second Sunday in March. 
Australian interest rate swaps are mostly traded in the OTC market. Swap trading comprises 
regular voice dealer trading and exchange-like trading platforms with central limit order books. In 
Australia, the exchange-like trading platforms for swaps include the Australian Market Licence 
(AML) regime, swap execution facilities (SEF) and multilateral trading facilities (MTF).

DATA AND METHOD 
The data for this study are sourced from the Thomson Reuters Tick History (TRTH) database 
maintained by the Securities Industries Research Centre of Asia-Pacific (SIRCA). From this 
database, we collect intraday bid and ask prices, bid and ask sizes, and traded price and volume 
for the Australian 90-day BABs futures contracts traded on the ASX from 2 March 2010 to

19 February 2014.3 Over-the-counter (OTC) quote data for the Australian 1-year interest rate swap 
contract are also collected from the TRTH database on an intraday basis for the period from 2 
March 2010 to 19 February 2014. This database includes indicative quotes provided by approved 
dealers and contributors.4 For both swaps and futures contracts, we calculate the mid-quote as the 
average of the best bid and ask quotes which reduces the effect of bid–ask bounce (Hauptfleisch, 
Putniņš, & Lucey, 2016). Data for interest rate swap and futures contracts are collected for the 
daytime trading session from 8:28 a.m. AEST to 4:30 p.m. AEST.  
This study investigates the impact of algorithmic trading (AT) on price volatility, market liquidity 
and price discovery in Australian futures and swaps markets. Using the introduction of co-location 
that occurred on 20 February 2012, we divide our sample period into two sub-periods. The period 
from 2 March 2010 to 19 February 2012 is classified as the “pre-AT” trading period, and the period 
from 21 February 2012 to 19 February 2014 is classified as the “post-AT” trading period.  
Macroeconomic announcements are collected from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), with 
the announcement data including the date, type and release time for these major announcements 
in Australia. Following Frino and Hill (2001), we select the types of announcements that have a 
significant impact on market volatility, with the six selected types being: Consumer Price Index 
(CPI), gross domestic product (GDP), Producer Price Index, retail sales, building approvals, 
Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) interest rate and unemployment rate. In total, 176 
macroeconomic information releases occurred between 2 March 2010 and 19 February 2014, with 
88 occurring in the pre co-location period and 88 in the post co-location period.5

3.6. Price-Matched Samples 
It may be possible that the level of unexpected information or the value of information on 
announcement days during the pre-AT period is systematically different to that on announcement 
days in the post-AT period. To ensure that the size of information is similar across the two subperiods, we match each announcement day in the pre-AT period to an announcement day in the 
post-AT period with the closest return. To ensure a close match, any matched returns that differ 
by more than four basis points (bps) are eliminated from the final sample. The purpose of this 
procedure is to control for the magnitude of price movements associated with macroeconomic 
announcement releases. We calculate returns using the last traded price 30 minutes prior to the 
announcement and the last traded price 30 minutes after the announcement is released.  
We also control for the size of information released across the samples of experimental 
(announcement) days and control (non-announcement) days by matching each announcement day 
to a non-announcement day with the closest return. Returns on non-announcement days are 
calculated using the last traded price 30 minutes prior and post to the time when announcements 
are usually released on announcement days. The final sample for the futures market consists of 63 
announcements and matched control days for the pre-AT period, and 57 announcements and 
matched control days for the post-AT period. Similarly, the final sample for the swap market

3.7. Information Share (IS) and Information Leadership Share (ILS) 
Currently, a popular common factor model, Hasbrouck’s (1995) information share (IS), is widely 
employed to study price discovery between different asset classes. Hasbrouck (1995) defines price 
discovery as the variance of innovations to the common factor, with information share (IS) 
measuring each market’s relative contribution to this variance. In this paper, our primary focus is 
to study which market, either futures or swaps, is the first to impound new information about the 
‘true’ underlying asset value, as well as studying how each market’s contribution to price discovery 
changes following the introduction of co-location in one of the markets. Hasbrouck’s (1995) 
information share (IS) is a good measure to identify the leader in impounding new information; 
however, it is also influenced by the relative amount of noise in the price channels. As the levels 
of noise in futures and swap markets differ significantly due to differences in market structures 
and trading participants, we also employ an additional price discovery measure, namely, 
information leadership share (Putniņš, 2013), to isolate the relative speed at which information is 
impounded by a price series from its relative level of noise.

4.2. Magnitude of Information Content across Sub-Periods  
It might be possible that the value of information released in macroeconomic announcements is 
systematically different before and after co-location which could bias the results. To test for this, 
we compare the absolute mean returns for each sub-period around the time that macroeconomic 
information is released. Specifically, we calculate the return using the mid-quoted price 30 minutes 
before the macroeconomic announcement and 30 minutes after the announcement is released.9 In 
Table II, we compare the magnitude of price changes on announcement days (experimental 
sample) and non-announcement days (control sample) for interest rate futures (Panel A) and swaps

between the absolute average returns across the two periods, t-statistics testing indicates that the 
mean differences of returns are not statistically significant for both swaps and futures. This implies 
that the magnitude of the macroeconomic information releases across the two sub-periods cannot

5. 
CONCLUSION 
The introduction of co-location services for futures traders in Australia creates a natural 
experiment in which it is possible to test the impact of algorithmic trading (AT) on the speed of 
adjustment to new information for the futures market, where co-location facilities are 
implemented, and for a related swap market, where co-location is not introduced. We find that, 
following the introduction of co-location, there is an improvement in the speed at which volatility, 
bid–ask spread and market depth return to equilibrium for both interest rate futures and swaps. In 
addition, we examine price discovery by comparing information shares (ISs) of the swap and 
futures markets during information releases between pre- and post-colocation periods. We find 
that AT improves the price discovery contribution of the futures market, with this improvement 
significant for announcement days.
