Non-Parametric Prediction of the Mid-Price
Dynamics in a Limit Order Book
Deepan Palguna and Ilya Pollak
Abstract
Many securities markets are organized as double auctions where each incoming limit order—i.e., an
order to buy or sell at a speciﬁc price—is stored in a data structure called the limit order book. A trade
happens whenever a market order arrives—i.e., an order to buy or sell at the best currently available price.
This order ﬂow is visible to every market participant in real time. We propose a novel non-parametric
approach to short-term forecasting of the mid-price change in a limit order book (i.e., of the change in
the average of the best offer and the best bid prices). We construct a state vector characterizing the order
book at each time instant and compute a feature vector for each value of the state vector. The features
get updated during the course of a trading day, as new order ﬂow information arrives. We cluster similar
states together so that each cluster has enough observations for reliable feature estimation. The feature
vector of a cluster is the average of the feature vectors of its constituent states. Our prediction at every
time instant during the trading day is based on the feature vector of the cluster observed at that time
instant. The distinction of our approach from the previous ones is that it does not impose a restrictive
parametric model. Implicit assumptions of our method are very mild. Initial experiments with real order
book data from NYSE suggest that our algorithms show promise. We illustrate their usage in a practical
application of executing a large trade.

Accurately forecasting the dynamics of the order book is crucial in a variety of application scenarios,

(1) A broker charged with executing a trade. The broker’s ability to forecast the behavior of the order
book would help him optimize the trade execution schedule—i.e., decide how to partition the total
number of units to be traded into small amounts, how to time each small order, and how aggressively

(2) An asset manager contemplating a portfolio rebalancing. A predictor of the order book dynamics
would help forecast the cost incurred through the price impact of the trades required to perform
the rebalancing [4], [5], [6].
(3) An exchange or a regulator charged with maintaining orderly markets. Order book forecasting tools
would help exchanges and regulators quickly detect the possibility of abnormal events (such as the
Flash Crash of May 6, 2010 [7], [8]) and take timely measures to prevent such events or to dampen
their effects.
The importance of order book forecasting has motivated academic research in modeling the order book
dynamics, developing the prediction algorithms, and empirically studying the behavior of order books [9].
In the problem of optimal trade execution, order book dynamics is a critical element for studying the
price impact of order sizes. Much previous work on optimal execution relies on macro-level modeling
of the price impact of a trade [10], [11], [12]. The execution algorithms proposed in this literature do
not model the state or the dynamics of the order book, and hence produce trading decisions that do not
take into account the order book information.
A number of more recent papers on optimal execution methods do take the order book information
into account. In [1], [2], [3], it is assumed that the order book has an underlying simple shape to which
it returns in the absence of large orders, a property called resilience. While the assumption of order book
resilience has some grounding in empirical observations [13], [14], the assumption of a single steady-state
shape is not supported by empirical studies and appears to be too strong and too simplistic to enable

work.
In the present paper, we propose a novel non-parametric approach to short-term forecasting of the
mid-price change (i.e., the change in the average of the best offer and the best bid prices). We introduce
a state vector describing the state of the order book at each time, similar to the state used in [16]. The
state vector can assume a ﬁnite number of discrete values. We construct a distance metric to characterize
the similarity of any two states, and develop a clustering algorithm to group similar states into clusters.
The purpose of clustering is to reduce the number of parameters that our prediction algorithm will need
to estimate from the data.
We compute two features for each state cluster, as well as a feature that does not depend on the
state. The features get updated during the course of a trading day, as new order ﬂow information arrives.
We propose two predictors based on the feature values. The ﬁrst algorithm predicts whether or not the
mid-price will change a ﬁxed amount of time in the future. The second algorithm predicts whether the
mid-price change will be positive or negative.
In order to evaluate our predictors, we incorporate them into an order execution strategy. Through
simulations on a set of liquid US stocks from NYSE’s OpenBook dataset, we show that our predictors
result in a statistically signiﬁcant average gain around three basis points.
The distinction of our approach from the previous ones is that it does not impose a restrictive parametric
model. Implicit assumptions of our method are very mild. The ﬁrst assumption is that the order ﬂow
is not temporally independent, and this assumption is supported by our experiments on real data. The
second assumption is that the order ﬂow is temporally Markov, with a small state dimension. While this
assumption is unrealistic, our experiments show that it leads to promising empirical results. The third
assumption is that mid-price changes are approximately stationary and ergodic over short time intervals.

II. TERMINOLOGY
A limit order is an order to buy or sell a desired number of units of an asset at a desired price. The
order book at any time t is the collection of all outstanding limit orders at all prices at that time. The
highest price among all the buy limit orders at time t is called the best bid price at time t. The lowest
price among all the sell limit orders at time t is called the best ask price or best offer price at time t.
The difference between the best ask and the best bid prices is called the bid-ask spread. The average of
the best ask and the best bid is called the mid-price. We denote this quantity V (t).
A market order is an order to buy or sell a desired number of units of an asset at the best currently
available price. When a buy market order comes in, it gets executed against the current limit orders at
the best ask price. If there are not enough units offered by the sellers at the best ask price to ﬁll the
entire market order, the remainder of the market order gets executed at the next best price, etc, until the
entire market order is executed. In the same way, incoming sell market orders get executed against the
bid side of the order book.
We assume that only three kinds of events can change the conﬁguration of an order book: limit orders,
limit order cancellations, and market orders. Figs. 1 and 2 illustrate the order book and its changes in
response to incoming orders.

B. The need for reducing the number of states
Our plan for mid-price prediction at time t is to use recently observed order book data to empirically
estimate certain conditional expectations given the state vector S(t), and then to use these estimates for
nonparametric prediction. In order for the empirical estimates of conditional expectations to be reliable,
there would need to be many occurrences of the state S(t) in recent order book data. On the other hand,
note that bid-ask spreads can range from one cent for highly liquid stocks to many dollars for illiquid
ones. The number of shares at the best bid and best ask can be in the tens of thousands. Therefore, the
number of possible values for the state vector S(t) as deﬁned in the previous subsection, is on the order
of 106. During a typical day’s trading for a particular stock, most of these states will either not occur at
all or occur very infrequently. This would make it meaningless to condition on such states.
To circumvent this problem, we group states into clusters, and condition our estimates on clusters
instead of individual states. The main purpose of clustering is to insure that each cluster has many states
that occurred in recently observed order book data. We deﬁne a similarity criterion for states and present
our clustering algorithm in the next two subsections.

A cluster is a set of states. We use Ωi to denote the ith cluster. The center yi of the ith cluster is

At the initialization step of the algorithm that we call PERFORMCLUSTERING, we assign each state
to a distinct cluster so that each cluster is a singleton set. Hence, at this step, the center of every cluster
is the single state itself and there are as many clusters as there are states. We then select the cluster
with the lowest probability and merge it with the cluster closest to it. The center of this merged cluster
is calculated as the centroid of its elements. The merge process is repeated until each cluster has a
probability larger than a threshold Pmin which is an input parameter. Fig. 3 uses pseudocode to describe

G. Prediction of the mid-price change
We propose two hypothesis testing algorithms: one for predicting whether the mid-price will change
a ﬁxed amount of time into the future, and the second one to predict whether the mid-price change is
positive or negative.
At time t, we cluster the state vectors that occurred during the interval [0, t). We compute the state
S(t), identify the cluster Ωto which S(t) belongs, and estimate the three features of Eqs. (1,2,3) over the
interval [0, t], as described above. Our ﬁrst algorithm solves a binary hypothesis testing problem where

IV. ORDER EXECUTION STRATEGY
To evaluate our predictors, we incorporate them into an order execution strategy for buying M shares
of a stock over a given time interval (Niδ, Nfδ], where M, Ni, and Nf are given positive integers with
Nf > Ni. The objective of such a strategy is to obtain a low average price per share. In other words,
strategy A is better than strategy B if A can be demonstrated to achieve a smaller average purchase price
per share than B.
Note that sending the entire purchase order at once is likely to adversely affect the price. Therefore,
it is typically better to partition the order into smaller orders and send them gradually during the alloted
time period. Many strategies for doing this have been proposed that do not attempt to use the prediction
of price changes [21], [20]. If one has the ability to predict price changes, then it is natural to expect
that any such execution strategy can be improved by incorporating the predictions into it. If, on the other
hand, our predictors do not work (i.e., if their output is independent of the price moves that actually
occur), then they would not enable improved execution.
In order to demonstrate that our predictors do work, we show in Section V that they produce a
statistically signiﬁcant improvement of a simple execution strategy. In the present section, we describe
this strategy and our modiﬁcation.
Our benchmark is a uniform execution strategy2 which uses market orders of size M/(Nf −Ni) at
times t = (Ni + 1)δ, (Ni + 2)δ, . . . , (Nf)δ.

A. Order execution experiments
One important issue in simulating execution strategies with order book data is that it is not possible
to replicate permanent market impact with historical data. This is because future data points, which are
made up of public orders, cannot respond to simulated orders. In our experiments with market orders, we
execute a market order at time t as if it will get executed when the order book is in the historical state
at time t. But we do not modify the order book since there is no way of simulating permanent market
impact. We now explain the simulation procedure and how the simulation parameters are chosen.
The stock universe consists of 100 liquid3 stocks as measured over the period June 1 to June 29, 2010.
We perform simulations by collecting features during June 30, 2010 starting at 9:30am and execute M
shares between 3 pm and 4 pm. In a real application scenario, a client would send an order to a broker
and specify M. Therefore, the broker who runs the execution algorithm would not have any control over
M. It is widely understood that if M is a signiﬁcant fraction of the market volume, then executing such a
large order might have an adverse impact on the execution price. Therefore, clients are typically reluctant
to request values of M that correspond to large fractions of the market volume. We assume here that M
is 1% of the average hourly volume, which corresponds to a realistic trade execution scenario.
We ﬁx δ = 1 second, π = 1, and conduct experiments with threshold values θ = 0, 0.1, and 0.5, and
with minimum cluster probability values Pmin = 0 and 0.0001.

VI. CONCLUSIONS
We have presented an algorithm for predicting mid-price changes in limit order books using features
that are estimated for different order book states. In order to reliably estimate the features that our
algorithm requires, we have also introduced an algorithm to cluster similar states. Incorporating these
two algorithms into an execution strategy that uses market orders alone shows statistically signiﬁcant
performance improvements over a benchmark that does not take the predictions into account. The average
improvement in performance is economically signiﬁcant in the context of institutional investors.
