Abstract 
The historical evolution of quantitative finance is conventionally narrated as a story of technological 
and computational progress—from factor models to machine learning, from stochastic calculus to 
reinforcement learning. This paper advances a structural interpretation: the field’s development has 
been driven by recurrent failures of representational and decision closure as markets have become 
more adaptive, state-dependent, path-sensitive, and endogenously unstable. 
Successive paradigms have been organized around dominant mathematical objects—conditional 
expectations, stochastic dynamics, regime states, and geometric path structures. While each expansion 
enhanced representational power, none fully resolved a deeper limitation: accurate forecasts do not 
guarantee participation continuity under dynamic continuation conditions. Equivalent forecasts can 
imply materially different admissible actions once state persistence, transition asymmetry, and path 
dependence are accounted for. 
Central to the argument is Theorem 3 (Dynamic Participation Optimality), which demonstrates that 
forecast-only policies are strictly dominated by state- and path-dependent policies in Bellmanconsistent environments. The structured decomposition 𝑎𝑡= 𝑔(𝑠𝑡, ℎ𝑡) ⋅𝑏(𝑟̂𝑡)  is non-commutative in 
decision semantics: 𝑔 first determines participation admissibility under the prevailing continuation 
state and history; only then does 𝑏 allocate opportunity. Reversing this order reinstates the very 
forecast-centric assumption the framework identifies as incomplete. 
The paper traces the Structural Failure Chain through four historical phases and examines the 
emerging transitional layers (Phases V–VII) that are shifting quantitative systems from exposure 
conditioning toward admissibility-constrained and Bellman-governed architectures. Quantitative 
finance is undergoing a profound ontological transition—from the pursuit of forecast closure to the 
establishment of participation closure. 
Participation governance is emerging as a first-class financial primitive. Rather than functioning as a 
secondary risk-management overlay, it defines the admissible domain within which forecasting and 
optimization can legitimately operate. 
Keywords: Quantitative Finance, Participation Admissibility, Forecast Closure, Participation Closure, 
Bellman Optimality, Decision Ontology, Structural Evolution，Participation Governance

1. Introduction 
1.1 Quantitative Finance as a Tool-Evolution Narrative 
The historical development of quantitative finance is most commonly narrated as a story of 
continuous technological progress. Under this interpretation, the field advances through successive 
improvements in modelling capability, computational power, statistical methodology, and dataprocessing capacity. 
Early frameworks were dominated by equilibrium-based statistical models, including mean-variance 
optimisation, factor pricing, and derivative valuation theory. During the 1990s and early 2000s, 
attention shifted toward statistical arbitrage, large-scale signal extraction, and data-driven prediction 
systems. More recent decades have witnessed the rise of machine learning architectures, deep neural 
networks, reinforcement learning, and large-scale computational optimisation. Parallel advances in 
stochastic analysis produced rough volatility models, neural stochastic differential equations, 
geometric signatures, and manifold-based representations of financial dynamics. 
Within this narrative, progress is understood primarily as technological refinement. New frameworks 
emerge because they deliver more accurate forecasts, richer statistical representations, greater 
computational efficiency, or enhanced modelling flexibility. The central assumption is that the 
underlying structure of the financial decision problem remains fundamentally stable, while the 
analytical tools used to address it continue to improve. 
This view captures an important dimension of the field’s history. Quantitative finance has indeed 
experienced repeated expansions in modelling sophistication, data scale, and representational power. 
Many significant advances can be naturally understood within this technological progression. 
This interpretation leaves a deeper structural question unresolved. It explains how modelling 
frameworks become more sophisticated, but it does not adequately explain why successive 
generations of quantitative systems repeatedly encounter fundamental limitations that force the field 
to redefine its core mathematical objects. 
1.2 The Limits of the Standard Evolution View 
Although the tool-evolution narrative accounts for many important developments, it leaves several 
critical features of quantitative finance insufficiently explained. 
Most importantly, it treats advances primarily as improvements in modelling capability. Successive 
generations of systems are seen as superior because they produce more accurate forecasts, better 
representations of stochastic dynamics, more expressive statistical structures, or more efficient 
optimisation procedures. The underlying financial decision problem itself is assumed to remain 
essentially unchanged. 
History, however, reveals a recurrent pattern that cannot be fully accounted for by incremental 
analytical refinement alone. New frameworks do not emerge merely because they are computationally 
superior. In many cases, they arise because the dominant mathematical objects of the preceding 
generation lose their ability to maintain representational and decision closure under evolving market 
conditions. 
Equilibrium-based statistical frameworks struggled with adaptive market behaviour and nonstationary relationships. Continuous-time stochastic models addressed dynamic path 
behaviour yet remained vulnerable to regime instability, endogenous feedback, and structural 

2 
 
transitions. Regime-based and hidden-state systems introduced greater persistence awareness 
but continued to encounter difficulties during transition periods. More recent geometric and 
structural learning approaches have substantially improved stochastic representation fidelity, 
still leave unresolved critical questions concerning decision admissibility under dynamically 
changing market conditions. 
These recurring transitions suggest that the evolution of quantitative finance involves more than 
progressive improvements in predictive technology. Across multiple generations, the field has 
repeatedly encountered situations in which accurate representations of market behaviour fail to 
produce correspondingly complete financial decision systems. Improvements in statistical accuracy or 
geometric consistency do not automatically translate into completeness of the underlying decision 
architecture. 
Under the standard narrative, such failures are typically interpreted as local modelling limitations. An 
alternative structural interpretation suggests they reflect deeper instabilities in the mathematical 
objects used to formulate financial decisions. From this perspective, the history of quantitative finance 
is not simply a story of better tools, but a sequence of transitions between unstable representational 
frameworks. 
1.3 Structural Evolution Hypothesis 
This paper proposes an alternative interpretation: the historical development of quantitative finance is 
best understood as a recurrent process of structural instability and mathematical object replacement. 
Each major phase of the field has been organised around a dominant mathematical object through 
which financial decision systems are represented and acted upon. These objects initially provide 
sufficient closure under prevailing market conditions. Over time, increasing market complexity 
exposes limitations that cannot be resolved through incremental refinement within the existing 
framework. 
New generations of systems therefore emerge not merely because they offer superior computational 
techniques, but because the preceding objects lose their capacity to maintain stable representation and 
decision closure. The evolution of quantitative finance can thus be interpreted as a sequence of 
transitions between successively more unstable representational frameworks. 
Under this hypothesis, the major historical shifts correspond to changes in the primary object through 
which markets are understood: from equilibrium-based statistics to conditional forecasting, from 
stochastic dynamics to regime and state representations, and from trajectory modelling to geometric 
and path-dependent structures. Each transition reflects the emergence of structural phenomena that the 
previous framework could not adequately accommodate. 
1.4 Core Thesis of This Paper 
The central argument of this paper is that the historical evolution of quantitative finance is driven by 
repeated failures of representational closure within prevailing financial decision frameworks. 
The major phases are distinguished less by differences in modelling sophistication or computational 
scale than by the dominant mathematical object around which decision systems are organised. These 
objects provide temporary stability under specific market conditions but eventually encounter 
structural limits as markets become more adaptive, state-dependent, path-sensitive, and endogenously 
unstable. 

3 
 
This paper further argues that many contemporary quantitative architectures continue to inherit an 
implicit forecast-centred decision structure, even as their stochastic representations have grown highly 
sophisticated. Under such systems, improved representations of market evolution are still expected to 
produce correspondingly improved decisions. In environments characterised by state persistence, 
transition asymmetry, path dependence, and endogenous instability, this relationship no longer holds 
reliably. 
More accurate representations of stochastic dynamics do not necessarily eliminate structural 
incompleteness in the decision architecture. Equivalent forecasts or equivalent local stochastic 
representations can remain compatible with materially different optimal actions under different 
continuation structures. 
The following sections examine this instability as a recurrent feature in the historical evolution of 
quantitative finance, tracing the conditions under which dominant mathematical objects lose 
representational closure and give rise to new frameworks. 
2. Existing Interpretations of Quant Evolution 
2.1 Technology-Stack Interpretation 
2.1.1 From Factors to Machine Learning 
One of the most common interpretations of quantitative finance views its history as a progression 
from simple statistical models toward complex predictive systems. In this narrative, the field advances 
through successive gains in modelling flexibility, computational scale, and data-processing power. 
Early frameworks relied heavily on factor-based representations of market behaviour. Linear multifactor models, cross-sectional return decompositions, and statistical arbitrage systems sought to 
extract persistent statistical relationships from historical data and convert them into predictive signals. 
The central goal was the identification of stable return regularities capable of generating systematic 
forecasting advantage. 
As computational resources and financial datasets expanded, more flexible statistical learning 
techniques were incorporated: nonlinear regression, ensemble methods, kernel-based approaches, and 
tree-based architectures. In recent years, deep neural networks, sequence models, attention 
mechanisms, and reinforcement learning systems have further extended representational capacity into 
higher dimensions and more complex temporal dependencies. 
This progression is typically understood as a continuous expansion of predictive power. Each new 
generation of models is seen as better able to detect and represent finer statistical structures within 
financial data. The underlying decision problem, however, is assumed to remain structurally 
unchanged: the central task is still the extraction of predictive information from observable market 
variables, followed by action generation. 
Under this interpretation, differences between successive generations lie primarily in representational 
capacity, statistical flexibility, and computational scale. The evolution from factor models to machine 
learning thus appears as a steady expansion of predictive architecture rather than a fundamental 
transition in the nature of financial objects. 

4 
 
2.1.2 AI as Forecast Acceleration 
More recent developments have extended the technology-stack narrative through large-scale statistical 
learning and high-dimensional predictive architectures. Advances in model scale and complexity are 
generally interpreted as further accelerations of forecasting capability. 
Deep neural networks, transformers, sequence models, and reinforcement learning systems have 
dramatically expanded the range of information that can be processed simultaneously — from prices 
and returns to order-book dynamics, textual data, macroeconomic releases, and cross-asset 
behavioural signals. Modern systems now attempt to capture temporal dependencies, nonlinear 
interactions, path sensitivity, and multi-scale stochastic behaviour within unified frameworks. 
While these architectures represent genuine increases in expressive power, they remain organised 
around the same core objective: improving the representation of future market evolution. The 
technology-stack interpretation thus continues to treat advances in quantitative finance primarily as 
enhancements in representational and forecasting capability, rather than as changes in the underlying 
mathematical objects governing decision systems. 
2.1.3 Implicit Forecast-Centric Assumption 
Despite vast differences in modelling architecture, computational scale, and statistical complexity, the 
technology-stack interpretation preserves a remarkably continuous formulation of the underlying 
financial problem. 
Across generations, the central objective remains the extraction of predictive structure from market 
information, expressed in the directional mapping: 
𝑋𝑡→𝑟̂𝑡+1 →𝑎𝑡 
Advances are interpreted as improvements in the forecasting process itself. More flexible 
architectures are expected to produce more accurate representations of future market behaviour, from 
which financial decisions naturally follow. Participation is treated as downstream from prediction. 
Once a sufficiently attractive forecast is obtained, the remaining tasks — position sizing, portfolio 
optimisation, execution, and risk adjustment — are viewed as conditional upon that forecast. 
This forecast-centric assumption often remains implicit precisely because it persists across highly 
divergent modelling traditions. Whether using linear factors, ensemble methods, or large-scale neural 
architectures, the fundamental logic stays the same: refined representations of market behaviour are 
expected to yield correspondingly improved decisions. The technology-stack view therefore frames 
the evolution of quantitative finance primarily as progress in predictive representation, rather than as a 
transformation in the deeper objects governing financial decision systems. 
2.2 Market-Efficiency Interpretation 
2.2.1 Adaptive Market Hypothesis 
Adaptive market frameworks treat financial markets as evolving competitive environments rather than 
stationary statistical systems. Market behaviour changes over time as participants adapt to observed 
regularities, competitive pressure, regulatory shifts, and evolving market structure. 

5 
 
Predictive relationships are not assumed to be permanent. Statistical edges that initially provide 
forecasting advantage tend to weaken as strategies become crowded, imitated, or absorbed into market 
dynamics. Quantitative finance thus evolves within a continuously adapting competitive landscape. 
The Adaptive Market Hypothesis formalised part of this view by replacing static efficiency with 
dynamic efficiency conditions. Periods of predictability and relative efficiency coexist, with statistical 
structure varying across regimes and competitive intensity. 
This framework explains several recurring empirical phenomena: alpha decay, instability in crosssectional relationships, changing signal effectiveness, and the periodic collapse of once-persistent 
anomalies. 
2.2.2 Alpha Decay and Market Adaptation 
A central observation in adaptive interpretations is the repeated decay of previously successful 
strategies. Statistical relationships that once delivered persistent forecasting advantage often weaken 
as participation increases, imitation spreads, and competitive saturation grows. 
Alpha decay is not merely modelling failure. It emerges from the interaction between predictive 
systems and the markets they inhabit. As more capital chases the same regularities, market 
participants collectively alter the statistical structure from which those regularities arose. 
Adaptive frameworks therefore treat predictive relationships as temporary features of evolving 
competitive systems rather than fixed probabilistic laws. The source of instability shifts from 
modelling insufficiency to the endogenous evolution of the market itself. 
2.2.3 Limits of Prediction-Based Adaptation 
While adaptive market interpretations successfully explain strategy decay and changing statistical 
structure, they leave the directional architecture of decision-making largely intact. The fundamental 
mapping — forecast to action — remains unchanged. Predictive systems become more adaptive and 
environment-sensitive, but financial decisions continue to be generated primarily from forecasts. 
Instability is still framed mainly as a problem of prediction. Difficulties under state persistence, 
transition asymmetry, liquidity discontinuities, and path-dependent instability are acknowledged, 
equivalent forecasts are still expected to support comparable decisions once properly adapted. The 
assumption that participation remains a downstream consequence of prediction is largely preserved. 
2.3 Microstructure Interpretation 
2.3.1 Execution-Centric Quant Systems 
A third major interpretation emphasises changes in market microstructure, execution technology, and 
liquidity mechanics. Financial markets are viewed as highly fragmented execution environments 
shaped by order flow, latency competition, transaction costs, and market impact. 
In this view, quantitative performance depends not only on signal generation but critically on the 
ability to execute efficiently in real-world conditions. Predictive signals that appear profitable under 
idealised assumptions often produce markedly different outcomes once slippage, liquidity 
fragmentation, and market impact are incorporated. 

6 
 
This perspective drove the development of high-frequency trading systems, smart-order routing, and 
execution-optimisation frameworks focused on latency reduction, fill quality, and order-book 
interaction. 
2.3.2 Liquidity Fragmentation and Latency Competition 
The expansion of electronic trading fragmented liquidity across venues, elevated execution speed as a 
competitive variable, and made market interaction highly dynamic. Realised performance became 
acutely sensitive to timing, routing efficiency, and short-horizon liquidity dynamics. 
Even identical predictive signals could yield materially different outcomes depending on execution 
quality. Microstructure-aware systems thus became essential for managing liquidity access, 
transaction costs, and order-flow interaction. 
2.3.3 Execution Realism Without Decision Transition 
Microstructure interpretations significantly improved practical realism. But they did not 
fundamentally alter the underlying decision ontology. Forecasts continue to generate trading 
intentions, while execution systems primarily govern how those intentions are realised. Participation 
itself remains implicitly treated as available once a predictive opportunity is identified. The forecastto-action structure stays intact. 
2.4 Complex Systems Interpretation 
2.4.1 Markets as Complex Adaptive Systems 
Complex-systems interpretations view financial markets as adaptive interaction systems rather than 
equilibrium or isolated stochastic processes. Market behaviour emerges from large-scale interactions 
among heterogeneous participants under changing informational, strategic, and liquidity conditions. 
Instability is seen as potentially endogenous — arising from leverage feedback, crowded positioning, 
liquidity contraction, and reflexive behaviour — rather than solely from external shocks or modelling 
error. 
2.4.2 Endogenous Instability and Reflexivity 
Reflexive feedback intensifies this process: prices shape expectations, expectations drive positioning, 
and positioning feeds back into prices. Small disturbances can amplify into large dislocations through 
recursive interaction. This framework explains liquidity collapses, forced deleveraging, and 
correlation breakdowns more convincingly than purely exogenous models. 
2.4.3 Missing Decision-Governance Layer 
Despite strengthening the representation of endogenous instability, complex-systems approaches have 
largely left the directional structure of decision-making unchanged. Financial decisions continue to 
flow primarily from predictive or state-conditioned representations. Reflexive instability altered the 
environment, but not the assumption that participation remains downstream from forecast or 
optimisation. A dedicated governance layer for participation admissibility under recursively changing 
conditions remains underdeveloped.

2.5 Reinforcement Learning and Control Interpretation 
2.5.1 Sequential Decision Systems 
Reinforcement learning and control-theoretic frameworks reframed financial decision-making as 
sequential processes under uncertainty. Actions affect future states, opportunity sets, and subsequent 
decision conditions. Performance depends on recursive interaction between current choices and future 
trajectories. 
2.5.2 Policy Optimization as a Shift Beyond Forecasting 
Forecasts became components within larger policy structures linking state evolution, action selection, 
and continuation value. This represented a genuine advance beyond isolated prediction toward 
trajectory optimisation. 
2.5.3 Optimal Action Versus Admissible Action 
Nevertheless, most formulations implicitly assumed that admissible actions remained continuously 
available once the state was defined. The distinction between optimal action and admissible action 
was rarely formalised as an independent layer. In practice, a statistically optimal policy may still 
generate non-executable or path-destructive outcomes under stressed continuation conditions. Control 
theory addressed trajectory dependence but did not fully resolve participation admissibility. 
2.6 Shared Structural Limitation 
2.6.1 The Forecast-to-Participation Assumption 
Despite profound differences in methodology, most quantitative frameworks share a common 
structural presumption: once a sufficiently attractive forecast exists, participation remains 
continuously available. 
This assumption appears across statistical factor models, adaptive frameworks, microstructure 
systems, complex adaptive interpretations, and even sequential control architectures. The 
disagreement typically concerns optimisation structure or adaptation mechanism, while the continuous 
availability of participation itself remains largely unquestioned. 
2.6.2 Why Existing Frameworks Remain Participation-Blind 
The persistence of this assumption stems from the dominant objects around which systems were 
constructed. Forecasts, states, trajectories, and optimisation have been central; participation has been 
treated as downstream and implicitly valid. As markets have grown more persistent, asymmetric, and 
path-dependent, this implicit validity has become untenable. The pressure has accumulated not from 
insufficient sophistication, but from a deeper ontological gap: the lack of an independent framework 
governing participation admissibility under dynamically changing continuation structures.

3. The Structural Failure Chain of Quantitative Finance 
3.1 Phase I — Statistical Prediction Finance 
3.1.1 Core Mathematical Object 
From the 1980s through the late 1990s, quantitative finance was organised around conditional return 
prediction. Markets were treated as statistical environments from which future price behaviour could 
be inferred from observable information. The central mathematical object was the conditional 
expectation of future return: 
𝐸[𝑟𝑡+1 |𝑋𝑡] 
The decision problem followed directly: if predictive structure could be extracted from historical data, 
improved estimation was expected to translate naturally into superior financial action. Cross-sectional 
factor models, statistical arbitrage systems, econometric forecasts, and later machine-learning 
architectures all operated within variations of this same structure. 
This framework flourished in a specific market environment. Electronic trading was still immature, 
institutional quantitative participation was limited, factor crowding was weak, and statistical 
inefficiencies often persisted long enough to remain economically exploitable. Expanding 
computational power and growing datasets reinforced confidence in statistical prediction. 
3.1.2 Historical Success Conditions and Forecast Sufficiency Assumption 
During this period, the relationship between prediction and participation appeared sufficiently stable. 
Stronger expected returns implied greater participation; weaker returns implied reduction. The path 
from forecast to position seemed structurally direct. Success reinforced a broader belief: better 
estimation produced better decisions. Prediction became the dominant object of quantitative finance. 
The deeper assumption was that participation itself remained continuously available once a 
sufficiently attractive forecast was identified. Under relatively stable conditions, this assumption was 
rarely tested and appeared operationally sufficient. 
3.1.3 Adaptive Market Failure and Forecast Non-Uniqueness  
As quantitative participation expanded, markets became more adaptive. Signals that once persisted 
began to weaken under crowding and imitation. Cross-sectional relationships destabilised. Statistical 
arbitrage strategies experienced abrupt breakdowns during deleveraging. The instability was no longer 
solely a matter of estimation error — it arose from the interaction between predictive systems and the 
markets they inhabited. 
A deeper weakness surfaced: equivalent forecasts no longer implied equivalent optimal actions. The 
same expected return could arise in environments with materially different liquidity, leverage, or 
continuation structures. Prediction alone ceased to uniquely determine participation behaviour. The 
forecast-to-action mapping began losing closure. 
3.1.4 Transition Toward Stochastic Dynamics 

9 
 
The limitations of static prediction forced the field to absorb time. Volatility persisted, correlations 
shifted, and path behaviour mattered. Quantitative finance moved from conditional expectations to 
stochastic processes. The market came to be seen as a dynamic process rather than a static statistical 
surface. While the mathematical object changed, the deeper decision architecture — forecast 
governing participation — largely survived. Phase I ended with a partial repair: the field had become 
dynamic in representation, but had not separated market motion from participation validity. 
3.2 Phase II — Continuous-Time / Stochastic Dynamics Finance 
3.2.1 Core Mathematical Object 
By the late 1990s and early 2000s, the dominant object shifted to continuous stochastic dynamics: 
d𝑋𝑡= 𝑏(𝑋𝑡)𝑑𝑡+ 𝜎(𝑋𝑡)𝑑𝑊𝑡 
This framework captured volatility persistence, path dependence, derivative pricing, and dynamic 
hedging. Uncertainty was no longer external noise but embedded within market motion itself. 
Trajectories, not isolated returns, became central. 
The forecast-to-action structure persisted. Improved representation of stochastic evolution was still 
expected to produce improved decisions. Participation remained largely downstream from trajectory 
modelling. 
3.2.2 Volatility Clustering and Path Dependence, and Crisis Regimes 
Volatility displayed memory. Large movements tended to cluster. Liquidity contraction persisted. 
Path dependence mattered: identical spot prices reached via different histories implied different 
continuation risks. Derivative hedging became recursive, feeding back into price motion. 
Crisis regimes exposed the architecture’s fragility. Liquidity deteriorated unevenly. Funding pressure 
triggered forced deleveraging. Participation continuity fractured even as local stochastic motion 
remained modellable. The market could still be represented, but participation could no longer be 
reliably maintained across unstable trajectories. 
3.2.3 Regime Dependence and Emergence of Conditional Market Structure 
Continuous stochastic finance assumed a unified probabilistic environment. Crisis regimes revealed 
otherwise: comparable volatility could produce entirely different participation consequences 
depending on liquidity, funding, and leverage backdrop. Trajectory representation remained useful 
locally but ceased to be self-sufficient for organising participation across changing market structures. 
The deeper inconsistency persisted. Improved stochastic representation did not guarantee stable 
participation. Phase II ended with a more sophisticated description of motion, the fundamental 
separation between market evolution and participation admissibility remained unresolved. 
3.3 Phase III — Regime / State Finance 
3.3.1 Core Mathematical Object 
The limitations of pure trajectory models led to the introduction of market state 𝑆𝑡 alongside 
observable dynamics 𝑋𝑡: 

10 
 
(𝑋𝑡, 𝑆𝑡) 
Observable trajectories no longer uniquely identified the underlying condition governing their 
continuation. Regime-switching, hidden Markov, and state-space models emerged to capture this 
hidden structure. 
3.3.2 Regime Persistence and Transition Asymmetry 
States themselves exhibited persistence and asymmetric transitions. Stable regimes could persist 
longer than expected; fragile regimes could deteriorate rapidly. Transitions were not symmetric: 
deleveraging spread faster than recovery. This temporal structure made state awareness essential. 
3.3.3 State-Aware Allocation and Transition-Zone Failure 
Allocation became conditional on state: 𝑎𝑡= 𝑓(𝑋𝑡, 𝑆𝑡) . But most systems still attached state 
information to forecast-driven rules. The deepest failures occurred in transition zones — periods when 
observable behaviour remained stable while underlying conditions deteriorated. Exposure continued 
expanding precisely when participation was becoming structurally fragile. Forecast quality alone 
could no longer govern participation. 
3.3.4 Transition Toward Structural Learning 
Learning itself began shifting. Trajectory prediction was no longer sufficient. The system needed to 
recognise conditions under which participation itself remained viable. Hidden state, persistence, and 
transition instability moved from auxiliary variables to central concerns. 
3.4 Phase IV — Geometric / Structural Stochastic Learning 
3.4.1 Core Mathematical Object 
As transition deterioration exposed the limits of trajectory representation, the field moved toward 
ordered evolution histories and geometric structures. The object shifted from local increments to pathdependent signatures and manifold-constrained dynamics. 
Rough paths, geometric signatures, and structure-preserving learning captured ordering, fluctuation 
accumulation, and nonlinear continuation. These advances significantly improved representational 
fidelity. 
3.4.2 Geometry Correctness Without Participation Closure 
Geometry correctness did not guarantee participation closure. A system could accurately represent 
continuation structure while still participating in regions where continuation had already become 
fragile. The rupture had moved closer to the decision layer itself: improved understanding of how the 
market evolved did not automatically resolve whether participation should continue. 
3.4.3 Why Dynamics Still Dominates the Ontology 
Despite repeated ruptures, the centre of financial ontology remained anchored in the representation of 
market evolution. Participation fragility was acknowledged through overlays and constraints but 

11 
 
rarely elevated to a primary object. The field continued treating trajectory and state modelling as the 
route to decision quality. 
3.4.4 Transition Toward Participation-Centric Systems 
Correct representation no longer guaranteed stable participation. Systems could understand 
continuation behaviour accurately remain exposed in regions where continuation had become 
untenable. The primary problem was shifting from “how the market evolves” to “under what 
conditions participation itself should remain active.” 
The rupture had reached the decision architecture. Participation admissibility was beginning to 
emerge as the next central financial object. 
4. Emerging Transitional Layers Beyond Structural Dynamics 
4.1 Emerging Phase V — State-Conditioned Participation 
The preceding phases documented the historical evolution of quantitative finance through 
progressively more sophisticated representations of market dynamics. Across all phases, one 
structural issue remained unresolved: the continuity of participation. 
As markets grew more interconnected and endogenously unstable, the primary locus of failure 
migrated from representational insufficiency to the architecture of participation itself. The following 
sections do not describe completed historical phases but rather transitional structures now emerging at 
this critical boundary. 
4.1.1 From Forecast Prediction to Exposure Conditioning 
Each major expansion of market representation preserved larger portions of unstable dynamics, the 
participation rupture persisted. Closure pressure therefore shifted from local modelling deficiencies to 
the core of the financial decision architecture. 
Forecasting capabilities continued to advance. At the same time, synthetic leverage, layered 
derivatives, shadow dependencies, and cross-institutional coupling intensified endogenous fragility. 
Under these conditions, comparable forecasts began producing materially different participation 
outcomes depending on surrounding liquidity, funding, and leverage structures. 
Financial systems responded with exposure-conditioning mechanisms—volatility targeting, risk 
parity, dynamic leverage scaling, and tail-risk overlays. These innovations did not replace forecastcentric logic but modified the translation from forecast to exposure. They compressed participation 
intensity during instability expansion even when directional forecasts remained positive, conditioning 
depth on survivability rather than expected return alone. 
Nevertheless, exposure conditioning remained incomplete. It adjusted how much to participate while 
still presuming that participation itself remained continuously admissible in some scaled form. The 
deeper rupture—whether participation should occur at all—remained unaddressed. 
4.1.2 The Persistence Assumption Behind Exposure Conditioning 

12 
 
Despite these adjustments, the underlying participation ontology stayed largely unchanged. Systems 
continued to assume that participation remained feasible provided exposure could be sufficiently 
compressed, hedged, or dynamically scaled. 
This persistence assumption became embedded in institutional practice. Under severe stress, 
participation continuity decoupled from exposure magnitude. Liquidity evaporation, funding 
discontinuities, and synchronized deleveraging demonstrated that even sharply reduced positions 
could become unsustainable. Exposure control operated within scalable participation logic, whereas 
admissibility pressure emerged precisely when participation itself approached structural discontinuity. 
4.1.3 Transition-Aware Participation Reduction 
As fragility migrated to transition intervals, systems developed greater sensitivity to underlying 
continuation deterioration that was not visible in price trajectories. Liquidity could weaken before 
volatility expanded; correlations could converge before broad stress materialized. 
Institutions responded with transition-aware reduction: tightening constraints during accelerating 
volatility regimes, crowded positioning, or deteriorating market depth. These mechanisms improved 
responsiveness but remained limited—they altered when and how participation intensity should 
change, without questioning whether participation itself remained admissible. 
4.1.4 Persistence-Conditioned Exposure Systems 
Further evolution led to conditioning exposure on estimated continuation durability across multiple 
horizons. Participation was restricted when liquidity continuity, funding stability, or diversification 
persistence appeared fragile, even under deceptively calm conditions. 
These refinements extended state-conditioned logic but stayed trapped within the assumption of 
scalable participation. The architecture could now better anticipate fragility, still lacked an 
independent framework for declaring participation fundamentally inadmissible. 
4.1.5 Structural Limitation of Continuous Participation Assumptions 
Across all variants of exposure conditioning, a common premise endured: participation continuity 
could always be preserved through sufficiently sophisticated scaling and adaptation. This assumption 
approached its structural limit under severe continuation stress. When liquidity, funding, and 
correlation shocks propagated faster than adjustment mechanisms could respond, scalable 
participation logic itself lost closure. 
The field thus confronted a deeper question: whether certain continuation regimes render participation 
impermissible regardless of forecast quality or exposure adjustment. This boundary marked the 
transition from Phase V toward admissibility-constrained architectures. 
4.2 Emerging Phase VI — Admissibility-Constrained Participation 
The transitional architectures of Phase V achieved increasing sophistication in exposure conditioning. 
Participation could be dynamically compressed during volatility expansion, selectively reduced during 
unstable transitions, and constrained according to persistence durability across multiple horizons. 
Beneath these refinements lay an enduring structural assumption: participation itself remained 
continuously admissible in principle, provided its intensity could be sufficiently adjusted. 
This assumption would prove to be the final frontier of forecast-centred finance. 

13 
 
4.2.1 State-Dependent Action Spaces 
As continuation fragility deepened across liquidity, funding, leverage, and cross-market persistence 
structures, the operational uniformity of participation began to disintegrate. What had long appeared 
as a broadly open action space fractured into conditional regions. 
Certain forms of participation destabilized far earlier than others. Liquidity-taking execution that 
remained manageable under dispersed conditions became difficult once liquidity continuity 
weakened. Highly leveraged positioning turned inaccessible when funding stability eroded. Inventorysensitive market-making could vanish abruptly during correlated stress intervals, even while other 
segments of the market remained locally active. 
Reducing overall participation scale no longer resolved these discontinuities uniformly. Some 
participation structures remained maintainable under unstable conditions, while others fractured or 
disappeared entirely. Execution pathways narrowed abruptly as liquidity depth deteriorated. Leverage 
expansion vanished under funding pressure. Entire forms of participation began vanishing under 
specific continuation regimes rather than merely shrinking. 
The feasible action space had become unmistakably state-dependent. Participation no longer existed 
within a universally open operational domain. Different continuation environments produced 
materially different regions of executable actions. Pathways available under one set of conditions 
could narrow sharply — or vanish altogether — under another. The market no longer offered a 
continuous action space; it presented a conditional one, governed by the underlying continuation 
structure. 
4.2.2 Participation Legality Versus Exposure Optimization  
This fragmentation exposed a deeper fracture within the architecture. Financial systems had long 
treated participation primarily as an optimization problem. Actions that appeared locally attractive 
under forecast logic, volatility estimates, or risk constraints were presumed inherently executable. 
That presumption eroded. Certain participation forms could remain locally optimal while 
simultaneously becoming structurally unsustainable. Highly leveraged positions continued to appear 
attractive even as funding continuity weakened beneath them. Liquidity-taking strategies could 
preserve short-term profitability while market depth collapsed. Forecast-positive positioning could 
remain theoretically sound while persistence continuity fractured across broader market structures. 
Optimization and sustainability thus diverged into distinct structural objects. Exposure optimization 
evaluated local attractiveness. Participation legality evaluated whether a given action remained 
executable and maintainable within the prevailing continuation environment. Actions that satisfied 
optimization logic could simultaneously undermine their own continuation viability. Local 
profitability no longer guaranteed operational sustainability. 
4.2.3 Hard Participation Gating 
When optimization alone could no longer guarantee sustainable participation, even sophisticated 
scaling proved insufficient. Under severe continuation stress, participation compression no longer 
preserved continuity. Liquidity could evaporate before exposure reduction fully transmitted. Funding 
could tighten faster than leverage could be unwound. Execution continuity could fracture while local 
optimization signals still appeared attractive. 

14 
 
Financial systems responded with hard participation gating — not merely modulating intensity, but 
temporarily suspending or outright blocking entire forms of participation. Trading interruption 
systems halted activity during extreme volatility regimes. Liquidity-sensitive engines suspended 
aggressive execution once market depth fell below executable thresholds. Funding-sensitive structures 
blocked leverage once financing continuity became unreliable. Certain market-making systems 
withdrew entirely when inventory continuity across correlated stresses could no longer be maintained. 
Participation no longer narrowed gradually. In critical regimes, entire regions of the action space 
became temporarily inaccessible. Hard gating marked the transition from “how much to participate” 
toward “whether participation is permissible at all.” 
4.2.4 Continuation Constraints and Transition Legality 
Hard gating revealed an even more fundamental constraint: not only individual continuation 
environments, but the transitions between them, carried irreducible participation risk. 
Earlier architectures had treated transitions as manageable adjustment intervals during which exposure 
could be compressed or repositioned. This assumption weakened. Certain transitions destabilized 
participation faster than any scaling or gating mechanism could adapt. Liquidity continuity could 
collapse mid-transition. Funding conditions could fracture before positions could be rebalanced. 
Forecast-positive strategies could become unsustainable precisely during the shift across regimes. 
Participation continuity thus depended not only on the stability of individual states, but on the legality 
of the pathways connecting them. Some continuation environments remained locally maintainable 
once reached, the transition into those environments produced destabilizing conditions that ordinary 
adjustment could not bridge. 
4.2.5 Admissibility as More Than Risk Management 
These developments exposed the fundamental limitation of treating participation primarily through 
risk management and exposure adjustment. Reducing magnitude, tightening volatility constraints, or 
applying hard gates still operated under the implicit belief that participation itself remained 
continuously maintainable provided intensity was sufficiently controlled. 
Transition discontinuities shattered this belief. Participation continuity could break even after 
aggressive exposure compression. Certain forms of participation failed not because exposure was too 
large, but because continuation itself was no longer crossable. In portions of the participation 
structure, continuity began failing before ordinary adjustment processes could stabilize conditions. 
Risk management and exposure optimization had reached their structural ceiling. Some participation 
forms now required something more fundamental — a prior determination of permissibility. 
Continuation itself began demanding explicit admissibility before any optimization or scaling could 
proceed. 
Participation could no longer be preserved through adjustment alone. In critical regimes, continuation 
first required permission. 
4.3 Emerging Phase VII — Bellman-Governed Participation 
The architectures of Phase VI marked the outer limit of scalable participation. Exposure could be 
conditioned, transitions could be monitored, and entire regions of the action space could be gated. 

15 
 
Even these advanced mechanisms ultimately operated under the same foundational assumption: 
participation itself remained continuously admissible provided sufficient adjustment was applied. 
This assumption would prove unsustainable. The system now confronted the necessity of a deeper 
structural transition 
4.3.1 Forecast Failure as a Primary Decision Object 
Forecast structures had long remained central to participation decisions. As continuation instability 
intensified, their role began to change. 
Certain forms of participation could remain strongly forecast-positive while simultaneously eroding 
continuation stability. Local optimization signals could stay attractive even as liquidity continuity 
deteriorated beneath the surface. Systems could continue allocating capital toward forecast-favourable 
opportunities while quietly accumulating exposure to liquidity fragmentation, transition 
discontinuities, and future breakdown. 
Forecast failure thus acquired a new and more profound meaning. Previously, it had primarily 
signified prediction error. A different form of failure now emerged: forecast-guided participation that 
remained locally profitable systematically undermined future continuation viability. 
Forecasts remained essential for evaluating opportunity. Forecast quality alone was no longer 
sufficient to govern participation across unstable continuation environments. Forecast failure itself 
became a primary object requiring explicit governance within sequential decision structures. 
4.3.2 Participation Admissibility as a First-Class Object 
The recognition of forecast failure as a governance problem exposed a deeper instability. Earlier 
architectures had maintained a clear hierarchy: forecasts evaluated opportunity attractiveness, while 
risk and exposure systems adjusted participation intensity. 
Beneath this hierarchy lay a critical assumption — participation itself remained continuously 
maintainable as long as intensity could be sufficiently adjusted. 
Continuation discontinuities shattered this assumption. Exposure reduction could already be in 
progress while continuation pathways simultaneously lost operational viability. Forecast-positive 
positions could still appear attractive while execution or funding continuity eroded beneath them. 
Participation could become fragile before conventional adjustment mechanisms completed their 
response. 
Participation could no longer be treated as continuously deployable by default. Continuation 
maintainability had to be evaluated before forecast-guided participation could proceed. Forecast 
attractiveness and continuation permissibility were no longer aligned by default. A new ordering 
pressure emerged: admissibility became a first-class decision object, prior to optimization. 
4.3.3 Bellman-Consistent Participation Control 
As admissibility pressures intensified, participation systems began to exhibit a more fundamental shift 
in structure. 
Earlier architectures largely behaved as though forecast attractiveness alone could determine 
participation deployment. As instability grew, participation systems started to treat the current 
continuation state as a primary constraint:

4.3.4 Sequential Admissibility Systems 
This recursion forced further evolution. Participation that remained admissible today could become

today’s participation did not foreclose tomorrow’s survival. 
4.3.5 Transition Toward Participation Governance Architecture 
Today’s participation had already begun shaping tomorrow’s participation conditions. Local decisions

Current participation could no longer be evaluated independently from its consequences on future 
continuation structures. Forecasts still evaluated opportunity. Risk systems still adjusted intensity. 
Neither was sufficient once participation continuity itself became conditional on future states. 
The architecture was transitioning toward a new foundation: Participation Governance — a dedicated 
layer in which admissibility, rather than forecast or scaled optimization, served as the primary 
decision primitive.

5.2 Bellman Incompleteness Under Dynamic Market Structure 
Forecast-only policies implicitly assume that the local forecast 𝑟̂𝑡 is sufficient to determine the locally 
optimal action. This assumption fails under three fundamental features of real financial markets: 
• State persistence: Future continuation value depends on the underlying state 𝑠𝑡. The same 
forecast can arise in materially different continuation regimes. 
• Transition asymmetry: Downside and recovery paths are not symmetric. Equivalent 
forecasts can lead to sharply different continuation values depending on the direction of 
transition. 
• Path dependence: Equivalent observable states reached via different histories imply 
different admissible actions. 
In all three cases, equal forecasts no longer imply equal continuation values, and therefore no 
longer imply equal optimal actions.

5.4 From Forecast Closure to Participation Closure 
For decades, quantitative finance pursued forecast closure: the belief that sufficiently accurate 
representation of market behaviour would eventually yield complete decision architectures. This 
pursuit has reached its structural limit. 
Equal forecasts no longer imply equal continuation values. Equal continuation values no longer imply 
equal admissible actions. The forecast object can no longer maintain decision closure by itself. The 
field is undergoing a profound transition — from the pursuit of forecast closure to the establishment 
of participation closure. 
Forecasts will continue to evaluate opportunity. But participation governance will determine whether 
that opportunity is admissible under the prevailing continuation structure. The centre of financial 
ontology is shifting. The next era of quantitative finance will be defined not by who forecasts best, but 
by how participation is governed under unstable continuation conditions. 
6. Discussion 
6.1 Quantitative Finance as an Ontological Evolution Process 
The historical development of quantitative finance is best understood not merely as a progression of 
more sophisticated techniques, but as a sequence of ontological shifts — successive reorganizations 
around changing primary financial objects. 
Early frameworks centred on price and return. Subsequent generations elevated volatility, covariance, 
factor exposures, latent states, stochastic trajectories, path dependence, and geometric structures. Each 

19 
 
transition occurred when prevailing objects could no longer absorb the prevailing forms of market 
instability. Previous objects were not discarded; their relative centrality simply diminished as new 
sources of structural pressure emerged. 
This ontological migration reveals a deeper pattern: quantitative finance advances most decisively not 
when it refines existing objects, but when it is forced to recognize new objects as structurally primary. 
The field is now approaching another such transition. The pressure has migrated from representation 
of market evolution toward the governance of participation continuity itself. 
6.2 Why Structural Correctness Does Not Guarantee Decision Closure 
A recurring feature of quantitative finance is that substantial gains in structural representation have 
not always produced corresponding gains in decision closure. Systems have become richer in 
modelling capability — from stochastic calculus to regime-switching models, path-dependent 
learning, and sequential optimization — while recurrent participation instability under unstable 
continuation conditions has persisted. 
Improved representation does not automatically equate to improved decision architecture. A model 
may accurately capture trajectory behaviour, volatility dynamics, or state persistence, still fail to 
resolve whether participation itself remains viable across changing continuation environments. 
Sophisticated representation can coexist with fragile participation continuity. 
This distinction was long obscured by relatively stable market regimes, in which continuation 
conditions remained sufficiently benign for forecast refinement to serve as a reasonable proxy for 
overall decision quality. As markets became more endogenously unstable, interconnected, and pathsensitive, the gap between representational correctness and decision closure widened. Structural 
sophistication advanced, but the deeper ontological problem — governing admissible participation 
under dynamic continuation structures — remained only partially addressed. 
6.3 Participation Governance as an Emerging Financial Primitive 
Participation governance is not an entirely new invention. Market participants have long practiced 
forms of it through discretionary sizing, defensive positioning, liquidity sensitivity, and capital 
preservation. What is new is its elevation from fragmented, implicit practice to a formal, first-class 
financial object. 
For decades, participation adaptation remained subordinate to trajectory-oriented frameworks. It was 
distributed across risk management, execution discipline, and tactical overlays, rarely granted 
independent ontological status. This subordination reflected a deeper asymmetry: trajectory structures 
lend themselves naturally to continuous mathematical formalization, cumulative refinement, and 
coherent narratives. Admissibility, by contrast, often manifests through discontinuity, withdrawal, 
gating, and conditional suspension — phenomena more resistant to smooth representational lineages. 
Participation governance is now emerging as a distinct primitive. It addresses a dimension that 
forecast, state estimation, and trajectory modelling alone cannot fully resolve: whether participation 
itself should remain active under given continuation conditions. Its formalization represents not a 
rejection of earlier objects, but their necessary completion. 
6.4 Limits of Current Forecast-Centric Architectures 

20 
 
Forecast-centric architectures achieved remarkable historical success because they operated in 
environments where participation continuity was sufficiently stable to make forecast quality a 
dominant driver of outcomes. Under deepening liquidity, durable diversification, and relatively 
persistent regimes, incremental improvements in prediction frequently translated into sustained 
performance. 
Those supporting conditions have become less reliable. Liquidity can evaporate more abruptly, 
correlations can converge more violently, persistence assumptions can weaken more rapidly, and 
continuation fragility can propagate faster than forecast-driven adjustments can compensate. In such 
regimes, even highly sophisticated forecast systems can remain directionally sound while generating 
severe participation fragility. 
The limitation is not that forecasts have become useless — they remain essential. Rather, forecastcentric architectures are approaching the boundary of their structural sufficiency. They excel at 
evaluating opportunity within admissible domains, but are strained when the admissibility of those 
domains themselves becomes conditional and unstable. 
6.5 Open Questions and Future Directions 
Several fundamental questions remain open. 
First, how should participation admissibility be formally represented and integrated into sequential 
decision systems? Existing toolkits are highly developed for prediction, optimization, and trajectory 
modelling, but the rigorous treatment of conditional admissibility, transition legality, and dynamic 
action-space boundaries remains comparatively underdeveloped. 
Second, what will the future hierarchy of financial objects look like? The relative centrality of 
forecasts, states, trajectories, and admissibility conditions may not be fixed, but may shift according to 
prevailing regimes of persistence and instability. 
Third, why have continuity-oriented representational frameworks maintained such strong institutional 
and intellectual dominance compared to admissibility-oriented governance? This asymmetry may 
reflect deeper properties of scientific modelling itself — continuous structures support cumulative 
progress and coherent narratives more readily than discontinuity-sensitive ones. 
Finally, it remains to be seen whether participation-governed architectures represent a domainspecific solution for financial markets or reflect broader principles applicable to other adaptive 
systems operating under unstable continuation conditions. 
The evolution of quantitative finance may not have reached its final ontological stage. The long 
progression from price estimation through stochastic dynamics, regime awareness, and path geometry 
may ultimately lead toward participation governance as a stable primary object. Whether this 
transition will produce a more robust decision architecture — or merely another temporary layer — 
remains one of the central open questions for the field.

7. Conclusion 
7.1 The Historical Drift of Financial Core Objects 
The historical development of quantitative finance can be interpreted as a continuing shift in the 
identity of its primary financial objects.  
Across different periods, the field repeatedly reorganized itself around changing sources of structural 
instability. Early quantitative systems focused primarily on price and return estimation. Later 
developments elevated volatility, covariance structures, and factor exposure into dominant 
explanatory roles. Subsequent frameworks introduced latent states, persistence dynamics, trajectory 
dependence, and geometric representations of market evolution. More recent developments 
confronted instability surrounding participation continuity itself.  
These historical transitions did not occur because previous financial objects became entirely invalid. 
Price did not disappear after volatility emerged. Forecasting did not disappear after state estimation 
developed. Trajectory structures did not disappear after geometric and path-dependent systems 
appeared. The historical movement instead reflected a gradual redistribution of structural pressure 
across changing layers of financial ontology.  
From this perspective, quantitative finance may be understood not merely as a sequence of more 
sophisticated forecasting systems, but as a continuing historical process through which the field 
repeatedly redefined what counted as a structurally central financial object. 
7.2 Participation Governance as a Structural Transition 
One of the central transitions examined throughout this paper concerns the changing relationship 
between prediction and participation. For much of modern financial history, forecasting structures 
remained sufficiently aligned with participation continuity to support large-scale financial systems 
across institutional markets. Under relatively stable continuation environments, attractive forecasts 
could extend naturally into sustained participation. As continuation environments became 
progressively less stable, but the relationship between prediction and participation began exhibiting 
increasing structural separation.  
Forecast correctness no longer necessarily implied admissibility continuity. Directional opportunity 
could remain positive while participation durability weakened beneath the execution structure itself. 
Exposure sustainability depended on transition survivability, persistence stability, liquidity continuity, 
and admissibility-sensitive conditions extending beyond trajectory estimation alone. Under such 
conditions, participation behaviour gradually became more governance-sensitive.  
Exposure adjustment no longer behaved merely as a secondary modification attached to predictive 
systems. It behaved as a structural condition governing whether participation itself remained viable 
across unstable continuation environments.  
This transition may ultimately represent more than an expansion of traditional risk management. It 
may reflect a deeper reorganization in how financial systems connect forecasting, participation, and 
sequential decision continuity under unstable structural conditions. 
7.3 Beyond Forecasting Architectures 
The developments discussed throughout this paper do not imply that forecasting architectures have 
lost relevance within quantitative finance. Predictive systems remain indispensable components of 

22 
 
financial decision-making and will likely continue advancing in mathematical sophistication, 
computational scale, and representational richness.  
The structural issue examined in this paper concerns a different question: whether forecasting 
structures alone remain sufficient to absorb the full burden of financial decision closure under 
unstable continuation environments.  
Throughout much of modern quantitative history, advances in representation quality frequently 
appeared capable of producing corresponding improvements in realized decision quality. Under 
sufficiently stable continuation conditions, improvements in prediction, state estimation, and 
trajectory modelling often remained closely aligned with participation continuity itself.  
That alignment is becoming unstable. Financial systems continue advancing in representational 
sophistication while simultaneously confronting recurring instability surrounding participation 
durability, admissibility continuity, transition survivability, and continuation stability itself.  
Recent developments in state-conditioned participation systems, admissibility-aware exposure 
structures, and sequential governance architectures suggest that participation-oriented financial 
frameworks may already be entering an early stage of formal systematization. The structural boundary 
confronting forecast-centred finance therefore may not concern the exhaustion of predictive 
intelligence itself. It may concern the growing visibility of decision conditions that prediction alone 
no longer fully absorbs. 
7.4 Final Remarks on Participation-Centric Finance 
For much of modern financial history, forecasting and participation remained sufficiently aligned for 
predictive systems to preserve operational stability across financial markets. That alignment is 
breaking down.  
The historical progression from price estimation to state representation, trajectory geometry, and 
admissibility-sensitive participation structures no longer appears reducible to forecasting refinement 
alone. Quantitative finance appears to be approaching a deeper reorganization in the identity of its 
primary decision objects. Participation governance no longer behaves like a secondary modification 
attached to predictive systems.  
It is beginning to behave like a structural condition of financial participation itself.
