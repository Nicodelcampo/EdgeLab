ESG motivations and market microstructure
Yuanyuan Chen1,
aDepartment of Finance and Insurance, School of Business, Nanjing University, China
Abstract
This paper investigates the impact of diverse motivations among ESG traders on the market microstructure
within the framework of Kyle (1985). Inspired by Starks (2023), I set up the model where two types of ESG
investors, value and values, prefer ESG investing for diﬀerent reasons: the potential for high expected return
and the non-peculiarity beneﬁts, respectively. My results show that it is the value ESG investor, rather than
the values ESG investor, who drives the ESG excess return. However, if there is no values ESG investor, the
value ESG investor may stop ESG investing.
Keywords:
ESG investing, Motivation, Expectation
1. Introduction
With sustainable ﬁnance drawing increasing attention from both industry and academia, there have been
a hot debate on how ESG-related investing aﬀects investors, corporations, and asset markets. As stated by
Starks (2023), the motivations driving investors to prefer ESG assets could make a big diﬀerence on the
eﬀects. The existing literature typically assumes that all investors are either motivated by personal values,
known as values ESG investors, or by the perception of ﬁnancial excess returns, referred to as value ESG
investors. However, according to a recent survey by Giglio et al. (2025), ESG investments are made by
investors with diverse motivations, and a single trader may embody both values and value ESG investor
characteristics.
This paper aims to ﬁll the gap by considering both the ESG value and values investors in one stock market,
and allowing traders to account for both ﬁnancial and non-ﬁnancial motivations. In particular, I extend the
classical model by Kyle (1985) to include traditional investors, value ESG investors, and values ESG investors,
to investigate how ﬁnancial or non-ﬁnancial beliefs are reﬂected in the market prices. Speciﬁcally, traditional
and value ESG investors are motivated by the expected ﬁnancial returns based on their own beliefs, while
values ESG investors are driven not only by ﬁnancial returns, but also by non-pecuniary proﬁts from holding
ESG assets.
Unlike classical stock price factors, ESG considerations are relatively new to investors so that investors
have a much shorter history to learn from. Consequently, investors’ beliefs about ESG excess returns can
vary signiﬁcantly, as shown in Giglio et al. (2025). Traditional investors, who have non-positive ESG excess

ESG excess returns are positive, and thus prefer ESG trading. In fact, even individual investors may update
their beliefs during ESG trading: a value ESG investor could possibly revert to being a traditional investor
if they ﬁnd ESG investing reduces their ﬁnancial returns.
Based on the equilibrium model, I ﬁnd that the values ESG investor, whose ESG preference is well-known
by others, does not aﬀect the execution price. In fact, while the equilibrium price of ESG assets may initially
be elevated due to the non-pecuniary proﬁt that the values ESG investor derives from holding them, this
diﬀerence is ultimately neutralized by the trading activities by the values ESG investor. On the contrary,
the belief of the value ESG investor on the ESG excess return does aﬀect the execution price, as investors
learn from the trade information on the ESG excess return.
Furthermore, I analyze the survival of value ESG investors, with the consideration that if the value ESG
investor ﬁnds his/her expected proﬁt decreases in ESG rating, he/she would stop being a value ESG investor.
I ﬁnd that the existence of values ESG investor is crucial for the survival of value ESG investors.
The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, from a modelling perspective, this paper establishes
the ﬁrst equilibrium model that explicitly accounts for ESG traders with diverse motivations. This model
enables us to examine the distinct impacts of varied ESG trading motivations and the interactions among
ESG traders with diﬀerent motivations. Second, in terms of economic insights, my model indicates that the
existence of values ESG investors, although unaﬀected on the execution price, could beneﬁt the survival of
value ESG investors. And the value ESG investors have a signiﬁcant impact on stock prices.
This paper thus contributes to the growing literature on the eﬀects of ESG trading on the ﬁnancial market
with heterogeneous investors. Goldstein et al. (2024) develop a rational expectations equilibrium model to
investigate how ESG investing reshapes information aggregation by prices. In their model, traditional and
green investors vary in their preferences for the ﬁnancial and ESG performances of a ﬁrm. Avramov et al.
(2024) develops and tests an equilibrium model for analyzing active fund management, considering traders
with varying non-pecuniary preferences for holding green assets. Diﬀerent from them, this paper considers
both value and values ESG investors, to investigate their distinct impacts on investors and the asset prices.
To simplify the problem, I focus on an economy with a single period and one risky asset.
My work is also closely related to recent empirical studies on the varied motivations behind ESG trading.
Riedl and Smeets (2017) ﬁnd that investors are willing to forgo ﬁnancial performance to perceive social
preferences and social signaling. Hartzmark and Sussman (2019) analyze U.S. mutual fund data to ﬁnd
that investors prefer high-sustainability, while high-sustainability funds do not outperform low-sustainability
funds. Barber et al. (2021) ﬁnd that in willingness-to-pay models, investors accept 2.5-3.7 percentage points
lower internal rates of return for impact funds. Giglio et al. (2025) analyze survey data on ESG beliefs
and preferences among a large panel of retail investors, ﬁnding that investors generally expected ESG investments to underperform the market; and even among the values ESG investors, ﬁnancial motives remain
an important driver of portfolio allocation. My work complements these works by theoretically modeling

3. Changes of the beliefs and Survival of value ESG Investors
Diﬀerent from the well-established ﬁnancial factors, ESG considerations are relatively new to investors.
In addition, the ESG preference starts from the motivation of being good, which is also quite diﬀerent from
the proﬁt-oriented motivations associated with traditional ﬁnancial factors. As a result, there is always a
debate on whether investors can do good for being good, or in other words, whether investors can beneﬁt
from ESG investing. If investors do beneﬁt from ESG investing, what’s the role the values ESG investor play?
Or in other words, is the presence of values ESG investors contributing to the proﬁts of ESG investment for
other investors?

In summary, based on the results in Proposition 3.1-3.3, I formalize the following two implications.
Empirical Implication 1. The value ESG investor is more likely to survive in markets where the ESG
excess return is underestimated.
As pointed out by Cornell and Damodaran (2020), investing in ESG assets can increase the investor
returns, only before the market recognizes the ESG excess returns in the assets.
Empirical Implication 2. The values ESG investors’ underestimation of the ESG excess return facilitates the survival of value ESG investors.
Although I demonstrate in Theorem 2.1 that the values ESG investor’s willingness to pay (WTP) does
not aﬀect the execution price of ESG assets, I ﬁnd that the existence of WTP is crucial for the survival of
value ESG investors.
3.2. Interconversion between traditional and ESG value investors
The discussion in last subsection reveals that traditional and value ESG investors may mutually transform. This is because both types of investors are motivated by ﬁnancial proﬁts and diﬀer in their beliefs on
ESG excess return. Once they receive negative feedback from their ESG investing, they could update their

4. Conclusion
In this paper, I introduce ESG traders with varied motivations into the classical Kyle’s model, to investigate the eﬀects of varied motivations on equilibrium price and the trader proﬁts. In the model, I assume that
investors could prefer ESG assets for both ﬁnancial and non-ﬁnancial reasons. The values ESG investors
perceive both ﬁnancial and non-pecuniary beneﬁts, while other investors focus solely on the potential excess
return that ESG assets may bring.
My results show that beliefs on ESG excess return aﬀect ESG trading strategies, for all types of traders.
Traditional investors, who believe in a negative ESG excess return, would sell more (or buy less) assets
with higher ESG ratings, eﬀectively becoming “traditional investors”. Conversely, value ESG investors, who
holds a positive belief on ESG excess return, would buy more ESG assets. As for values ESG investors,
both a high expectation of ESG excess return and a high non-pecuniary beneﬁt can contribute to their ESG
holdings.
The presence of values ESG investors does not aﬀect the trade price of ESG assets, since they would
pay for their own moral pursuits. However, the market average belief on ESG excess return can signiﬁcantly
move the market price. In other words, the price would be pushed up if there are more value ESG investors.
Meanwhile, the presence of values ESG investors is crucial to the survival of value ESG investors in the
market.
