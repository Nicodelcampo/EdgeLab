Quantitative Skill and Introductory Finance: 
Does Ability Dominate Attitude? 
 
 
Matthew M. Ross 1 
Western Michigan University 
 
A. Michelle Wright 
Wright Research LLC 
 
 
This study examines quantitative skill among 164 students in an 
introductory finance course. Results indicate that undergraduate 
Bachelor of Business Administration (BBA) students exhibit alarming 
deficiencies in quantitative skills for introductory finance with only 
about one in four students demonstrating even the basic required skills. 
We augment extant BBA quantitative skill literature, which focuses 
primarily on ability, by introducing standardized metrics of student 
attitudes toward mathematics. We utilize ACT Math scores as direct 
measure of ability and GPA as an indirect proxy for ability. The 
Attitudes Toward Mathematics Inventory (ATMI) provides a direct 
measure of math attitude and the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count 
(LIWC) dictionary for “work” words provides an indirect measure of 
math attitude. Both proxies for ability and both proxies for attitudes are 
significant determinants of quantitative preparedness. Furthermore, 
dominance analysis, a statistical technique to rank order the importance 
of predictors, indicates that student attitudes about mathematics 
accounts for more variance in quantitative performance than GPA, the 
commonly employed proxy for ability. These attitude measures are 
viable tools for assessing quantitative preparedness in math intensive 
business courses, such as introductory finance.

INTRODUCTION 
 
US students underperform the average student in Organisation for Economic Co-operation 
and Development member nations on math tests (OECD, 2016). These poor math skills present 
substantial challenges in quantitative disciplines of Bachelor of Business Administration (BBA) 
programs, such as finance. Beyond generally uninspiring quantitative skills, specific factors 
contributing to inadequate preparation for finance also include poor study habits (Nofsinger and 
Petry, 1999), students working while in school (Borde, Byrd, and Modani, 1998), and insufficient 
testing procedures to ensure students have mastered prerequisite material (Fields, 2013). Yet, to 
the best of the authors’ knowledge, no study demonstrates the relative importance of two attributes 
on quantitative skill required for finance: math ability and attitudes about math. To determine 
which matters more for finance preparedness—ability or attitudes—we employ dominance 
analysis, a statistical technique to execute a “horserace” between proxies for these areas. 
A number of studies link quantitative skill to performance in finance courses (Blaylock and 
Lacewell, 2008; Borde, Byrd, and Modani, 1998; Bredthauer and Fendler, 2016; Chan, Shum, and 
Chhachhi, 2005; Fields, 2013; Islam and Islam, 2013; Hall and Wynne, 2004; Terry, 2002). 
However, these studies primarily focus on indirect measures such as course grades rather than 
specific math skills directly applied to finance problems. Our approach captures criterionreferenced quantitative skills relevant to a finance application. In doing so, we avoid the 
confounding effects of graded attendance, extra credit, and homework which may directly impact 
final course grades while only indirectly reflecting finance skill.  
Our results highlight several key take-away points. First, many students are substantially 
underprepared for the quantitative rigor of introductory finance. Second, there is a strong 
relationship between students’ attitudes about math and their demonstrated quantitative skills 
relevant to finance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to employ two standardized math 
attitudes measures in a finance pedagogy context: the short version of the Attitudes Toward 
Mathematics Inventory (ATMI; Lim and Chapman, 2013) and Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count 
text analysis (LIWC; Pennebaker, Boyd, Jordan, and Blackburn, 2015). Both means of attitude 
assessment are relatively straightforward and offer opportunities for programmatic 
implementation. These tools have the potential to identify students that are underprepared for the 
quantitative areas of business. Third, we find that the ACT Math component score is a powerful 
predictor of quantitative skills while cumulative college GPA is only marginal. This finding 
contrasts with extant business pedagogy literature indicating the primacy of GPA. 
 
BACKGROUND 
 
The Purpose of Business Prerequisites 
 
Finance is an academic subject area deemed essential to the practice of business under the 
Association to Advance Collegiate Schools of Business standards (AACSB, 2017). Since students 
must pass a business finance course to earn a BBA degree, prerequisite quantitative skills sufficient 
to conduct finance calculations are essential. Thus, quantitative prerequisites help achieve 
standardization to better ensure adequate skills for more advanced coursework. Theoretically, the 
series of quantitative prerequisite courses for introductory finance establishes a baseline skill level 
for enrolling students. Despite passing the prerequisite courses, many students are not ready for 
the rigor of quantitative business courses (e.g., Borde et al., 1998; Nofsinger et al., 1999; Terry,

significant time outside the classroom to catch-up, or (2) dedicate significant class time throughout

Some business schools are now evaluating if a pre-testing process prior to quantitative

pretest focused on basic mathematical ability, is predictive of success in introductory finance 
(Grover, Heck, and Heck, 2010), intermediate corporate finance (Fields, 2013), and online 
introductory finance courses (Bredthauer and Fendler, 2016). Given these results, we utilize a 
criterion-referenced math skills pretest that directly applies to finance, thereby demonstrating if 
students are prepared for the quantitative rigor of business finance. In the following sections, we 
discuss possible reasons why students arrive underprepared for business finance. 
 
What about Ability? 
 
Quantitative ability is a critical component of finance. Research is consistent—students 
who demonstrate poor quantitative ability struggle with finance courses. Trine and Schellenger 
(1999) report a significant positive relationship between finance course grades and ACT Math 
scores, while Li, Uvah, and Amin (2012) demonstrate this finding extends to quantitative course 
grades. Similarly, Ballard and Johnson (2004) find a significant positive relationship between ACT 
Math scores and an introductory quiz assessing the basic algebra skills needed for a 
microeconomics course. Given the quantitative nature of finance, it is not surprising that a direct 
measure of math ability is predictive of success in finance courses. However, direct measures can 
be more difficult to obtain, which leads to widespread use of indirect measures for determining 
math ability. 
We assert that GPA, while a useful measure, serves as an indirect proxy of quantitative 
ability. Cumulative college GPA often serves as a measure to predict performance in introductory 
finance courses (Blaylock and Lacewell, 2008; Borde, Byrd, and Modani, 1998; Bredthauer and 
Fendler, 2016; Didia and Hasnat, 1998; Nofsinger and Petry, 1999; Sen, Joyce, Farrell, and 
Toutant, 1997; Simpson and Sumrall, 1979). Indeed, Biktimirov and Armstrong (2015) identify 
GPA as the most significant predictor of introductory finance performance. GPA includes courses 
that require quantitative skill, thus reflecting ability. Yet, however predictive GPA is of finance 
course grades, GPA captures more than mathematical ability. For example, GPA at least partially 
reflects effective time management and determination. The GPA may also represent certain 
undesirable aspects of student behavior (e.g., “padding” one’s GPA with credit from courses 
offering a generous grade distribution, keeping one’s GPA high by delaying the “difficult” courses 
until the last year of college, etc.). Despite these issues, we identify no study acknowledging the 
indirect nature of GPA when predicting quantitative skills necessary for finance course 
performance. 
The aforementioned studies suggest a robust relationship between prior quantitative ability 
and performance in finance courses. As such, we expect that both ACT Math and GPA demonstrate 
a positive relationship with the quantitative skill assessment scores. We contribute to existing 
literature twofold. First, we test a direct measure of quantitative ability, ACT Math, for utility in

What about Attitudes? 
 
Math achievement and math attitudes are generally identified as reciprocal: achievement 
in mathematics both influences and is influenced by attitudes about mathematics (Williams and 
Williams, 2010).2 Students who focus on “growing” and “trying harder” have significantly higher 
mathematics scores than students who focus on “getting the right answer” even when these two 
groups of students started with the same baseline mathematical ability (Blackwell, Trzesniewski, 
and Dweck, 2007). Additionally, students who are highly math anxious but write about this anxiety 
show marked improvements in their mathematical performance (Park, Ramirez, and Beilock, 
2014). Research in introductory economics courses indicates that students with positive attitudes 
toward mathematics significantly outperform their peers (Arnold and Straten, 2012; Ely and Hittle, 
1990). Similarly, research examining negative math attitudes indicates math anxiety—a feeling of 
fear or tension about math—actually impairs mathematical performance (Hembree, 1990; Ma, 
1999; McLeod, 1994). The seminal study by Ashcraft and Kirk (2001) indicates that anxiety about 
math can actually override basic cognitive processes such that a highly math anxious individual 
typically underperforms their actual math potential.3 Math anxiety can be found at all math ability 
levels (Foley et al., 2017) and the relationship between “feeling good” about math and “being 
good” at math is complicated (Kruger and Dunning, 1999). The general population of BBA 
students exhibit an inverse relationship between math anxiety and quantitative performance 
(Yenilmez, Girginer, and Uzun, 2007; Zanakis and Valenzi, 1997). To our knowledge, no research 
specifically examines the relationship between student attitudes toward math and the quantitative 
skills required for an introductory finance course. Given the strong and consistent impact that math 
anxiety can have on math performance and the highly quantitative nature of finance, we address 
this gap in the literature. We assess student attitudes toward math following the same format as 
our math ability variables: one direct and one indirect measure. 
The Attitudes Toward Mathematics Inventory (ATMI) (Tapia and Marsh, 2004) provides 
a standardized direct measure. Developed for high-school students, the ATMI is also valid for 
college students (Tapia and Marsh, 2005). We use a short version of the ATMI, which yields 
results similar to the original scale (Lim and Chapman, 2013) and henceforth, throughout the 
paper, refer to this 15-question version as ATMI. Nevertheless, standardized Likert-type scales are 
subject to bias including social desirability or impression management (Paulhus and Reid, 1991), 
straight-line responding, i.e. all “disagree” or “agree” to each item (Galesic and Bosnjak, 2009), 
or misrepresenting or inaccurately reporting responses in order to complete the survey (Lehnen 
and Reiss, 1978). While evidence suggests that ATMI is a direct and efficient measure of attitudes, 
we select a non-scale approach to construct the indirect attitude measure. 
A text-analysis indirect measure mitigates some of the potential issues associated with a 
standardized scale. Text analysis research demonstrates that word choices in narratives can offer 
a mechanism to assess one’s attitudes and provide insight regarding motives (Schultheiss, 2013). 
One tool for text analysis, Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC), allows for quantitative 
analysis of text rather than subjective coding and has been found to be both a reliable and valid 
method of assessing words to gain insight into attitudes (Tausczik and Pennebaker, 2010). LIWC 
analyses text by identifying words that fall into specific categorical dictionaries. We focus on the

business students—specifically those focusing on finance—using more “work” words actually 
demonstrate higher academic achievement. As a non-scale text-based measure, LIWC reflects 
elements of attitude that ATMI or similar direct measures may not capture.  
LIWC exhibits utility in predicting both academic and financial performance. Robinson, 
Navea, and Ickes (2013) find that word choices in beginning-of-semester introductory narratives 
predict final course grades. Similarly, Pennebaker, Chung, Frazee, Lavergne, and Beaver (2014) 
find that language use predicts college GPA four years later in analysis of 50,000 college entrance 
essays even after controlling for the traditional measures of academic performance (e.g., SAT 
scores, ranking within high school class). Specific to the finance discipline, LIWC also helps detect 
deception in financial reports (Larcker and Zakolyukina, 2012) and similar inquiry (see Fisher, 
Garnsey, and Hughes (2016) for a summary LIWC use in accounting, auditing, and finance). 
Literature suggests that positive attitudes towards math are associated with better math 
performance, even if methods to capture attitudes remain imperfect.  
We hypothesize that the validated and standardized measures, ATMI and LIWC “work” 
words, exhibit a positive relationship with quantitative skills assessment scores. We augment 
business pedagogy literature by testing the utility of a direct attitude proxy, ATMI, and an indirect 
attitude proxy, LIWC “work” words. Then, we compare the relative importance of ATMI and 
LIWC “work” in determining quantitative skills necessary for introductory finance. 
 
Relative Importance 
 
 
If both ability and attitude are important, which matters more for quantitative skills 
preparedness? Within an introductory economics course, Arnold and Straten (2012) find that 
highly motivated students who struggle with math do score at similar levels compared to their 
more skilled but unmotivated counterparts. Research involving business students examines both 
ability and attitudes (Bredthauer and Fendler, 2016; Yenilmez, Girginer, and Uzum, 2007; Zanakis 
and Valenzi, 1997) but does not assess the relative importance of ability and attitude on student 
performance. The ability versus attitude question is germane to instructors, university admissions, 
and administrators as they consider cut-off scores, predictive validity, and the time-consuming 
process of data collection and record keeping. The answer is relevant to aspects of the classroom 
and curriculum in a business school that are more difficult to quantify, such as how to most 
effectively prepare students for the workforce. 
Given the paucity of research concerning the relative importance of math ability versus 
attitudes relevant to finance, orthodox economic theory drives our third hypothesis. Specifically, 
that a representative agent (i.e., finance students) rationally motivated by a reward (i.e., extra credit 
on the quantitative skills assessment) is sufficient to model quantitative skills performance, while 
behavioral aspects (i.e., student attitudes) are appropriately relegated to an error term. 
Consequently, we hypothesize that ability dominates attitude, regardless of the measurement 
method—direct or indirect. We expect the two ability proxies to be stronger predictors than the 
attitude or control variables. To test this notion, we jointly regress both ability and attitudes proxies 
to determine if the ability proxies show significant relationships with the quantitative skills

Control Variables 
 
Prior research examining relationships between demographic variables, quantitative 
ability, and prerequisite preparedness in finance courses is mixed. With regard to gender, Sen et 
al. (1997) find that females outperform males in both the prerequisite courses and final course 
grades, while Terry (2002) finds the opposite, and Didia and Hasnat (1998) detect no difference 
between genders. Borde, Byrd, and Modani (1998) find no impact of age on finance performance 
whereas Baloglu and Kocak (2006) find that older students report greater math anxiety than their 
younger classmates. Minority status may also play a role in prerequisite readiness for finance as 
minority students typically face more limited mathematics options in high school (e.g., calculus) 
(Oakes, 1990). Finally, there is substantial variation in how many credit hours are earned prior to 
course enrollment with this participant sample composed of sophomore, junior, and senior status 
students. Given the aforementioned, we control for the following demographic variables: Female, 
Minority, Age, and Credits Earned. 
 
METHODS 
 
Participants and Procedures 
 
The sample comprises 164 undergraduate students enrolled in an introductory finance 
course at a large, Midwestern university. All students enrolled in this particular course during the 
2016-2017 academic year were given the opportunity to participate. On the first day of the course, 
students read and signed an informed consent document to become participants in exchange for 
the opportunity to earn a small amount of extra credit in the course. Participants then completed a 
15-item attitudes scale and answered five open-ended questions about their mathematics 
experience and perception. Participants subsequently completed a 10-item quantitative skills 
assessment where each correct response earned one additional extra credit point. In order to 
mitigate bias, students completed the attitudes scale and open-ended questions before completing 
the quantitative assessment. Potential participants included 180 students, but 6 were not present 
for data collection and 10 did not complete one or more of the items required for inclusion in the 
study.  
A careful reader may wonder how these participants compare to the average BBA student 
at this university. Table 1 presents summary statistics and demographic information of the sample 
to illustrate this point. Study participants show significantly stronger performance (at the 1% level 
or better) on both GPA and ACT Math scores.4 The greater quantitative ability among the sample 
implies that any quantitative deficiency detected here presents an even greater issue among the 
whole BBA population.

Measures 
 
We extract demographic information (i.e., Age, Female, Minority) and previous academic 
information (i.e., ACT Math, GPA, Credits Earned) from university records. Definitions for each 
variable are available in the Appendix. Since university admissions accept multiple types of 
qualifications, the ACT Math score is unavailable for 53 students (32.3% of the sample). 
Additionally, GPA is unavailable for nine transfer students (5.5% of the sample) who were new to 
the university in the term of data collection. With the exception of transfer credits, all student 
GPAs in this sample include at least one course from each; accounting, math, and statistics. 
Additionally, many other courses utilize quantitative skills in some capacity suggesting that GPA 
serves a legitimate proxy for quantitative ability. 
Attitudes toward math are assessed with the short form of the Attitudes Toward 
Mathematics Inventory (ATMI; Lim and Chapman, 2013). The short form of the ATMI includes 
15-items with each response ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items six 
through ten are reverse coded prior to creation of the scale score. Following criteria guidance from 
Nunnally (1978), we show that the scale demonstrates good reliability within our sample 
(Cronbach’s alpha = .83). Throughout the remainder of this paper we refer to this variable as our 
direct measure of mathematics attitudes. Table 2 provides ATMI items and summary statistics.

Participants typed responses to five open-ended questions assessing their attitudes about 
mathematics.5 We analyze this data using the text analysis program Linguistic Inquiry and Word 
Count (LIWC 2015; Pennebaker, Boyd, Jordan, and Blackburn, 2015). LIWC scans text for words 
within specific dictionaries or categories and yields results as a percentage (e.g., 5.90% of the 
words used by a student belong to the LIWC “work” dictionary). We analyze text files using the 
LIWC dictionary for Work words, which includes 444 total words (e.g., boss, career, calculus, 
financ*, hire, math*, organize, paycheck, responsibility, studied, test).6  
The sum of correct responses to a 10-item mathematics assessment (see Table 3), Total

(i.e., the quiz was not multiple choice). Scores could range from 0 (no correct responses) to 10 (all 
items answered correctly) with no partial credit.

Ability and Attitude 
 
Model 2 in Table 5 indicates that participants with higher scores for the ability proxies 
perform significantly better on the quantitative assessment. These direct and indirect ability 
variables remain significant even in the presence of the aforementioned control variables. Students 
demonstrating mastery of quantitative skills prior to the first day of finance through standardized 
measures, ACT Math and GPA, perform better on the introductory finance quantitative skills 
assessment. Model 3 in Table 5 includes attitude variables associated with quantitative skill 
required for introductory finance. Participants reporting more positive attitudes about math, as 
measured by the ATMI, score higher on the quantitative skill assessment. Additionally, 
participants using more Work words in their open-ended responses demonstrate significantly 
higher quantitative ability. Thus, we find solid evidence that prior quantitative ability and attitudes 
about mathematics are important determinants of quantitative skill necessary for introductory 
finance. 
 
Relative Importance 
 
Given the limited sample size and some missing data, we examine robustness with Models 
4 and 5 ahead of the final model. First, as the university does not have ACT Math scores for all 
students, we include only the ability proxy of GPA, along with the attitudes proxies and control 
variables. The coefficients in Model 4 are similar to those in Models 2 and 3. Specifically, GPA, 
ATMI, and Work all remain significant determinants of quantitative ability. Model 4 demonstrates 
that missing observations of the ability variable, ACT Math, do not substantially alter the overall 
joint results presented in Model 5.9 Second, because dominance analysis does not allow for 
dichotomous predictors, we include all control variables, ability proxies, and attitude proxies in 
Model 5 as a final robustness check prior to the final model. Minority and Female, our only 
dichotomous predictors, are non-significant so we drop these variables from the final model. 
Model 6 includes all four proxies, both direct and indirect, for the ability variables as well 
as the attitudes variables. Model 6 produces the best model fit (F = 6.64, p < .001) and the largest 
proportion of variance accounted for in the DV (Adjusted R2 =. 24). This is our final model and 
the basis of all subsequent results we present. 
We find that all four proxies are significant in determining the quantitative skills necessary 
for introductory finance. The direct measure of ability, ACT Math, is a significant determinant of 
quantitative skills assessment scores at the 1% level. An 8.33-point higher score on the ACT Math 
translates to one additional correct response on the assessment.10 The indirect measure of ability, 
GPA, is also a significant determinant (5% level) with a 1.63 GPA increase corresponding to one 
additional Total Correct response. Both attitudes proxies are also significant predictors, even when 
included with the ability proxies. Specifically, a 1.25 increase on the direct measure of attitudes, 
ATMI, results in a one-point increase in quantitative skills score (1% level). Finally, the indirect

DISCUSSION 
 
Implications 
 
Our results are consistent with the predominant research findings—students are alarmingly 
underprepared on the first day of class. Even among finance-oriented students, only about one in 
four possess the basic quantitative skills deemed critical by finance faculty, despite these students 
demonstrating greater quantitative skill than the average college of business student.13 This finding 
is especially troubling yet is consistent with the subpar math skills of US students (OECD, 2004; 
2016). Poor incoming skills matter a great deal since mathematical pretest scores are a robust 
predictor of finance course performance (Bredthauer et al., 2016; Fields, 2013; Grover et al., 2010). 
These results suggest that the mean student in American business colleges lacks the prerequisite 
quantitative skills needed to master introductory finance. 
Introductory finance is often rated the most difficult course in the business curriculum and 
that, if given the choice, most students would not take a finance course (Krishnan, Bathala, 
Bhattacharya, and Ritchey, 1999). What are faculty or administrators to do when students with 
poor math skills occupy a large fraction of seats in quantitatively rigorous business courses? Other 
researchers suggest completion of a finance pre-course (Mahrt-Smith, 2007) and administering a 
specific pretest prior to entry into the course (Fields, 2013). Both of these approaches provide the 
student with information (e.g., “How prepared am I for this course?”), the faculty with information 
(e.g., “How much remedial math review is needed?”), and the business school with information 
(e.g., “Are administrative decisions involving math skills needed?”). Alternatively, the institution 
could either augment quantitative prerequisite courses or elevate the threshold for passing to ensure 
that students arriving in finance courses possess adequate quantitative skill. Rather than focusing 
on skill level directly, attitudes about mathematics may also be a suitable target as they are 
malleable and improving math attitudes translates to improving math performance (Blackwell et 
al., 2007; Park et al., 2014). Our results show that while demonstrated ability is obviously 
important, attitudes about mathematics are also strong and robust predictors of quantitative skill

We explore which attributes are most strongly associated with students that are 
underprepared for the quantitative demands of finance. In a relative importance “horserace”, 
ability measure ACT Math ranks first, while attitude measures, Work and ATMI, rank second and 
third, respectively. In contrast with the predominant body of research which suggests that GPA is 
among the strongest predictors of finance course success, our results show that GPA ranks fifth, 
even behind a control variable, Credits Earned, in determining criterion-referenced quantitative 
skill. Dominance analysis indicates that GPA has poor predictive power relative to ACT Math and 
the attitude measures. These results suggest that a direct measure of quantitative ability, ACT Math, 
is more important to quantitative preparedness for introductory finance than is the indirect 
quantitative ability measure GPA. While GPA is nearly ubiquitous in pedagogy research, our 
results suggest caution in emphasizing this variable too heavily. 
We introduce attitudes measures that are novel to the finance pedagogy arena and show 
promise as utilitarian tools. The ATMI is standardized, reliable, easy to administer, and takes just 
minutes for students to complete. We believe the indirect attitudes proxy, Work, reflect students’ 
understanding of why math is important. That this LIWC dictionary provides the second strongest 
predictor—accounting for approximately twice the variance in quantitative skills performance 
when compared to GPA—is eye-opening. Student narratives suggest that Work not only captures 
an awareness of mathematical and academic work but also “business” work.14 Both attitude 
measures show potential for the BBA arena. 
Focus on attitude rather than ability metrics is already reflected in the literature. Both 
Krishnan and colleagues (1999) and Sen and colleagues (1997) suggest that multiple course 
offerings may improve student perceptions and performance in introductory finance—a more 
rigorous course for finance/accounting students and a slower paced, less rigorous course for other 
majors. While we acknowledge the multi-course option may be attractive, our results point to 
another potential solution. Specifically, instead of reducing quantitative rigor in the finance course, 
greater focus on “attitude” may yield substantial gains. Our results appear to align with the 
Balachandran and Skully (2004) finding that accounting and finance majors believe introductory 
finance is interesting and useful to their future career, where as other types of business majors find 
the course is too difficult. We suggest emphasizing how students benefit from improving 
mathematics and finance skill (i.e., personal finance demands it; business careers require math!). 
Better understanding about employer expectations involving mathematics may increase student 
focus and, as such, help students better prepare for the first day of finance. 
Specific to our findings, university administrators may also consider early intervention 
regarding math preparedness by examining ACT Math scores and mathematics attitudes. This 
approach could allow for the earliest possible college intervention—the first semester—as ACT 
Math scores and student narratives are typically part of an application. Administrators could jointly 
assess students’ math ability and attitudes upon admittance to the university or the business 
college. We believe that the traditional “gold standard” approach of using cumulative college GPA 
to predict finance course performance has utility; but it is inferior to other measures for two 
reasons. First, it falls last in the dominance analysis of ability and attitude measures. Second, 
cumulative college GPA—by its very nature—requires a college track record, which prohibits the 
potential for immediate intervention. The other three measures provide for earlier student 
identification which allows more options to guide students.

Limitations and Future Research 
 
As with most research involving human subjects, our study would have benefited from a 
larger sample size. Specifically, university record limitations involving ACT Math scores and GPA 
reduces our sample size in the joint model. Future research could also increase generalizability by 
recruiting students from more than one university and across more than one academic year. 
Additionally, our direct measure of attitudes relies on a self-reported math attitudes assessment, 
the ATMI. Although this self-reporting attitudes scale appears to be the best available, additional 
measures of attitudes, such as peer or instructor rated attitudes, may be more informative. 
Relatedly, our analysis of the open-ended responses focuses on quantifiable proxies (i.e., word 
frequencies); however, a more qualitative analysis method (e.g., thematic coding) may yield more 
nuanced results. 
 
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 
 
 
Consistent with previous research, we find that undergraduate business students are 
underprepared for the quantitative rigors of introductory finance. We examine correlates of 
quantitative skill, prior math performance (i.e. ability) and feelings about math (i.e. attitude), to 
ascertain which is most salient. Students reporting positive attitudes on the ATMI and those selfselecting a higher proportion of LIWC “work” words in writing about math demonstrate 
significantly better quantitative skills required for finance. The impact of the ability measures, the 
ACT Math component score and cumulative college GPA, on quantitative preparedness for finance 
is generally consistent with prior research. Yet this research demonstrates that ACT Math is a 
substantially better indicator than college GPA. The ordinal rank of quantitative skill indicators 
(from best to worst) is ACT Math, “work” words, ATMI, and finally GPA. We believe the 
combination of these measures and this ranking hold potential value as a tool for instructors and 
administrators to better serve BBA students. 
That both attitude and ability matter is no great surprise. However, the degree to which 
attitude impacts quantitative skills is not only surprising, but hints at potential avenues for 
intervention. The historical focus on ability proxies, particularly GPA, to determine students’ 
quantitative preparedness may overlook behavioral dynamics associated with motivation. Our 
findings show that while students typically exhibit criterion-referenced quantitative skill 
deficiency, relatively accessible measures are available to help identify which students are 
underprepared for introductory finance.

Definition 
 
 
 
 
Female 
Dummy variable equal to zero if student identified as male and one otherwise. 
Minority 
Dummy variable equal to zero if student identified as "White", "Asian", or 
"International" and one if "Black or African American", "Hispanic", "Two or More 
Races", "American Indian or Alaska Native", "Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific 
Islander", or "No Response". 
Age 
Student age is years on the data collection date ((data date - birth date)/365.25). Data 
dates are 7SEP16 and 10JAN17. 
Credits Earned 
Number of completed credit hours as recorded by the registrar, including transfer 
credits. 
ACT Math 
Score on the mathematics portion of the ACT achievement test for college 
admissions. 
GPA 
University grade point average for all non-transfer credit hours. Any GPA equal to 
zero is assigned as a missing value. 
ATMI 
The mean scale result of the 15-question ATMI where items five through ten are 
reverse coded. 
Work 
LIWC percentage of "work" related words in response to five open ended questions. 
Total Correct 
The sum of correct answers on the 10-question quantitative assessment. 
Total Critical 
The sum of correct answers on the assessment questions rated critical (1, 2, 4, & 5).
