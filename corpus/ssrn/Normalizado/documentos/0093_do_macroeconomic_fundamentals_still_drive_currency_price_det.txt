Do Macroeconomic Fundamentals Still Drive Currency
Price Determination? Evidence from Market
Microstructure
Abstract
This paper examines the relative importance of macroeconomic fundamentals
and market microstructure factors in determining foreign exchange prices.
Applying Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling to six major
currency pairs, the analysis decomposes pricing influences into inflation, economic conditions, monetary policy, volatility shocks, and trader heterogeneity, distinguished between institutional and non-institutional participants.
Results for the 2010–2020 sample show that traditional fundamentals have
limited explanatory power relative to monetary policy and trader positions,
although the strength of these relationships varies across currencies and periods. Model performance varies systematically with market characteristics,
revealing currency-specific pricing mechanisms. These findings have implications for exchange rate forecasting, central bank policy transmission, and
the role of microstructure in international finance.
Keywords:
Foreign exchange microstructure; Structural equation modeling;
Trader heterogeneity; Price determination
1. Introduction
Foreign exchange microstructure research is fundamentally motivated by
the need to understand exchange rate dynamics. The foreign exchange market, one of the largest and most liquid financial markets, plays a central role
in most free-market economies. The shift from fixed to floating exchange
rates in 1973 provided economists with opportunities to test theories of price
formation, yet unresolved issues remain and the determination of exchange
rates continues to be a challenging area.
Under our assumptions, difficulties in explaining exchange rate behaviour
arise from limited understanding of market microstructure and an overreThis preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6806248

liance on macroeconomic indicators.
The key insight of this paper is to
assess whether tensions exist between traditional theoretical frameworks and
the behaviour observed in modern FX markets.
Our approach integrates
a broad set of predictive variables to explain movements in both spot rates
and volatility skewness, despite the practical challenge of managing extensive
information sets.
This requires a methodology capable of comparing multiple theoretically
distinct channels within a single estimation framework; we employ Partial
Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) for this purpose.
PLS-SEM allows the explanatory power of multiple interconnected factors to
be assessed within a single framework.
The contribution of this paper is not to claim that macroeconomic fundamentals are irrelevant in all settings, but to explain why their shortto medium-run transmission into exchange rates may be attenuated in the
2010–2020 environment. During much of this period, inflation differentials
across major advanced economies were compressed, policy rates were near
the effective lower bound, and monetary transmission increasingly operated
through expectations, forward guidance, and portfolio rebalancing rather
than through broad money aggregates alone. In such an environment, slowmoving macroeconomic indicators may contain limited incremental information for weekly price formation once markets have already incorporated the
prevailing macro backdrop.
PLS-SEM is well suited to exploratory analysis [48], handles large numbers of indicators and cross-asset data [24], accommodates deviations from
normality [27], and remains robust with comparatively small samples [26].
This enables us to examine complex relationships and compare influences
across currencies.
At the same time, FX prices are formed in markets characterised by
heterogeneous information sets, balance-sheet constraints, inventory adjustment, and uneven risk-bearing capacity. Under these conditions, the transmission from fundamentals to prices may be mediated, delayed, or partially
subsumed by intermediary behaviour, trader positions, and option-implied
expectations. The empirical question is therefore not simply whether fundamentals “work”, but whether their influence remains dominant once monetary
conditions, shock-related variables, and trader heterogeneity are modelled
jointly within a unified framework.

four constructs: inflation, economic, monetary, and surprise variables. Sur2

This research contributes to the microstructure literature in three main
ways. First, it provides the first cross-currency comparative analysis of how
institutional and non-institutional positions affect prices, showing that these
effects vary with market depth and participant composition rather than applying uniformly across currency pairs.
Second, it quantifies the relative
importance of fundamentals versus microstructure within a unified framework, demonstrating that the weakening of traditional price determination

remain relevant in some currencies, while monetary policy and trader heterogeneity dominate in others.

could be evaluated more directly in future multi-market work.
These contributions are relevant for global investors and policymakers
seeking to understand pricing mechanisms across both spot and skewness
dimensions. The contribution is therefore conditional rather than universal:
the paper identifies which pricing channels were most salient in the 2010–
2020 environment, not invariant determinants that must dominate across all
monetary regimes. By identifying classes of macroeconomic, monetary and
behavioural indicators that drive price formation, the study helps inform
policy design and trading decisions, and encourages researchers to adopt a
more multi-dimensional perspective on exchange rate determination beyond
traditional macroeconomic approaches.

2. Literature
This section reviews the existing theoretical and empirical literature on
foreign exchange microstructure and the structural equation modelling methodology. The aim is to identify factors influencing foreign exchange rates beyond
traditional macroeconomic theories and to discuss outstanding issues in price
determination research. The review is organised around three core themes:
the empirical limitations of macroeconomic fundamentals in explaining exchange rate movements; the emergence of order flow, trader heterogeneity,
and market microstructure as alternative explanatory channels; and the suitability of structural equation modelling as a framework for integrating these
competing mechanisms.
2.1. Traditional price determination theories and problems
Following the collapse of the Bretton Woods system in the early 1970s,
theories of exchange rate determination relied heavily on macroeconomic fundamentals and focused on long-run relationships. Purchasing Power Parity
(PPP) is the classic framework, positing that inflation differentials determine
currency values through arbitrage [8]. Although PPP predicts stable longrun tendencies, actual exchange rates display far greater short-run volatility
[6].
Economic factors such as productivity, labour market conditions, and
balance-of-payments positions have also been used to explain exchange rate
movements.
Productivity growth in tradable sectors tends to appreciate
real exchange rates [2], unemployment fluctuations influence currency values
[1], and persistent trade imbalances affect demand for domestic currency
[23, 5, 34]. Recent research highlights that these relationships can be complex
and asymmetric across countries and time [3].
Monetary models explain exchange rates through relative money supplies
under assumptions of capital mobility and long-run PPP [20]. Interest rate
differentials further affect exchange rates via capital flows [20], while money
aggregates influence relative currency values [21]. Although monetary policy
affects foreign exchange prices only indirectly, its importance has grown in
recent decades.
Financial shocks represent a further class of determinants.
Exchange
rates commonly overshoot following shocks [50], and markets react strongly
to news surprises [13, 21]. Volatility connectedness has been found to depend on regional ties, commodity exposure, and exchange rate regimes [53].

Stress episodes in particular reveal dynamics not captured by traditional
fundamentals, emphasising the relevance of microstructure features [15].
Despite this extensive body of work, forecasting exchange rates remains
difficult. Fundamental models are frequently outperformed by a random walk
[41, 11, 25]. Trading activity offers superior explanatory power over fundamentals [18]. Order flow studies, such as King, Sarno and Sojli [32], demonstrate strong predictive content in disaggregated flow data, especially for
commodity-linked currencies. Other research documents persistent failures
of traditional monetary models [42], while interest rate-based work highlights
systematic deviations from UIP driven by carry trade activity [30].
Additional evidence indicates that floating rates cannot be understood
without accounting for extreme volatility episodes [19], and that the news
surprise framework alone is insufficient due to unrealistic assumptions about
instantaneous information processing [31]. This reinforces the need for frameworks that integrate macroeconomic and microstructure mechanisms.
2.2. Market microstructure
As FX markets became more electronic and competitive, microstructure
research developed to explain short-run price dynamics that macroeconomic
theories could not account for. Market microstructure examines how trading processes, information flows, and participant behaviour translate into
price formation [16, 38, 49, 12, 40]. These findings underscore that market
microstructure itself is not merely a transmission mechanism but an active
determinant of pricing dynamics.
A central theoretical insight is participant heterogeneity. Differences in
information and behaviour shape adverse selection [22], market segmentation
and pass-through [14], and the effects of transparency on spreads, depth
and volatility [45].
Market activity varies systematically across currency
pairs and trading sessions [46], supporting the view that currencies differ
structurally in liquidity and participant composition. Heterogeneous trading
behaviour is evident in volume–price relationships [52] and in differences in
order aggressiveness and size [36]. Intermediary heterogeneity further affects
exchange rates via leverage constraints and balance-sheet capacity [10].
Microstructure research has also expanded to consider network effects
and spillovers. Volatility connectedness depends on trade ties and regime
characteristics [53]. Policy uncertainty generates cross-currency transmission
mechanisms that operate through microstructure channels rather than fun5

Despite these advances, several gaps remain. Existing studies often analyse order flow or fundamentals separately, offering limited insight into their
relative importance. Work on trader heterogeneity tends to focus on single
currencies, limiting cross-market comparability. Moreover, most studies treat
spot rates and option-implied skewness independently even though modern
markets jointly price both. This research addresses these gaps by modelling
fundamentals and microstructure determinants together across six currencies and by integrating spot and skewness within a unified latent-variable
framework.
Taken together, the literature points to a common tension in FX research.
Traditional macroeconomic models remain informative about long-run valuation forces, but they often struggle to explain short- to medium-run price
formation in liquid markets where information is processed through intermediaries, inventories, and option-based expectations. This motivates an empirical design that compares macroeconomic, monetary, shock-related, and
trader-position channels within a single framework rather than evaluating
them in isolation.
2.3. Structural Equation Modelling
Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) has become increasingly relevant
for analysing financial markets where multiple latent mechanisms interact
[24]. PLS-SEM is particularly suitable for modelling FX price determination
due to its ability to handle high-dimensional indicator sets, multicollinearity, and non-normal data [27, 26]. It emphasises prediction, accommodates
both formative and reflective measurement, and produces stable estimates in
samples that would challenge covariance-based methods.
While traditional regression approaches analyse relationships among observed variables only, SEM enables simultaneous estimation of relationships
among latent constructs [51]. In PLS-SEM, latent scores are constructed by
projecting observed data onto weight matrices, with the structural model
linking latent variables and the measurement model defining their relationship to indicators [35, 29, 54, 55]. Reflective indicators represent manifestations of latent constructs, while formative indicators define them [24]. This
research uses both, allowing macroeconomic, monetary, and behavioural influences to be incorporated within a single framework.

2.4. Conceptual Microstructure Framework
To connect theory with empirical implementation, this subsection outlines the mechanisms through which the included constructs influence exchange rates. FX markets feature heterogeneous information sets, inventory
considerations, position adjustment, and asymmetric volatility expectations
[37, 38]. These characteristics allow institutional and non-institutional positions, monetary policy variables, and volatility shocks to influence prices
alongside macroeconomic fundamentals.
Inventory-risk mechanism. Institutional traders adjust positions based on
balance-sheet constraints and risk-management considerations [10]. Marketmakers respond to sustained imbalances by adjusting quotes [22], and cumulative order flow transmits such pressures to prices [17]. The institutional
construct captures these effects.
Information-dispersion mechanism. Institutional participants absorb and act
on information more rapidly than non-institutional traders, whose behaviour
reflects slower-moving sentiment and biases [43]. Heterogeneous order-flow
categories exhibit different predictive power [32], and aggregated positions
therefore provides information not contained in fundamentals alone [18].
This motivates distinguishing institutional from non-institutional latent constructs.
Position-rebalancing mechanism. Position data offer a stable representation
of net exposures relative to high-frequency order flow [46]. Persistent positioning imbalances exert price pressure when positions are extended or unwound. Differences in order aggressiveness and leverage constraints across
trader types support modelling separate institutional and non-institutional
constructs [36, 10].
Option-market expectation mechanism. Risk reversals capture expected asymmetry in future currency movements [39, 4].
Skewness reflects forwardlooking information distinct from historical volatility [7]. Volatility shocks
influence both spot and skewness [50], and stress conditions amplify microstructure effects [15]. Including risk reversals in the price construct therefore incorporates option-market expectations into the overall pricing process.

Synthesis and empirical implementation. These mechanisms jointly justify

The PLS-SEM framework operationalises this by aggregating multiple indicators within each theoretical

full range of market outcomes, recognising that exchange rates reflect both
fundamental information and heterogeneous trading behaviour [32].
Theoretical foundations of trader heterogeneity effects. The influence of trader
heterogeneity on exchange rates is supported by theoretical models in which
agents differ in information, risk tolerance, and capital constraints. Such
differences generate persistent price effects even in rational-expectations settings [52]. Adverse-selection models link trader identity to bid-ask spreads
[22], and intermediary-heterogeneity models show that leverage constraints
create time-varying FX risk premia [10]. Behavioural evidence further indicates distinct patterns across institutional and retail traders [43, 36]. The
latent constructs used in this research test whether these theoretical mechanisms manifest in observable pricing outcomes when fundamentals are controlled for.
2.5. Mapping Theoretical Mechanisms to Latent Constructs
For clarity, the empirical model maps the theoretical mechanisms discussed above into seven latent constructs. The Inflation construct captures relative price-level dynamics associated with purchasing-power considerations. The Economic construct captures the broader relative real-side
macroeconomic environment, including production, external balance, and
labour-market conditions. The Monetary construct captures the relative
stance of monetary policy and liquidity conditions. The Shock construct
captures news-driven and volatility-driven repricing through surprise indices
and option-implied volatility.
The two trader constructs map directly to the microstructure mechanisms. Institutional captures inventory pressure, risk-bearing capacity,
and information processing by asset managers and leveraged-money traders.
Non-Institutional captures slower-moving positioning associated with
commercial and retail participation, including hedging demand and sentimentbased behaviour. Finally, the Price construct combines spot exchange rates
and risk reversals in order to measure both realised exchange-rate movements
and forward-looking asymmetry embedded in option markets.

3. Data and methodologies
3.1. Data
This study uses weekly data from 2010 to 2020 covering six major currency pairs, as summarised in Tables 8, 9, and 10. We examine determinants
of foreign exchange pricing using the spot market rate and volatility skewness.
Macroeconomic and money-aggregate series are included as control variables
and held constant within each month; this steady-state arrangement conditions the relationships among higher-frequency financial variables under
prevailing economic environments. Industrial production serves as a proxy
for productivity.
Market data are sourced from Bloomberg, and traders’
positions from the Chicago Mercantile Exchange and the Financial Futures
Association of Japan. Position variables are measured as of Tuesday, whereas
spot exchange rates and risk reversals are recorded at the Friday close of the
same week.
Position data are prioritised over order-book data given the latter’s limitations, including scarcity of information, susceptibility to manipulation, and
regulatory constraints.
One limitation of weekly position data relative to high-frequency order
flow is the loss of intraday and intraweek dynamics, including the immediate
price impact of individual trades. However, position data capture cumulative net exposures that reflect sustained directional conviction across trader
categories, which is the relevant dimension for identifying systematic pricing
channels at the weekly frequency used here.
Moreover, the public availability and regulatory standardisation of CFTC reports ensures replicability
across currencies, which is not generally feasible with proprietary order-flow
datasets.
The disaggregation of trader positions into institutional and non-institutional
categories follows King, Sarno and Sojli [32], who show that heterogeneous
customer order flow groups exhibit distinct predictive power for exchange
rates. For indicators where the economic interpretation is directionally opposite to the underlying construct (e.g. base currency interest rates, base

3.2. Methodologies
The empirical methodology employs Partial Least Squares Structural
Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) to analyse price formation through latent
variables. PLS-SEM is selected over covariance-based SEM due to its suitability for exploratory, prediction-oriented analysis with many correlated
indicators, its tolerance for non-normality, and its robustness in relatively
small samples [24, 48, 27, 26]. This component-based approach aligns well
with foreign exchange microstructure research, where economic, monetary
and behavioural variables interact simultaneously. One trade-off is that emphasising explained variance may reduce focus on strict model fit, raising
potential concerns about overfitting and external validity.
Six latent constructs are specified: inflation, economic, monetary,
shock, institutional and non-institutional. Constructing latent variables aggregates related indicators and mitigates multicollinearity but may
also mask within-construct heterogeneity and implicitly weight indicators
equally. Both formative and reflective measurement are used: economic fundamentals enter formatively as determinants of construct variation, while
trader positions are modelled reflectively as expressions of underlying behaviour. Modelling institutional and non-institutional traders separately addresses a gap in behavioural FX pricing research, though this two-group distinction may still simplify differences in leverage, timing and risk exposure
across categories.
The composite price construct combines spot exchange rates with risk reversals (volatility skewness), capturing both realised movements and forwardlooking asymmetry derived from options markets. This enriches price measurement but assumes both components contribute comparably, which may

PLS latent scores are obtained by projecting observed data X onto weights

In PLS-SEM, the outer (measurement) model links indicators to latent variables, either reflectively via X = Λξ + ε or formatively via ξ = ΠX + ε,
while the inner (structural) model relates latent variables through η = Bξ+ζ
[35, 54, 55]. Overall, PLS-SEM provides a coherent framework for integrating
macroeconomic, monetary and behavioural determinants of spot and skewness, while acknowledging limitations in formal hypothesis testing compared
to covariance-based SEM.
3.3. Specifying Latent Constructs
Informed by the price determination literature, indicators such as CPI,
unemployment, trade balance, industrial production, policy rates, money
aggregates, surprise indices and implied volatility are combined to form four
latent variables (Table 1).
Inflation captures relative price dynamics between the base country
and the United States; Economic summarises the broader relative realside environment through production, trade, and labour-market conditions;
Monetary represents the relative policy and liquidity stance; and Shock
captures news- and volatility-driven repricing through surprise indices and
implied volatility. The Institutional and Non-Institutional constructs
proxy heterogeneous trading pressure from different trader classes, reflecting
differences in information processing, risk-bearing capacity, and trading motives. Because all exchange rates are defined against the U.S. dollar, U.S.
variables are included not for statistical completeness alone, but to represent
the benchmark conditions against which the base currency is priced. The
constructs should therefore be read as summaries of relative bilateral pricing
channels rather than stand-alone domestic conditions.
The PRICE construct, the dependent variable of the model, includes spot
market rates and risk reversals, the latter representing volatility skewness.
Risk reversals are included because they reflect option traders’ expectations
of price asymmetry. While spot prices incorporate activity from a broad set of
participants, the option market is dominated by institutional traders. Given
that microstructure features influence both spot and option pricing [45], capturing both dimensions within a single framework is essential. Recognising

contributes materially to price formation.

4.3. Outer loading and outer weight analysis
Construct reliability and validity are assessed by evaluating the outer
(indicator) model jointly with the structural paths. Whereas total effects
capture both direct and indirect relations between latent variables, outer
loadings quantify the correlation between a latent construct and its indicators in reflective measurement, while outer weights capture the relative
contribution of indicators in formative measurement [33]. Reflective loadings
are correlations between latent scores and indicators; formative weights are
estimated from a regression of the latent composite on its indicators, with
larger absolute values indicating stronger unique contributions.
Table 12 reports outer loadings (reflective) and weights (formative) by
construct and currency. Absolute values ≥0.70 are flagged with a star to denote strong measurement contribution. High loadings imply reliable measurement and well-defined constructs, while high weights indicate that individual
indicators uniquely shape the latent construct. These results complement:
weak ECONOMIC and INFLATION→PRICE relations at the structural
level co-occur with small or negative weights on their indicators, whereas
MONETARY, SHOCK, and PRICE blocks exhibit consistently stronger measurement properties.
For the ECONOMIC construct, contributions are heterogeneous. Industrial production loads strongly in CAD (0.908) but near zero in JPY and
negatively in EUR and AUD. Trade balance contributes strongly in EUR
(0.808) and JPY (0.890) but negatively in CAD (-0.234) and GBP (-0.271),
consistent with cross-country differences in the role of external balances.
Unemployment contributes little across currencies, aligning with the weak
ECONOMIC→PRICE relations reported earlier.
INFLATION contributions are also uneven.
Base-country CPI loads
strongly in EUR and GBP (≈1.0) but negatively in AUD (-1.006).
US
CPI contributes positively in JPY (0.963) but negatively in CAD (-0.903).
These asymmetries highlight the non-uniform transmission of inflation signals across economies.
Within the MONETARY construct, policy rates dominate. The basecountry policy rate is strongly positive in EUR (0.888) but negative in JPY
(-0.966) and in CAD, GBP, AUD, and NZD. US policy rates contribute
positively in CAD and GBP, but little in EUR and JPY. By contrast, M1
aggregates show negligible contributions. These results confirm that policy
rates, rather than monetary aggregates, drive the MONETARY→PRICE
channel.

5. Methodological Considerations and Robustness
This section addresses methodological issues relevant to applying Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) to foreign exchange time series data. We discuss stationarity properties, justify the use
of PLS-SEM over conventional time-series approaches, interpret the high explanatory power obtained in our models, and highlight limitations that guide
the interpretation of our findings.
5.1. Subperiod robustness
To assess temporal stability, the weekly sample is divided into two chronological halves. The objective is not to treat the split as a formal break test,
but to examine whether the main full-sample associations are broadly replicated across two distinct subperiod environments. The results indicate partial persistence rather than full stability: some core relationships reappear
across halves, but the relative ranking and precision of individual channels
vary materially across currencies. The first subsample covers January 2010 to

The first half broadly corresponds to the post-crisis environment of subdued
inflation, low policy rates, and commodity-price adjustment, whereas the second covers a period of greater monetary-policy divergence and early policy
normalisation, including the Federal Reserve’s December 2015 rate increase.
The purpose of the exercise is not to impose a structural break ex ante,
but to test whether the relative importance of fundamentals, monetary variables, shocks, and trader position is preserved across two different market
environments.
Given the size of the model, the subperiod analysis focuses on the structural relations into the Price construct and on the explanatory power of
Price, rather than on a full comparison of all measurement coefficients. The
results indicate partial but not complete stability. The broad cross-currency
message of the paper survives, but the relative strength of individual channels
is clearly regime-dependent.
For JPY, trader heterogeneity remains the most persistent feature across
subsamples. In the first half, both Institutional and Non-Institutional
effects are statistically significant and economically material, with total effects of 0.653 and −0.338, respectively. In the second half, the institutional

while the non-institutional effect becomes smaller (Non-Institutional →

first half exhibits a richer multi-channel structure that narrows considerably
in the second.
CAD shows the clearest change in composition across subsamples. In

with a large negative economic effect (Economic →Price = −0.642) and a

5.2. Sensitivity to Construct Composition
A concern in latent-variable modelling is that the estimated paths may
depend on the precise composition of the formative blocks.
To examine
this, we re-estimate the model after excluding base-country M1, US M1, and
the US surprise index, which show weaker and less consistent measurement
contribution than policy-rate and implied-volatility indicators in the baseline
specification.
The restricted specification does not overturn the main full-sample interpretation, but it does reveal some sensitivity at the boundary between the
macroeconomic and monetary blocks. Inflation remains weak across almost
all currencies, while monetary and trader-position channels continue to account for many of the strongest supported effects. At the same time, the
Economic construct becomes significant in EUR and GBP as well as CAD,
indicating that some full-sample inferences depend on how weaker macro and
monetary indicators are grouped. The exercise therefore supports robustness
in broad direction, though not complete invariance of individual path estimates.

5.3. Interpretation of High R2 Values
The explanatory power of the model is high by the standards of exchangerate research, but these values require careful interpretation.
Nature of the dependent construct. The Price construct combines spot exchange rates with option-implied risk reversals. This enriches the dependent
variable by incorporating both realised price movements and forward-looking
asymmetry. Because risk reversals are themselves related to volatility conditions, expectations, and positions, the model captures more information
than a spot-rate specification alone.
Informational content of microstructure variables. A substantial literature
shows that order flow and related microstructure measures can explain a
meaningful share of exchange-rate variation. In this study, trader-position
variables proxy for cumulative exposures and inventory imbalances at a lower
frequency. The fact that Institutional, Non-Institutional, and Shock
constructs repeatedly emerge as important in several currencies is therefore economically plausible. At the same time, the subperiod results show
that their importance is uneven across currencies and periods, which argues
against interpreting the high explanatory power as evidence of a single universal mechanism.
In-sample emphasis of PLS-SEM. PLS-SEM is designed to maximise insample explained variance, so the reported R2 values should be interpreted as
internal explanatory measures rather than evidence of out-of-sample forecasting superiority. The subperiod exercise provides a limited check on stability,
but it is not a substitute for rolling or holdout validation. A formal pseudo
out-of-sample exercise would therefore be a useful extension once a longer
sample is available.
Cross-currency heterogeneity. Differences in explanatory power across currencies remain informative. Some currencies retain relatively strong explanatory power across both halves, while others show substantial changes in the
contribution of individual channels. This heterogeneity is consistent with
the broader conclusion of the paper: FX price determination is not governed
by a single universal model, but varies with market structure, participant
composition, and the prevailing monetary and volatility environment.
Overall, the robustness checks strengthen the paper by showing that the
full-sample results are not driven solely by one half of the sample, while also

This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6806248
Preprint not peer reviewed

qualifying the interpretation of pooled estimates. The main contribution of
the paper is therefore comparative rather than structural: it identifies which
pricing mechanisms are more important in different currencies and periods,
but it does not imply that those mechanisms are constant through time.
5.4. Timing Convention and Reverse-Causality Considerations
A potential concern is reverse causality between trader positions and
exchange-rate movements. In our data construction, however, position variables are measured on Tuesday, whereas the dependent price indicators are
taken at the Friday close of the same week. This sequencing reduces simultaneity concerns because the explanatory variables are observed earlier than
the end-of-week pricing outcomes. At the same time, the design does not
fully eliminate endogeneity, since Tuesday positions may themselves respond
to price movements realised earlier in the week. The results should therefore
be interpreted as temporally ordered predictive associations rather than fully
identified causal effects.
5.5. Justification for PLS-SEM Over Traditional Time Series Methods
The choice of PLS-SEM over more traditional econometric approaches
such as VARs, VECMs, local projections, or dynamic factor models is motivated by the specific objective of this paper: to compare the relative importance of multiple macroeconomic, monetary, shock, and trader-position
channels within a unified cross-currency framework. This choice entails advantages in dimensionality reduction and construct formation, but also important trade-offs in dynamic interpretation and causal identification.
Dimensionality and construct formation. Our empirical design includes 115
observed indicators used to form six exogenous latent constructs (Inflation,
Economic, Monetary, Shock, Institutional, Non-Institutional)
and one endogenous Price construct for each currency.
A conventional
multivariate time-series system with this information set would face severe
dimensionality constraints. Even a relatively small VAR with six observed
variables and two lags requires 6 × (6 × 2 + 1) = 78 coefficients per equation,
and 468 coefficients in the system as a whole. Extending such a framework to
the full indicator set would be impractical without imposing strong additional
restrictions or carrying out a separate dimension-reduction step.
PLS-SEM addresses this problem by aggregating correlated indicators
into economically motivated latent variables, thereby allowing the analysis

Distributional robustness. Trader-position variables, surprise indices, and volatility measures exhibit skewness, fat tails, and episodic jumps. Covariancebased SEM typically relies on likelihood-based estimation under stronger
distributional assumptions, and standard time-series models are often estimated under Gaussian or quasi-Gaussian approximations. PLS-SEM is less
demanding in this respect: it is component-based, does not require multivariate normality, and inference is obtained through bootstrapping [27, 26].
This is useful in the present setting, where the data combine macroeconomic
controls with financial and position variables that have heterogeneous timeseries properties.
Trade-off relative to dynamic time-series models. The main limitation of
PLS-SEM relative to VARs, VECMs, local projections, or state-dependent
dynamic models is that it abstracts from dynamic adjustment and feedback
effects. Exchange rates, option-implied skew, policy variables, and trader
positions may influence one another over time, and a dynamic specification
is better suited to tracing persistence, lag structure, and impulse propagation. By contrast, the present PLS-SEM framework should be interpreted as
a reduced-form comparison of contemporaneous predictive channels at the
weekly frequency, rather than as a model of full intertemporal transmission.
This trade-off is deliberate. The purpose of the paper is not to estimate
impulse responses or to identify structural monetary-policy shocks, but to
evaluate which classes of variables are most strongly associated with FX
prices once a high-dimensional information set is organised into comparable
latent constructs. In that sense, PLS-SEM is used here as a comparative mapping device: it identifies whether monetary, macroeconomic, shock-related,
or behavioural channels are more salient in different currencies, while leaving
the precise timing of dynamic adjustment to future work.

Causal interpretation and identification. PLS-SEM does not by itself solve
the identification problem. The estimated coefficients represent predictive
associations conditional on the model and the chosen construct design; they
should not be interpreted as structural causal parameters. This limitation
is particularly relevant for policy interpretation, because some explanatory
variables may react endogenously to exchange-rate movements or to common
underlying shocks. The paper therefore interprets the results as evidence on
relative pricing associations rather than as proof of causal transmission.
At the same time, the main qualitative conclusions are likely to remain
relevant in a more explicitly dynamic framework. In particular, the weak role
of inflation and broad macroeconomic fundamentals, and the stronger role of
monetary and trader-position channels in several currencies, are unlikely to
disappear once lags are introduced. What would likely change in a VAR- or
local-projection setting is not the broad ranking of channels, but the timing,
persistence, and decomposition of effects into direct, indirect, and feedback
components. A fully dynamic analysis would therefore be a complement to,
rather than a substitute for, the present latent-construct approach.
Positioning of the method within the paper. For these reasons, PLS-SEM is
best viewed as appropriate for the present paper’s exploratory and comparative objective, but not as a complete substitute for traditional time-series
analysis. The method allows us to study multiple theoretically motivated
pricing channels jointly in a high-dimensional setting, while the costs are
weaker dynamic interpretation and the absence of structural identification.
We therefore treat the results as conditional evidence on the relative importance of FX pricing mechanisms in the 2010–2020 environment, and not as
invariant causal coefficients.
Theoretical flexibility.. VAR and VECM models treat all variables as endogenous and impose no theoretical structure. Our research question, however,
concerns the relative importance of competing determinants—monetary policy, fundamentals, shocks, and trader heterogeneity—in shaping prices. PLSSEM accommodates this directional structure by allowing latent variables to
influence Price in line with established exchange rate theories, without requiring full system identification or dynamic structural restrictions.
This flexibility is an advantage for exploratory analysis but entails a tradeoff: PLS-SEM does not yield causal parameters or conventional fit statistics.

6. Conclusion and Discussion
6.1. Summary of Key Findings and Contributions
This research provides three substantive empirical contributions to the
study of foreign exchange price determination. First, it offers the first systematic cross-currency quantification of trader heterogeneity, showing that
pricing dynamics differ materially across markets: both institutional and
non-institutional trader blocks are significant in JPY and GBP, with the
non-institutional block larger in total-effect magnitude, non-institutional position is most influential in AUD, and both categories are largely insignificant
in less liquid markets such as NZD. These findings reject the presumption of
uniform microstructure transmission across currencies.
Second, we quantify the limited and uneven contribution of traditional
fundamentals to FX pricing within the 2010–2020 sample. Inflation is statistically significant in only one of six currencies (EUR), and economic conditions matter for EUR, GBP, and CAD. This suggests not that fundamentals
are universally irrelevant, but that within the 2010–2020 sample their explanatory power is relatively small compared with monetary and behavioural
factors.
Third, the NZD results suggest a possible boundary condition for the
salience of microstructure-based pricing.
In particular, the comparatively
weaker and less stable estimates for NZD are consistent with the view that liquidity conditions and participant composition may shape the extent to which
trader-position channels appear in the data. This interpretation should be
treated as suggestive rather than definitive, since the present design does not
directly identify the mechanism linking liquidity, institutional participation,
and pricing strength.
Overall, within the 2010–2020 sample, the latent variable framework and
use of PLS-SEM indicate that FX pricing was more strongly associated with
trader heterogeneity, monetary policy transmission, and volatility-linked expectations than with inflation or broad economic fundamentals. These findings highlight the multidimensional nature of price formation in an environment characterised by low inflation and subdued growth.

6.2. Principal Findings
The main findings are:
(i) Traditional fundamentals play a limited and non-uniform role within
the sample. Inflation shows negligible influence, while economic conditions are significant in approximately half the currencies examined.
Inflation is statistically significant in only one of six currencies (EUR).
By contrast, economic conditions reach significance in EUR, GBP,
and CAD, a frequency comparable to that of trader-heterogeneity constructs. However, the magnitude of economic-condition effects is substantially smaller than that of monetary and trader-position channels
where both are significant, indicating that fundamentals contribute to
pricing but are not the dominant channel in the 2010–2020 sample.
(ii) Monetary policy dominates traditional macro channels. Policy rates
exert strong influence in EUR, JPY, GBP, and CAD, whereas monetary aggregates contribute little. Pricing therefore reflects expectations
shaped by forward guidance rather than broad money dynamics.
(iii) Trader heterogeneity appears as a recurrent pricing channel in several
currencies. Both institutional and non-institutional position generate
persistent price effects in JPY, GBP, and AUD. These results challenge homogeneous-agent assumptions and indicate that differences in
information, risk tolerance, and investment horizons are economically
meaningful.
(iv) Currency-specific regimes and possible boundary conditions matter. Explanatory power varies sharply across currencies. High R2 values in
EUR, GBP, CAD, JPY, and AUD contrast with weak fit for NZD,
reflecting structural differences in liquidity, composition of market participants, and exposure to idiosyncratic shocks.
(v) Volatility shocks transmit selectively.
Shock-related effects are most
clearly identified for GBP/USD in the full sample, while remaining
weak or unstable elsewhere. This suggests that volatility-linked repricing is currency-specific rather than universal, and that local market
structure and episode-specific conditions can shape the strength of the
shock channel.

These results advance the literature methodologically by demonstrating
that macro fundamentals and trader heterogeneity can be jointly modelled
through latent constructs; theoretically by documenting the declining relevance of classical drivers in a low-inflation regime; and empirically by providing the first cross-currency decomposition of trader-type influences on FX
pricing.
6.3. Implications for Policy and Market Practice
For market participants, the findings imply that traditional macro indicators such as inflation, productivity, and trade balances offer limited guidance for short- to medium-run pricing. Real-time indicators of central bank
communication, shifts in trader positions, and sentiment now play a more
prominent role.
For central banks, the results highlight the sensitivity of exchange rates to
policy rates and forward guidance. In EUR, JPY, GBP, and CAD, monetary
announcements translate directly into pricing pressures, underscoring the
need for careful communication.
For regulators, the prominence of trader heterogeneity suggests that surveillance frameworks should monitor both institutional and retail positions, as
each can generate persistent pricing effects depending on the currency.
For international coordination, the strong currency-specific differences
imply that unified exchange rate management frameworks are unlikely to be
effective. Policy designs must reflect the microstructure characteristics of
each currency.
Accordingly, the paper should be read as evidence on relative pricing
channels under a specific macro-financial regime, not as a rejection of macroeconomic models in all periods.
6.4. Contextual Boundaries and Regime Specificity
The findings reflect conditions of the 2010–2020 decade, characterised by
low inflation, compressed cross-country differentials, and zero-bound interest
rates. Under such conditions, purchasing power parity mechanisms are weak,
and microstructure factors gain prominence. This is consistent with the theoretical expectation outlined in the introduction: when inflation differentials
are compressed and policy rates are near the effective lower bound, macroeconomic indicators contain limited incremental information for weekly price
formation, shifting the relative explanatory weight toward monetary signals,
positions, and volatility expectations. In periods of high inflation divergence

or structural volatility, traditional fundamentals may regain relevance. Applying this framework to the post-2020 environment would therefore provide
an important out-of-sample test.
6.5. Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations define the scope of the study. First, PLS-SEM identifies predictive associations rather than causal relationships. Second, weekly
data frequency omits high-frequency dynamics and immediate responses to
news. Third, parameters are assumed stable across the decade, potentially
masking structural breaks.
Fourth, the analysis is conducted in-sample throughout; a rolling-window
or pseudo out-of-sample forecasting exercise would provide a more demanding
assessment of predictive stability and is a priority for subsequent research
using an extended sample.
A further limitation is that the trader-heterogeneity results remain reducedform. The present design distinguishes participant categories by business role
and trading behaviour, but it does not separately identify whether the estimated effects operate primarily through relative trade size, informational advantage, inventory adjustment, or herding. The evidence is therefore stronger
on the existence of segmented trader effects than on the exact mechanism
through which those effects enter prices. The subperiod results offer only
indirect support for state dependence, most clearly in GBP, where shockrelated pricing becomes more prominent in the later half of the sample. A
direct interaction or state-dependent specification would be needed to test
this mechanism more formally.
6.6. Concluding Remarks
This study shows that, within the 2010–2020 sample, FX pricing is more
consistently associated with trader heterogeneity, monetary conditions, and
volatility-linked expectations than with inflation or broad macroeconomic
fundamentals. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating market participant behaviour into exchange rate models and suggest that accurate forecasting requires integrating microstructure considerations alongside
macroeconomic indicators.

Note: Outer loadings and weights are pivotal in latent construct measurement, each serving a unique
purpose (Hair Jr et al., 2012).
Values exceeding 0.7 indicate a substantial contribution of indicator
variables to a construct and suggest a reliable measurement model in which the construct is well-defined
by its indicators. In reflective models, outer loadings are correlation coefficients between latent variable
scores and manifest variables and are presented in absolute terms to avoid negative values.
Outer loading (Xi, LV ) = ρ(Xi, LV ), where outer loading (Xi, LV ) is the loading of manifest variable Xi
on latent variable LV , and ρ(Xi, LV ) is the Pearson correlation coefficient between Xi and LV .
Outer weights, central in formative measurement models, assess the unique contribution of each manifest
variable to a latent construct. Derived from a multiple regression model, these weights determine the
individual impact of manifest variables on the latent variable. High outer weights indicate a strong unique
contribution, while smaller weights can still be meaningful if they add unique information.
Outer weight (Xi, LV ) is the regression coefficient of Xi in predicting LV , where the coefficient estimates
Xi’s unique contribution in the regression with LV as the dependent variable. This condensed explanation
balances the significance of both outer loadings and weights in their respective contexts.
