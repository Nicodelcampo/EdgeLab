Abstract
Many core ideas in mathematical statistics—such as the law of large numbers,
central limit theorems, and dependence diagnostics—underpin standard tools in quantitative finance. Yet these principles are often presented in purely analytical form,
detached from the visual and empirical diagnostics that practitioners routinely rely on.
This paper develops a set of simple, simulation-based visualizations that illustrate
how fundamental statistical principles manifest in stylized return data. We focus on
three themes: (i) convergence of sample mean and realized volatility via the law of
large numbers; (ii) approximate normality of aggregated portfolio returns via the central limit theorem; and (iii) the contrast between weak autocorrelation in raw returns
and strong dependence in volatility proxies such as absolute returns. For each theme,
we provide a concise theoretical statement, a short proof or proof sketch, and a corresponding graphical diagnostic.
The goal is not to introduce new estimators, but to build a transparent bridge
between formal probability results and the visual language of quantitative risk management. The resulting figures can be generated from simulated data and adapted
to real data, making the framework suitable for teaching, exploratory analysis, and
communication of model assumptions.
Keywords: Quantitative finance; law of large numbers; central limit theorem; volatility
clustering; visualization.

Dependence and Volatility Clustering
4.1
Returns vs Absolute Returns
Empirical asset returns often display weak linear autocorrelation but strong dependence
in volatility proxies such as squared or absolute returns. A simple way to visualize this
phenomenon is through the sample autocorrelation function (ACF).
For a mean-zero stationary time series (Yt), the lag-k autocovariance and autocorrelation
are
γ(k) := Cov(Yt, Yt+k),
ρ(k) := γ(k)
γ(0).
In a pure i.i.d. setting as in Assumption 1, γ(k) = 0 and ρ(k) = 0 for all k ≥1. However,
in more realistic GARCH-type models, returns may be uncorrelated while absolute returns
are highly persistent.
4.2
Simple AR(1) Volatility Proxy
As a toy model, consider returns of the form
Rt = σtεt,
where (εt) are i.i.d. with zero mean and unit variance, and the volatility σt follows a stationary
AR(1) process
σt = α + ϕσt−1 + ηt,
with |ϕ| < 1 and (ηt) i.i.d. noise. Then Rt are uncorrelated but exhibit volatility clustering.
Proposition 2 (Weak Autocorrelation of Returns). Under the model above, assume (εt),
(ηt) are independent, centered, and independent of each other, and that σt is stationary.
Then
Cov(Rt, Rt+k) = 0
for all k ≥1.
Proof. For k ≥1,
Cov(Rt, Rt+k) = E[RtRt+k] −E[Rt]E[Rt+k].
Since εt and εt+k are independent with zero mean, and independent of (σs),
E[RtRt+k] = E[σtσt+kεtεt+k] = E[σtσt+k] E[εt] E[εt+k] = 0.
Similarly, E[Rt] = E[σt]E[εt] = 0, so the covariance is zero.

Heavy Tails and QQ-Plots
5.1
Gaussian vs Heavy-Tailed Models
A common criticism of Gaussian return models is their inability to generate extreme events
at observed frequencies. One elementary way to illustrate tail behavior is through quantile–
quantile (QQ) plots, comparing empirical quantiles of standardized returns to those of a
standard normal distribution.
7

As a stylized heavy-tailed alternative, consider i.i.d. returns drawn from a standardized
Student-tν distribution with ν > 2 degrees of freedom. These returns have mean zero and
variance ν/(ν −2), but exhibit heavier tails than the normal distribution.
5.2
Visualization: QQ-Plot of Heavy-Tailed Returns
Figure 4 displays a QQ-plot of simulated standardized t-distributed returns against the
standard normal quantiles. In the centre of the distribution, points lie near the 45-degree
line, while in the tails they deviate systematically, bending away from the line.
Figure 4: QQ-plot of simulated standardized Student-t returns against the standard normal
distribution. Deviations from the 45-degree line in the tails highlight the presence of heavierthan-Gaussian tails, a common feature of financial returns.
In empirical settings, similar QQ-plots are used to diagnose tail behavior and to motivate
heavy-tailed models or extreme-value methods.
6
Conclusion
This paper provides a compact set of visual diagnostics that connect fundamental results in
mathematical statistics to stylized features of asset returns. By combining formal statements
and short proofs with simulation-based figures, we aim to make the underlying statistical
principles more tangible for practitioners and students of quantitative finance.
The examples presented here can be extended in several directions, including multivariate
settings, realistic microstructure noise, and empirical data from specific asset classes. The
8

key message is that rigorous statistical theorems and simple graphics are complementary
tools: the former provide asymptotic guarantees, while the latter reveal how those guarantees
manifest in finite samples and in the presence of model imperfections.
