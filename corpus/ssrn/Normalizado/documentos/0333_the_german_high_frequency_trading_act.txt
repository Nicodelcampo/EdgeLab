The German High-Frequency Trading Act: Implications for
Market Quality
Martin Haferkorn and Kai Zimmermann
Chair of e-Finance
Goethe University Frankfurt
Abstract
With the introduction of the High-Frequency Trading (HFT) Act in May
2013, Germany has become the ﬁrst country that regulates securities
trading ﬁrms based on their infrastructure and order book activity characteristics.
In order to increase the transparency of HFT ﬁrms and to
facilitate market integrity, all non-regulated proprietary trading ﬁrms that
rely on low latency connections, high intraday trading message volumes
and autonomous decision making systems are required to be authorized by
German regulators and fulﬁll a variety of organizational obligations. This
study analyzes the impact of this act by identifying whether a change in
daily trading messages was observable and how this change impacted order
book liquidity levels. We ﬁnd that the German HFT Act has successfully
reduced the amount of intraday trading messages leaving trade executions
only marginally aﬀected. However, a large fraction of those messages lost
were providing high quality liquidity at competitive limits and thereby
decreasing implicit transaction costs, which results in increased bid-ask
spreads after the introduction.
But as those messages were of negligible
sizes, the overall liquidity supply to the order book, measured by the order
book depth level, remains unaﬀected. Therefore, consequences originating
from the absence of unregulated HFT could be considered marginal for
securities markets quality.

Act, Liquidity, Securities Markets, Market Quality, Regulation

Introduction
Over the past decades, the activity of High-Frequency Trading (HFT) ﬁrms around
the world has dramatically increased and shaped the modern securities trading environment. Although the ﬁrst emergence of highly automated trading desks and fully electronic
securities markets can be dated back several years already, academics, politicians and trading ﬁrms still argue over the direct and indirect consequences of this development. Is HFT
responsible for the reduction in transaction costs? Has HFT increased overall market liquidity? Does HFT make markets more fragile? Although many of those questions could
partly be answered by academic studies, the tensions and disagreements among debaters
seem to be steadily fostered by every new piece of evidence being released on this topic.
Especially in the light of the U.S. Flash Crash, the sharp increase in overall intraday trading message volume in the recent past as well as the spectacular software failure of Knight
Capital (a major U.S. HFT), the inherent risks of such activity became apparent and raised
concerns among regulators world-wide who are forced to react in order to prevent potential
future failures and retain market integrity.
On February 28th 2013, the German Bundestag approved the Act for the Prevention of Risks and the Abuse of High-Frequency Trading (German High Frequency Trading
Act, 2013) and pioneered a regulation of HFT ﬁrms within the German ﬁnancial services
sector. The act introduces an authorization requirement as well as further organizational
obligations for unregulated HFT ﬁrms in order to increase transparency of their overall
trading activity as well as their market impact. Although this law is applicable to market participants active in German securities and derivatives markets only, the German
HFT Act actually preempts similar HFT regulation proposed in the MiFID II (Markets in
Financial Instruments Directive) that will be applicable on a European level in 2017. Subsequently, the scope of operation for HFT ﬁrms is getting increasingly restricted although
consequences of such steps are yet to be assessed. The last couple of years already showed
several national attempts in the European area to regulate HFT. In 2012 the French and
in 2013 the Italian government passed resolutions to introduce a national taxation of ﬁnancial transactions (FTT). Both taxes inherit the taxation of HFT based on a cancellation
to transaction ratio greater than 60%. It is non trivial to disentangle the HFT tax eﬀect
from the FTT eﬀect, especially as both taxes allow for various exemptions, e.g., the daily
net inventory position as taxable amount (Coelho, 2014). Coelho (2014) indicates a sharp
lock-in eﬀect especially by HFT participants that relinquish trading in the presence of the
FTT and the additional HFT tax. However, as consequences for overall market quality can
be attribute to the FTT as well as to the HFT taxation, we still do not know whether and
how HFT ﬁrms aﬀected market quality directly. The German HFT Act can therefore be
seen as a natural experiment that could give deeper and unbiased insights on the impact
of HFT regulation on trading behavior and market quality.
Within this paper, we analyze the structural change in trading and order book message activity originating from the introduction of the German HFT Act in May 2013. Most

the total volume of intraday trading messages in securities trading within a short time
range. We therefore conclude that several HFT ﬁrms have adapted their trading behavior, migrated towards non-regulated trading venues or simply quit trading to avoid being
authorized by BaFin. We observe a sharp decline in the number of daily order submission
and modiﬁcations while the turnover and number of trades only showed a marginal decline. However, by decomposing the change in the trading message traﬃc, we ﬁnd that a
signiﬁcant amount of these missing messages were providing high quality liquidity supply.
These messages were located at the top of the respective order book, i.e., at the best bid
and best ask limit, or even further, were reducing the bid-ask spread directly. Thus, the
average bid-ask spreads increase after the application of the German HFT Act. In comparison, we ﬁnd top order book depth levels not aﬀected at all and therefore conclude that
although the missing messages posted competitive limit prices, their actual volumes where
negligible. In addition, the results further indicate that the larger fraction of the messages
missing after the introduction of the HFT Act where beyond the best bid and best ask and
therefore considered less relevant for immediate liquidity supply.
The German HFT Act
Following the approval of the German HFT Act on February 28th 2013, the ﬁnal
introduction was scheduled to May 15th 2013. The act involves a authorization requirement

for unregulated HFT ﬁrms. Therefore, the act stipulates transitional periods of 6 months,
until November 14th 2013, for ﬁrms domiciled in Germany as well as 9 months, until
February 14th 2014, for ﬁrms that are not domiciled in Germany (German High Frequency
Trading Act, 2013). These transitional periods allow trading participants to adapt towards
the new legal requirements by granting a transition authorization for this period only in
the case they actual apply for authorization. In order to be required for authorization
a ﬁrm has to fall under the deﬁnition of the German HFT Act criteria. Comparable to
the deﬁnition proposed by the European Council within the ﬁnal version of the MiFID
revision (MiFID 2) in 2014 (European Commission, 2014), the German HFT Act deﬁnes
HFT trading ﬁrms based on several infrastructural and activity characteristics. Therefore,
a trading ﬁrm that engages directly or indirectly, i.e., via a broker, in a sale or purchase
of a ﬁnancial instrument for its own account in a German regulated market or multilateral
trading facility falls under the license requirement of the the German HFT Act, if the ﬁrm
applies (I) infrastructures to minimize latency, (II) decision systems that create, transmit or
execute orders autonomously without human intervention and (III) a high intraday trading
message volume caused by submissions, quotes or cancellations (German High Frequency
Trading Act, 2013).
Subsequently, all ﬁrms not already owning a BaFin registered physical oﬃce in Germany are subject to the license obligation if they meet all criteria. Foreign HFT ﬁrms
without German subsidiary can rely on a license granted by another member state of
the European Economic Area (EU passport) if this license allows for proprietary trading.
Otherwise these ﬁrms must apply for a BaFin license and will be supervised as ﬁnancial
services institutions under the German Banking Act. Next to signiﬁcant minimum capital
requirements and a permanent German subsidiary, this regulation requires comprehensive
organizational obligations as well as numerous reporting and disclosure requirements that
must be fulﬁlled after the respective transitional periods. E.g., the trading system applied
by the ﬁrm must ensure suﬃcient reliability to limit a possible software failure and prevent
market impact. The submission of erroneous orders as well as the creation or continuation
of a disorderly market environment must be prevented. The trading system must not be
used for trading strategies that violate the market-abuse directives. Further algorithmic
ﬂagging is introduced, i.e., each HFT order must carry a tag which allows the identiﬁcation
of the algorithm that created the order as well as the respective strategy. The HFT Act
also introduces a number of provisions aimed at regulating certain trading behavior, including an adequate ratio between the submission, modiﬁcation and deletion of orders and
actual transactions (order-to-trade ratio). Trading venues determine the order-to-trade
ratio individually and must charge additional fees for trading behavior which exhibits an
excessive message volume (German High Frequency Trading Act, 2013).
In order to determine the eﬀects of the German HFT Act we therefore analyze the
most obvious pattern observable in trading data that is addressed in the act, the amount of
intraday trading messages. Most of the academic literature identify high intraday message
rates as key characteristic for the tradings activity of HFT ﬁrms (Hagstrmer & Nordn,

Data & Methodology
Dataset & Sample Selection
Since the German HFT Act was adopted domestically, a possible data sample is
restricted to the act’s legislative scope. In particular, the German HFT Act is only applicable to all trading ﬁrms active in German securities and derivatives markets regardless
of a ﬁrms’ physical location. This implies that HFT ﬁrms active in German cross-listed
stocks traded at foreign markets like multilateral trading facilities (MTFs) are not aﬀected
by this act, i.e., the HFTs active at these venues do not need a German HFT authorization. Subsequently, this situation yields a control group setup by comparing the time-series
development of identical, across-listed stocks traded on aﬀected and non-aﬀected markets.
Given the exclusion of market-speciﬁc confounding events, stock pattern and trading characteristics should be comparable between both markets before the implementation of the
law as prices and volumes are closely linked by arbitrage strategies. The highest fragmented stocks in Germany are blue chip stocks of the German DAX 30 blue chip index
mostly traded on the electronic trading system Xetra of Deutsche Boerse AG. Since the
introduction of MiFID in 2007, many European MTFs have claimed a stake of the overall
trading volume of the DAX 30 stocks providing candidates for a suitable control group. The
most dominant competitor in terms of trading volumes for German blue chips stocks is the
London-based BATS Chi-X Europe regulated by the FSA, the U.K. Financial Services Authority. However, in May 2013, close to the ﬁrst implementation date of the German HFT
Act, BATS Chi-X Europe MTF received the status of a Recognised Investment Exchange
(RIE) by the FSA (BATS CHi-X Europe, 2014). This event could very likely confound
the eﬀects caused by the introduction of the German HFT Act, especially if we benchmark changes in Xetra trading characteristics only to BATS Chi-X Europe. Therefore,
we benchmark against the next biggest competitor in terms of traded volume, Turquoise
MTF, and further apply BATS Chi-X Europe RIE as robustness check. For the stocks
traded on Xetra and Turquoise, we rely on data retrieved from the Thomson Reuters Tick
History (TRTH) which covers times and sales tick by tick as well as order book tick data.
We exclude stocks with stock splits and missing data on TRTH. These selection criteria
leave 26 DAX stocks traded on both markets in the ﬁnal sample (excluded constituents are
Commerzbank AG, Deutsche Post AG, Henkel AG & Co. KGaA , Volkswagen AG).

We collect daily trading statistics as well as market quality measures 30 days before
and after each event date, i.e., May 15th 2013, November 14th 2013, as well as February 14th
2014. As stated in the previous section, the introduction was scheduled to May 15th 2013.
Only if market participants applied for authorization, a transitional period was granted.
Otherwise, ﬁrms must quit or change their trading activity on May 15th 2013, to avoid the
application for the BaFin license. Therefore, the ﬁrst event date is of utter importance in
this study. Although no trading ﬁrm has applied for authorization, it is very likely that a
few HFTs exhausted the transitional periods by intending to apply for a license but never
do so. We therefore further analyze the second and third event date respectively. Especially
the third event date is of importance. Currently, the European passport initially introduced
by the Investment Services Directive (ISD) allows ﬁrms supervised by domestic regulators
to operate in other European markets although this might be limited by diﬀerences in
domestic supervision categories. Many ﬁrms were therefore anticipating that, by holding a
local license with other EU regulators, they would be exempted from having to obtain an
additional license with BaFin. On January 22nd 2014, BaFin announced that in general
this would not be the case. In particular, provided their license does not include proprietary
trading authorization as deﬁned in Annex I A 3 of the MiFID, these ﬁrms are still subject
to the German license requirement (Financial News, 2014). Given the late clariﬁcation,
it can be assumed that many foreign ﬁrms quickly adapted their trading behavior or quit
trading after February 14th 2014.
Deﬁning the event window is crucial for the explanatory power of an event study.
Therefore, we follow event windows of similar studies by providing a short term 10 days
symmetrical pre-, and post baseline analysis. To further add robustness to our analysis
and to capture short-term and medium-term eﬀects separately, we additionally focus on
30 days windows before and after the respective events. These observation periods do not
overlap and therefore provide an unbiased analysis of each event day. However, we are not
able to further extent the medium-term windows as on April 1st 2014, Deutsche Boerse
AG updated the exchange infrastructure to allow the ﬂagging of orders and trades initiated
by algorithmic trading systems stipulated by the German HFT Act (Deutsche Boerse AG,
2014). Following April 1st 2014 , we observe a tremendous increase in the overall intraday
trading message volume that indicates a systematic shift in the way intraday messages
are sent to Thomson Reuters. We observe no comparable eﬀect on our control exchanges.
This confounding eﬀect would signiﬁcantly bias our results as this increase would falsely be
captured by our event dummy, therefore we rely on the 30 day event window. Within the
next section, we highlight the applied market quality parameters as well as a description
on how we measure submissions and modiﬁcations to the order book.

The ﬁrst step in our analysis is to evaluate the eﬀect on trading and order book
activity. Thus, we apply established measures to quantify trading activity, i.e., the number of executed trades and the natural logarithm of the Euro denoted turnover per day.
Academia has shown that HFT activity is connected to a high intraday message volume
(Hagstrmer & Nordn, 2013) (Hendershott & Riordan, 2013), we therefore expect that a
possible retreat of HFT materializes in a change in the amount of messages being sent to
the exchange. Subsequently, we further capture the amount of messages submitted to the
order book of the analyzed exchanges. However, as Thomson Reuters does not disclose
the messages it receives from market participants on a tick by tick level, we use a modiﬁed proxy introduced and tested by Haferkorn, Zimmermann, and Siering (2013). Like
Haferkorn et al. (2013), we use tick-by-tick order book snapshot data supplied by TRTH
to estimate the number of messages. TRTH provides data snapshots up to ten order book
price limits (level 1 - level 10) starting from the best bid and best ask limit respectively.
Every time the order book is updated by either a message of a trader (submission, modiﬁcation or deletion) or a trade, a new snapshot is generated. Snapshot-wise, we compare
all respective bid and ask limits between the previous order book situation (t−1) and the
present order book situation (t0) as well as the creation or discontinuation of a limit level.
A submission of a new order to the order book at a prevailing limit price in (t−1) or a
modiﬁcation of a resting order in (t−1) is therefore indicated by an increase in shares at
the same limit price in (t0). Likewise, a decrease in the number of shares from (t−1) to (t0)
would indicate a trade execution, a deletion or a modiﬁcation of a resting order. However,
since a trade execution may theoretically aﬀect multiple limit levels, we cannot just sum
all increases and decreases and subtract the number of trades. This approach would clearly
overestimate the number of deletions and hence the number of messages. We therefore only
concentrate on a respective increase in the number of shares. Therefore our message proxy
only comprises order submissions as well as order modiﬁcations that lead to an increase
in the number of shares. Respectively, we add the number of newly emerged limit levels
in (t0) that were not available at (t−1). In this approach, we have to carefully control for
displacements in the order book. E.g., in the case a limit order at position level 11 just
moved up to the levels we observe (level 1 - level 10) because of a execution at the top
level, is not counted as new submission or modiﬁcation. For further clariﬁcation we show
this process in a two example situations in table 1.
Therefore, every increase in shares at a prevailing limit, counts as one single order
book message. In the second step of our analysis we turn to a ﬁner resolution of the order
book characteristics, giving a more detailed view on market quality changes. In particular
we focus on liquidity quantiﬁed by several measures in order to capture changes in various
order book dimensions (Harris, 2003). Previous studies have found that HFT has an impact
on the bid-ask spread, e.g. Malinova, Park, and Riordan (2013) and (Hendershott, Jones,
and Menkveld (2011)) ﬁnd that (algorithmic trading) HFT decreases the bid-ask spread.

Methodology
We apply a control group setup to disentangle the event eﬀect of the German HFT
Act from concurring time eﬀects that may apply to both stock samples, the control and
treatment group.
Diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences analysis has become widespread in empirical
research determining the eﬀects of political events or medical treatments. In the simplest
possible setup outcomes are observed for two groups and two time periods. One of the
groups is exposed to a treatment in the second period but not in the ﬁrst period. The
second group is not exposed to the treatment in either periods. Systematic diﬀerences
within the ﬁrst period removes confounding biases between the treatment and control
group within the second period and therefore yields the unbiased event eﬀect (Wooldridge,
2002). This concept is easily extended to a setup, where multiple treatment and control
entities are observed over pre- and post-event time series and thus allows for additional
robustness checks within the time and entity dimension. Our methodology is guided by
the following regression equation:

Results
All changes to the market quality characteristics and order book composition occurring due to the introduction of the German HFT act should be highlighted by a change in
the intraday order book message volume, which is characteristic for HFT driven strategies.
We therefore start by analyzing shifts to the trading and order book activity characteristics
after each respective event date. Subsequently, we provide a follow up in-depth analysis of
these events that showed a structural change. Table 1 aggregates estimation results of the
change in daily number of trade executions (Trades), of the natural logarithm of the daily
Euro turnover (Turnover) and the daily number of submissions and modiﬁcations to the
order book (Messages) after each event date respectively.

market reaction. Within 10 days after the introduction of the German HFT Act, although

and modiﬁcations experience a sharp decline of about 10,900 messages. Compared to the

market quality. We repeat the diﬀ-in-diﬀestimation by applying the order book characteristics and market quality proxies as dependent variables. At ﬁrst we therefore separate

a new limit that relieves the prevailing best bid or best ask limit. These messages provide
liquidity in a very competitive and eﬃcient level. They are immediately executable and
reduced the cost of trading, i.e., the bid-ask spread. We therefore analyze, how may of
these high quality messages actually displace after the introduction of the German HFT

Comparable to the ﬁrst event date however, the displacing of those messages does not aﬀect
liquidity depth and therefore again indicates that the volumes of those messages were of
negligible sizes.

Long Term Eﬀects
The analysis so far does not exceed a medium-term 30 days observation window. Because of the change in Deutsche Boerse message reporting following April 2014, a tremendous increase in the amount of order book messages would bias the analysis of the third
event date in case of a larger observation window. Since this represents a systematic bias
in all stocks traded at Xetra, our interaction term will largely underestimate the actual
event eﬀect. However, the ﬁrst event date, May 15th 2014, does allow for a longer time
period observation and therefore for a additional level of robustness. We therefore repeat
the analysis within a 60 days pre- and post event window, doubling the number of observed days compared to the medium term interval. In addition, we drop the initial 10
days following May 15th 2014 in order to omit the initial short-term eﬀect. As seen in
the results section above, the eﬀect on the ﬁrst event date distinctly peaks within the ﬁrst
10 days followed by a period of recovery. By excluding these days, the mean long-term
eﬀect will not be aggravated by the initial peak but only concentrate on the prevailing
long-term eﬀects of the German HFT Act after the recovery. Again, the calculations are
done based on the BATS Chi-X Europe CXE and the Turquoise control sample. Regression
estimation follows equation (1). Appendix table 12 comprise the results on the analysis.
Although the results on the long-term estimation remain comparable between both control
groups, table 12 indicates that after the initial peak especially order book messages recover.
Still, the number of trades remain signiﬁcantly declined after 60 days, but the order book

Conclusion
The stepwise introduction of HFT in securities trading has been the most deﬁning,
extensive and likewise controversial innovation in modern securities trading. Contentious
debates have been held about market fairness versus revenues from investments in IT infrastructure, individual risk management opportunities versus systemic risk, immediate
liquidity provision versus cream-skimming as well as the overall beneﬁt of HFT for the
economy. For many of today’s market participants, politicians and regulators these numerous questions can be broken down to one fundamental question: Are we better oﬀ
without HFT? Within the last years, national policy makers introduced the ﬁrst indirect
measures for HFT regulation via the implementation of national transaction taxes. As
many side-eﬀects arise with the introduction of a transaction tax the observed eﬀects can
not be attributed to HFT solely. We overcome this limitation by analyzing the most recent
case, a direct regulation of HFT ﬁrms in Germany by the introduction of the German HFT
Act in 2013. Our research aims at a deeper academic understanding of the phenomena
HFT but also provide guidance for regulators. The latter one is especially crucial as a
similar regulation will soon be introduced on an European-wide level and therefore heavily
aﬀect the way securities are traded within European markets.
We exploit the fact that the regulation is only applicable within German securities
markets to benchmark the development in market quality at Deutsche Boerse’s Xetra trading system against the Turquoise MTF with a diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences analysis. Our results
indicate that the need for authorization of HFT ﬁrms results in a adaption of prevailing
trading strategies that largely aﬀect trading and order book message characteristics. However, the overall eﬀect on market quality is manageable. In particular we observe signiﬁcant
changes in the order book activity levels in two out of three relevant dates. By further
analyzing both dates, we observe a strong decline in the number of order book messages
accompanied by a weak reduction in the number of trades and turnovers at Xetra. This
initial drop does not fully recover after 30 days, especially after February 14th 2014, a
signiﬁcant fraction of order book messages remain permanently absent. By evaluating the
characteristics of the missing order book messages, we ﬁnd that a considerable amount of

those messages provided immediate liquidity at competitive limit prices at the top of the
order book, eﬀectively reducing implicit transaction costs. On the other hand, our analysis
also provides evidence, that these messages were mainly of smaller volumes, so the German
HFT Act did not aﬀect order book depth levels at the top of the order book. In particular,
the average amount of volume depth at the top of the order book (3 basis points from the
best bid or best ask limit) remained unaﬀected. Concerning the long-term eﬀects of the
German HFT Act, our results stay mixed. After the introduction date May 15th 2014, we
observe a recovery eﬀect in the number of messages sent to the order book. Subsequently,
after 60 days no prevailing eﬀect is measurable anymore. In contrast, liquidity levels remain
deteriorated even after dropping the initial 10 peak days. Given that the eﬀect distinctly
peaks after 30 day at the third event date, February 14th 2014, it remains questionable if
the third event date is also followed by a equally strong recovery. Putting all together, a
prevailing long-term eﬀect is existent but readily comprehensible.
Our contributions are as follows. (I) We show that many HFT ﬁrms operating in an
unregulated fashion prefer to adapt their trading strategies rather to be regulated and to
bear the organizational requirements of such authorization. (II) Even though the German
HFT Act applies to a very small fraction of the overall HFT participants, their impact
on securities markets is perceptible in the order book message traﬃc and market quality
levels. Considering that major HFT ﬁrms as well as most market makers that rely on
such technologies are already authorized before the act and were therefore not aﬀected,
the impact on market quality levels is manageable.
Based on our results, these ﬁrms
falling under the authorization requirement did not operate with high volumes but with
considerable high precision at a cost of high message volumes. (III) Given an Europeanwide implementation of a similar authorization by MiFID 2, we provide ﬁrst evidences on
possible consequences of a direct HFT regulation. We add to the recent debate within
Germany whether the German HFT Act had an inﬂuence on securities trading by showing
distinctive eﬀects of market quality.
However it is important to note that we are also
aﬀected by limitations as the short-term and medium-term analysis might not be suﬃcient.
We overcome this limitation by utilizing a second benchmark to gain additional robustness
and provide a long-term analysis of the ﬁrst introduction event.
As we only focus on
trading in blue chips securities, conclusions for derivative markets, for small- and midcap securities can not be drawn so far. Finally, we conclude that the German HFT act
successfully stipulated the scope for HFT ﬁrms in Germany.
