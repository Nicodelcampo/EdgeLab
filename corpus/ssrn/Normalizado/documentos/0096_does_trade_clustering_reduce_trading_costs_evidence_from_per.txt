 
Does Trade Clustering Reduce Trading Costs? 
Evidence from Periodicity in Algorithmic Trading* 
 
Dmitriy Muravyev 
Michigan State University 
 
Joerg Picard 
Grand Valley State University 
This Draft: June 2022 
Abstract 
We study how trading activity affects liquidity and volatility by 
introducing two periodicities in trading activity. First, trades and 
quote updates are much more frequent within the first 100 
milliseconds of a second than during its remainder. Second, trading 
activity often spikes at intervals of exactly one second. For these two 
periodicities, higher trade and quote intensities lead to higher 
volatility, but they do not significantly affect stock liquidity. These 
periodicities are likely caused by algorithms that trade predictably by 
repeating instructions in loops with round start times and time 
increments. Such predictable behavior is exploitable and may provide 
an example of behavioral biases in trading algorithms. 
 
Keywords: trading seasonality, trading activity, liquidity, algorithmic trading,
algorithmic biases

1. Introduction 
The relation between trading activity, volatility, and liquidity is at the core of every 
financial market. But trading activity is so intertwined with other market variables that it is 
difficult to identify its effect on volatility and liquidity. E.g., volume, volatility, and the bid-ask 
spread all spike when news arrives. An ideal test would compare liquidity between two 
periods that differ in trading activity but are otherwise similar. Classic intra-day and intraweek volume seasonality studies come close to this ideal test and show that trading activity 
and the bid-ask spread are higher during the start and end of a day.1 Unfortunately, news also 
exhibits an intraday periodicity; news accumulates overnight and are processed at the open 
(Hong and Wang (2000)) causing a trading activity spike. Thus, these low-frequency 
periodicities cannot easily separate the effects of news and trading activity. 
We introduce two high-frequency periodicities in trading activity to study the 
relations among trading activity, volatility, and liquidity. In short, execution algorithms trade 
predictably and are much more likely to submit orders in a narrow interval within the next 
second. First, for the absolute periodicity, trading activity is higher at the start of a second, 
e.g., between 11h:11m:11s:000ms and 11h:11m:11s:099ms. About 40% more trades and 
quote updates arrive within the first 100 milliseconds (ms) compared to the preceding 
100 ms and other 100 ms intervals—a sharp discontinuity at the start of a second. After this 
initial spike, trading activity quickly reverts to its average and stays there for the rest of the 
second.2 Similar to the U-shaped intraday volume seasonality, this absolute periodicity is

for the relative periodicity, trading activity spikes are likely to repeat at exactly one-second 
frequencies. E.g., if trading activity spikes at 11s.300ms, it will likely spike again in exactly 
one second at 12s.300ms relative to 100 ms before or after (12.200 or 12.400). The absolute 
periodicity focuses on the first 100 ms of a second, while the relative periodicity focuses on 
the 100ms one second after a large activity spike.3 Both periodicities are of similar magnitude 
and likely share the same underlying mechanism. We confirm these periodicities in two 
independent datasets, the NASDAQ dataset and the Nanex dataset. 
What explains the two periodicities? Patterns in the data suggest that algorithms 
execute trades in a one-second loop. Some trading algorithms are only active during the start 
of a second because their code uses loops with rounded-second start times or time steps. Due 
to a programmer’s psychological preference for round numbers, a time loop often starts at a 
round time, such as 9:30am, and repeats at rounded-time intervals, such as one second.4 
Every second, an algorithm wakes up, checks market conditions, adjusts quotes or initiates 
trades, and deactivates until the next iteration. The relative periodicity is likely driven by the 
one-second loops with non-round starting time perhaps because the algorithm’s clock is not 
synchronized with the exchange. Humans are too slow for such subsecond trading and thus 
cannot cause this periodicity; it takes us at least 200ms to respond to a visual stimulus. 
These two periodicities produce large and concentrated spikes in trading and quoting 
activity while market conditions and news arrivals remain unchanged. The only difference is

To study how these predictable activity spikes affect liquidity and volatility, we 
compare a 100ms time bin with high ex-ante expected trading activity with two adjacent 
100ms bins with lower expected activity.  The absolute periodicity implies higher activity at 
the start of a second, while the relative periodicity implies higher activity exactly one second 
after an activity spike. Volatility is about 20% higher at the start of a second, which is 
comparable to the increase in trading activity; but liquidity—as measured by Kyle’s lambda 
(Kyle 1985)—and the bid-ask spread, does not change significantly. The results are similar 
for the relative periodicity. Trading activity and volatility both spike, but liquidity does not 
change. 
How do high-frequency traders (HFTs), for whom one second is an eternity, respond 
to these activity spikes? In general, many HFTs act as market-makers and provide liquidity, 
while some HFTs take liquidity trading on short-term signals. We find that HFTs provide as 
much liquidity as other investors during activity spikes; that is, the share of trades for which 
HFTs provide liquidity (versus non-HFT liquidity providers) remain constant over a second. 
But HFTs take less liquidity than non-HFTs during the activity spikes. Thus, HFTs mostly 
provide liquidity during activity spikes, and the spikes are caused primarily by non-HFTs. 
How do investors respond to the activity shock as to generate the observed patterns? 
Models of strategic trading can guide us on how investors may respond to the spikes. We do 
not explicitly test the models and merely seek to interpret our results through their lenses. 
In these theories, informed investors respond to spikes in uninformed trading. In the Back 
and Pedersen (1998) model, informed investors are flexible with timing, and thus they would

shift their trading to the uninformed activity spikes until the bid-ask spread becomes 
constant over a second, which matches what we find. In the Admati and Pfleiderer (1988) 
model, informed investors are inflexible, perhaps because their signals are short-lived, and 
thus they cannot easily shift trading to the uninformed spikes. This model implies lower bidask spreads during the expected activity spikes as a higher fraction of trades is uninformed. 
While strategic informed trading is not directly observed, our results are consistent with the 
share of informed traders being constant over a second. Finally, market-makers may prefer 
quoting stable bid-ask spreads because investors dislike flickering quotes (Hasbrouck 
(2013)). Market makers cross-subsidize between spike and non-spike periods, which is also 
consistent with our empirical results.  
The subsecond periodicities shed light on how shocks to volume affect volatility and 
liquidity. But do these results generalize to longer horizons that institutional investors care 
about? We show that the results hold for a regular spike in trading activity on the first day of 
a month. Etula, Rinne, Suominen, and Vaittinen (2019) show that investment funds deploy 
cash inflows on the first day of the month causing a 11% higher volume than on other days. 
This volume spike leads to higher volatility, but the bid-ask spread is similar to other days. 
Overall, these results are consistent with our subsecond periodicity findings.  
Do some execution algorithms behave irrationally? Behavioral finance focuses on 
retail and institutional investors. We propose that trading algorithms can also display unique 
behavioral biases. The two periodicities imply that execution algorithms trade predictably in 
a one-second loop. Such predictable behavior should expose algorithms to front running and 
predatory trading. Instead of checking the market only at the start of a second, an algorithm 
can simply check the market more often. Such changes are easy and cheap to make. We show

that algorithms trade predictably, but our data are not sufficiently granular to quantify the 
costs that these exploitable algorithms incur. 
Besides the relation between trading activity, volatility, and liquidity, we also 
contribute to several other literatures: We connect the literatures on behavioral finance and 
high-frequency trading by showing that trading algorithms can be susceptible to behavioral 
biases. We raise the idea that the biases of trading algorithms, which reflect human 
programmers’ biases, should be considered. E.g., our results suggest that the flickering quote 
puzzle proposed by Hasbrouck (2013) might have a “behavioral” explanation: An HFT reacts 
to its own quote updates and thus creates a self-reinforcing circle of flickering quotes. 
Hasbrouck and Saar (2013) find that HFTS trigger spikes in low-latency activity. We extend 
their findings by introducing another periodicity; we find that activity spikes lead to higher 
volatility and unchanged liquidity. Harris (1991) shows that investors prefer round stock 
prices. We show that trading algorithm prefer round times and time intervals. The prior 
literature focuses on the intraday relation between activity, liquidity, and volatility. Our turnof-the-month test studies this relation beyond simple daily correlations. 
In theory, investors can benefit from coordinating their trading. But how exactly they 
coordinate is unclear. The subsecond periodicities provide a rare example of such a 
synchronization mechanism in action. Algorithms that check the market only at the turn of a 
second trigger the initial distortion, and other trading algorithms respond to this rhythm.

2. Data Description and Variable Construction 
Our main data are provided by NASDAQ. They include trade and quote data for 120 
stocks between 1 January 2008 and 31 December 2009. Although trade records are available 
for all trading days, the best quoted prices or inside quotes (BBO) are only available for 44 
selected trading days within the 2-year period. This dataset is extensively used in the HFT 
literature, and Brogaard, Hendershott, et al. (2014) describe it in detail. Because most of our 
market metrics require matched quotes and trades, we limit our sample to the 44 trading 
days with available quotes. 
Several data features are worth noting: First, the timestamps are synchronized with a 
1ms precision, which lets us study subsecond periodicity. Second, transactions by dark pools 
and internal crosses are excluded, so our results are not affected by indirect market 
participants. Finally, trade records are marked by the trade direction and by the trader type—
HFT or non-HFT that takes or provides liquidity—which lets us study the strategies of HFTs. 
These data are only for NASDAQ transactions and not for other exchanges. 
To ensure the robustness of our results, we also use more recent equity market data 
that include all US exchanges and is analogous to TAQ. The data are provided by Nanex, which 
consolidates price and quote information across exchanges with synchronized timestamps 
of 25 ms precision. We construct a sample selecting trades and NBBO quotes for the same 
120 stocks as in the NASDAQ sample for the 67 trading days between 1 February 2013 and 
15 May 2013. This dataset does neither provide trade direction nor HFT flag. Some tickers

We apply mild data filters to the NASDAQ and Nanex data: Because of the special

3. Periodicities in Trading Activity 
We first show that the order arrival rates exhibit striking periodicities. We call the first 
periodicity “absolute” because it is anchored around full seconds in absolute time. Within the 
first 25 ms of a second, trade and quote arrival rates jump from below average to 40% above 
average before retreating to the average around the half-second mark, as shown in Panel A 
of Figure 1. This pattern is even stronger in 2013 for the Nanex data (Panel B of Figure 1). 
Regression results in Table 3 confirm that the periodicities are statistically and economically 
significant.

other trades? We regress a sub-period indicator of whether a given trade is executed at the

What causes these periodicities? The sharp discontinuity at the turn of a second is 
consistent with algorithms repeating instructions in loops with round start times and round 
time steps; i.e., programs “wake up” at the start of the second and place trade and quote 
orders (the spike) before “going back to sleep” (the decay). Software programmers, like all 
humans, prefer round numbers and programmed algorithms to check the market once a 
second. Even if the programmers are not explicitly aware of the one-second periodicities, 
their algorithm may implicitly respond to the activity spikes and thus amplify the effect.

We also rule out two explanations related to data artifacts. No significant amount of

is not caused by a missing value substitution. To ensure the periodicity is not driven by a

feeds provide information at one-second or lower frequencies. This hypothetical argument 
can affect the absolute one-second periodicity but not the relative periodicity. Finally, does 
news arrival exhibit the one-second periodicity? Using a large database of news events with 
millisecond time stamps, we find that news arrive equally likely throughout a second. We do 
find that news arrival spikes within the first minute of an hour and the first second of a 
minute, but if anything, the two subsecond periodicities are weaker during the first minute 
and the first second. The results are also robust to excluding every minute divisible by five 
from the sample. Overall, most likely these subsecond periodicities in trading activity are 
triggered by algorithmic traders’ preference for round numbers. 
We focus on the subsecond time scale because (i) most trading algorithms operate at 
this or shorter frequencies and (ii) most trading is conducted by execution algorithms, 
making them the dominant market players. One second may seem short, but it is an eternity 
for trading algorithms. For example, HFTs rarely hold positions for more than a second, and 
they are willing to pay millions of dollars to reduce latency by few milliseconds.5

4. Does Trade Clustering Affect Volatility and Liquidity? 
Does trade clustering affect volatility? We find that volatility increases almost one-forone with trading activity; periodicity in activity creates periodicity in volatility. We measure 
volatility in two ways: (i) as the absolute return computed from 100 ms quote midpoints and 
(ii) as an indicator variable for whether the absolute return exceeds a 0.1% threshold. Such 
large price changes are rare (0.2% of the sample) and can be considered price jumps. They 
are less prone to microstructure noise than the absolute midquote returns. The subsecond 
periodicity of these price jumps closely follows the pattern of the trading and quoting activity 
(Figure 3): the jump intensity increases by 15% at the turn of a second. 
To quantify how higher trading activity translates into higher volatility, we can divide 
the relative increase in volatility at the turn of a second by the relative increase in trading 
activity at the same time.  An instrumental variables regression implements this intuition 
and does inference. In the first stage, trading (quoting) activity is instrumented by an 
indicator for the first 100 ms bin. This stage reflects the absolute periodicity in trading 
activity (Section 3). In the second stage, volatility is regressed on the instrumented trading 
activity. Modern statistical packages estimate both stages jointly.  Due to data size, we first 
run the estimation for each stock and day and then aggregate the coefficient estimates 
clustering them by stock and by day. Equations (3.1) and (3.2) summarize the 2SLS setup:

5. Trade Clustering and High-Frequency Trading 
How do HFTS respond to trade clustering? The NASDAQ data provide the HFT and 
liquidity taker flags that are not included in public datasets like TAQ. HFTs are sophisticated, 
typically hold positions for less than one second, and often act as de facto market-makers.  
They are arguably the most informed investors at the subsecond horizon and can potentially 
front-run investors who trade predictably in one-second cycles. Alternatively, they can 
provide more liquidity and trade against this uninformed order flow. We find that HFTs are 
less likely to take liquidity than non-HFTs during the activity spikes. HFTs mostly provide 
liquidity during the spikes, and the spikes are caused primarily by non-HFTs.  
Consistent with the results in Section 3, trading activity spikes for all four investor 
types (active/passive by HFT/non-HFT) at the turn of the second. But liquidity taking by nonElectronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=2496669

6. Daily Periodicity 
After we study the relation between trading activity, volatility, liquidity using 
subsecond periodicities, we show that this relation also holds for longer horizons that 
institutional investors are interested in. While institutional investors rely on execution 
algorithms that potentially cause the subsecond periodicities, these investors typically

18 
 
operate at daily or longer frequencies.  The literature focuses on the intraday relation 
between activity, liquidity, and volatility. Thus, the low-frequency tests in this section extend 
the time horizon typically used in the literature. 
Etula, et al. (2019) show that investment funds deploy cash inflows on the first day of 
the month causing a 11% higher volume than on other days. Thus, trading activity is higher 
on the first day of the month, similar to how it is higher for the first 100 ms of a second (only 
the magnitude is lower, 11% versus 40%). Investors primarily trade to deploy funds rather 
than to exploit private information on this day. 
We compute trading volume, volatility, and liquidity from daily CRSP data. The sample 
includes all common stocks (share codes 10, 11, and 12) with a price of at least $5 and market 
capitalization of more than $100 million; the sample period is from 2000 to 2019. Microcaps 
are of little interest to institutional investors. The final sample consists of 15,289,956 stockdays for 10,004 unique stocks. Trading activity is measured as the logarithm of daily dollar 
volume or as turnover defined as share volume over number of shares outstanding. The bidask spread (spread over midquote) measures liquidity. Volatility is measured as the absolute 
daily return and as the logarithm of high-to-low prices. All variables are winsorized at the 
0.05% level to avoid outliers. Table 9 reports summary statistics.  
We estimate the effect of higher trading activity on market variables with an IV 
regression as in Equations (3.1) and (3.2) applied to the daily sample. In the first stage, log 
volume is instrumented with the first day of a month. In the second stage, instrumented 
trading volume affects the bid-ask spread and volatility. The regressions include stock fixed 
effects. Standard errors are clustered by stock and day.

month. The coefficients of first stage regressions of trading volume and turnover on a dummy

7. Cognitive Bias in Algorithmic Trading? 
Cognitive biases have been extensively studied including their effect on the economy 
and financial markets.6 Three prominent contributors to this field, Daniel Kahneman, Robert 
Shiller, and Richard Thaler, have been awarded Nobel Prizes. Behavioral finance so far 
focused on biases of individual and institutional investors. For example, uncertainty, stress, 
peer pressure, anchoring, and mental heuristics all contribute to irrational investor 
decisions. On the other hand, most trading nowadays is done by execution algorithms. Can

these algorithms behave irrationally? To our knowledge, we are the first to raise this question 
and provide a tentative example of such a bias.  
Machines themselves, of course, do not suffer from cognitive biases since they 
deterministically process an input into an output. But a human programmer must decide on 
how to implement an algorithm, and she is subject to cognitive biases. She employs mental 
heuristics as a shortcut for complex decisions. For example, the programmer’s task is to 
design an execution algorithm that checks market conditions and trades. This is a complex 
problem without a known optimal solution. The programmer may resort to mental heuristics 
and check the market once per second, starting every day with the market opening at exactly 
9:30am. Harris (1991) finds that trade prices cluster on round stock prices, because human 
traders use a round number mental shortcut to reduce mental costs of choosing an optimal 
price. Human programmers apply a similar shortcut while designing a trading algorithm. 
Can this clustering of activity at rounded-time intervals be exploited? In untabulated 
results, we confirm that rounded-time algorithms generate persistent order imbalances. 
Buying at the start of a second is followed by more buying at the start of the next second. 
Front runners can exploit this order imbalance persistence by trading ahead of round-time 
algorithms. However, our data do not allow us to directly show that round-time traders suffer 
negative consequences of their predictive behavior. 
We connect algorithmic trading and behavioral finance. Cognitive biases in trading 
algorithms are common and deserve greater attention. For example, these biases are 
correlated across investors and thus may pose a systematic risk to financial markets.

8. Conclusion 
We introduce two high-frequency periodicities in trading activity and use them to 
study the relation between trading activity, volatility, and liquidity. Trading activity spikes (i) 
at the start of a second compared to its end and (ii) exactly one second after a prior activity 
spike. These periodicities are likely driven by programmers’ preference for round numbers, 
which causes their algorithms to activate only at the start of a second. Using the two 
periodicities, we find that higher trading activity leads to higher volatility but does not affect 
liquidity and trading costs. Non-HFTs take more liquidity during the periodic spikes, while 
HFTs provide more liquidity. Trading activity affects volatility but not liquidity during turnof-the-month, a low-frequency activity periodicity that supplements our high-frequency 
results. We interpret these results in the context of models of strategic trading. The 
subsecond periodicity provides a rare example of how investors synchronize their trading in 
practice. We show that trading algorithms may behave irrationally and argue that behavioral 
algorithmic trading requires more academic research.

Appendix A: Econometric Models 
Our NASDAQ and Nanex data are panel data of 120 individual stocks for 44 (NASDAQ) 
and 67 trading days (Nanex) with 216,000 observations per day and stock (6 trading hours 
× 60 minutes × 60 seconds × 10 100 ms time bins). Because of the immense size of the data, 
we parallelize the computations by estimating each stock on each day separately (first-stage 
regression) and then averaging these coefficients. This also helps control for stockidiosyncratic and market-wide systematic biases. In addition, we control for possible 
unobserved effects throughout the time of day by employing hour fixed effects for each day 
and stock in the first-stage regression. For this first-stage parallelized regression, we employ 
an ordinary least squares, instrumental variables, or autoregressive model depending on our 
econometric model. 
The only exception to this procedure is the analysis of order imbalances in Section 4, 
Equation (6). Here, we tag each record according to its 100 ms bin occurrence within each 
second and then aggregate the data over 5-minute intervals. The resulting dataset of 
380,1608 observations then becomes the sample for a linear regression model.

stage in case of IV specification for the tables:

Appendix C: Robustness Tests 
Finally, we discuss alternative explanations for the subsecond periodicities. The first 
concern is that high-frequency periodicities are temporary because algorithms learn, 
improve, and become less predictable by means of their software engineers. This concern 
does not affect our conclusions, but it questions the generality of the subsecond periodicity 
test introduced in this study. We posit that the periodicities are quite persistent. One reason 
is that algorithmic trading was already a mature industry by the start of our sample period 
in 2008. The next reason is that we cannot find any direct negative implications for 
algorithms that engage in such cyclical and predictable trading in our data. Most importantly, 
we find strong high-frequency periodicities in independent recent data from the beginning 
of February to the middle of May 2013 in our Nanex sample. Our other results are also quite 
similar in the Nanex sample. This out-of-sample test confirms that the periodicity is not 
driven by a particular sample or time period. 
The second concern is that corporate and macro news may be more likely to be 
released at rounded-times, causing spikes in trading activity and potentially also creating a 
periodicity at rounded-times. As a robustness test, we check our results to ensure they do 
not change when we exclude the first minute of every hour and every half-hour from our 
sample. Also, the bid-ask spread and the price impact are unchanged during the first 100 ms, 
which is inconsistent with higher information asymmetry sparked by news releases. In 
addition to analyzing spikes at every whole second in absolute time (rounded-times), we also 
repeat our tests using one-second periodic trading patterns that do not originate at the whole 
second, using relative periodicities as discussed previously. These tests confirm our main 
results, which rules out that news released at the top of a whole second drive our findings.

Finally, another alternative is that dark pools and internalizers may report orders at 
the beginning of a second. TheNASDAQ dataset explicitly excludes trades by dark pools, but 
there could be trades from other internalizers. If so, then such trades should have no effect 
on quotes. However, most of our results (i.e., quote intensity, volatility) use only quoted prices 
and therefore internal crosses by internalizers are not driving our results. 
The fact that we find very similar results in two independent datasets, reduces the 
chance of our findings being driven by a particular sample period or stock selection.
