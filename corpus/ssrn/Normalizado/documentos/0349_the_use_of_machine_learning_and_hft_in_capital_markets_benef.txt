1. 
Introduction 
 
1.1. Problem statement 
Electronic trading has brought several innovations to capital markets, including a 
global electronic order routing, broad dissemination of quote and trade 
information, and new types of trading systems.1 Considering that regulated 
markets have the primary function of facilitating the interaction of multiple thirdparties buying and selling interests in financial instruments,2 electronic trading 
systems have revolutionized their role in finance. They allow pre and post-trading 
transmissions to be incredibility faster to the point that stock exchanges have 
become essentially a well-structured computer system over which transactions 
are entered, routed, executed and cleared electronically in their order books with 
little or no human intervention.3 
However, alongside the benefits, electronic trading has opened up space to 
several risks, especially in relation to the use of high-frequency trading (“HFT”) 
by investors. This technology consists of algorithms that automate the emission 
of orders in a very short fraction of time, and might be used to ameliorate the 
accuracy of orders, low transaction costs for traders and increase market 
liquidity.4 On the other hand, it might also be applied to commit market 
manipulation, abusive offerings and jeopardize the stability of capital markets.5 
Governments and financial authorities from several jurisdictions tried to provide 
an adequate response to the misuse of HFT, creating regulation and supervisory 
measures to protect their internal markets and investors. Some of these 
measures include using  artificial intelligence solutions (“AI”), such as machine 
learning, to improve market surveillance.6 The problem is that AI can be applied 
to malicious purposes as well. Together with automated trading, it might also 
pose risks to market integrity and perpetuate unlawful practices such as frontrunning and layering/spoofing. In this scenario, this paper will attempt to answer: 
How does the European legislator address the use of such technologies in 
regulated markets? Is there a way to attenuate the risks of these technologies 
while preserving the benefits they bring to the sector?

1.2. Research method 
This paper will study machine learning and HFT applied to the capital markets 
and how the European financial regulation addresses this issue. In order to 
accomplish that, this paper will first take a descriptive approach in Part 2 to 
explain the concepts and functioning of HFT and machine learning technologies 
and how they might be applied in trading venues. Then, Part 3 will study the legal 
aspects involving the use of machine learning and HFT in the capital markets, the 
benefits and downgrades of the innovations they introduced in exchange markets 
and what regulatory responses it has motivated. Afterwards, there will be an 
evaluation of how the European legislator has chosen to address the risks 
inherent to these technologies and what challenges may persist. Finally, there 
will be a conclusive overview of the paper’s findings and the possible answers to 
its research questions.  
The method chosen to perform this research is a systematic analysis of 
institutional statements, legislations, academic publications, and reports from 
authors and organizations specialized in electronic trading, machine learning and 
financial regulation, specifically regarding organized exchange markets. 
2. 
Explaining HFT and machine learning in capital markets 
 
2.1. HFT 
2.1.1. Concept 
There is no consensus about the definition of HTF.7 However, according to the 
European Commission, HFT is a sophisticated type of algorithmic trading, which 
is a model of trading through the use of software where the algorithm determines 
the aspects of the order, such as price and quantity, and most of the time places 
them in the trading facility without human intervention.8 Specifically, HFT’s 
primary characteristic is to provide direct market access to automated order 
management and submission by following pre-designed trading decisions and 
observing market data in real-time.9 
Although HFT is not a strategy itself, it generally follows a modus operandi 
consistent with operating a very high number of orders through rapid order 
cancellation (over milliseconds or even microseconds) and high-speed 
connections (at low latency) in order to make a profit from price changes.10 These

However, some other features might be included depending on the authority

reduce the time between the trading server and the marketplace server).12 
2.1.2. Applicability 
According to the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (“ASIC”), the 
central HFT strategies are statistical arbitrage, electronic liquidity providers 
(“ELPs”) and liquidity detection, as detailed hereafter.13 However, as regulators 
usually lack data that publicly identifies orders and trades from HFTs, there is no 
accurate estimative on the real impact of such HFT strategies in capital markets.14 
Statistical arbitrage attempts to exploit pricing inefficiencies by identifying 
discrepancies between related companies or markets and leveraging them in 
very short periods.15 The ASIC reported that arbitrageurs sell relatively lower 
intrinsic value instruments while simultaneously buying other instruments that 
would react in a very similar way to market changes, and which they deem to 
have a relatively higher intrinsic value for then liquidating their positions.16 The 
profit results from the difference between the individual valuation of the 
instruments at the time the position is opened by arbitrageurs and their influenced 
prices at the time the arbitrageurs’ position is liquidated. 
ELPs involve placing multiple orders in both bid and ask sides of the order book 
at different price ranges in order to induce the cancelling of other orders in a very 
short period and make a profit in the bid-ask spread of the order book.17 This 
strategy is facilitated by maker-taker models, in which the maker component 
receives a rebate to limit order traders and generate liquidity (providing liquidity) 
while the taker component pays the fee charged by the offers promoted by the 
maker (demanding liquidity). The profit comes from the difference between the 
rebate paid by the liquidity demander and the fee charged by the liquidity 
provider.18

For instance, the HFT algorithm places orders to buy 100 shares at EUR 50,00 
and sell 100 shares at EUR 50,01. If all these orders are filled, the algorithm 
would have risked EUR 5000,00 to capture a spread of EUR 1,00. However, if 
the market offers a rebate (paid by takers that need liquidity) of EUR 0,25 for 100 
shares, the algorithm would profit from this rebate on both sides of the book, 
making a final profit of EUR 1,50 and not only EUR 1,00.19 Therefore, the ELPs 
strategy benefits from the investors that demand liquidity in a given market. 
Liquidity detection is considered a predatory strategy where the algorithm sends 
a high number of small orders to gain market information.20 According to SCOPINO, 
such orders operate as a sonar to detect when institutional investors will make 
large trades in futures contracts in order to buy the liquidity in the contract before 
these investors and sell it back to them at higher or lower prices, depending on 
the bid/ask side they are.21 This practice is a form of front running and constitutes 
an abusive practice, as discussed in Section 3 of this paper. 
Therefore, although HFT strategies use a great amount of real-time raw market 
data as a basis to achieve their negotiation goals, they do not analyze this raw 
data to predict future price movements. There is where machine learning comes 
into place.22 
 
2.2. Machine learning 
2.2.1. Concept 
Machine-learning algorithms use statistics in a significant amount of data to find 
patterns.23 The first artificial neural network was established in 1957 by Frank 
Rosenblatt under the denomination of “perceptron”. He developed an 
electromechanical system that recognized similarities from different types of input 
by creating patterns determined by probabilistic and statistical measurements of 
that raw data.24 At the time, these networks had just two layers of neurons, an 
input layer and an output layer; however, this model has been refined to include 
thousands of layers of computational nodes (“neurons”) to analyze data inputs 
and estimate results in the form of a prediction.25

This new technology was called deep learning and was developed by GEOFFREY 
HINTON. His model was based on a high-dimensional vector space, in which each 
neuron’s activity corresponds to a number and each number to a coordinate of 
an enormous mathematical vector.26 Thus, these vectors act as a map of 
neurons’ coordinates, and the learning network finds similarities in comparing 
data that activates the same neurons in this map.27 The more data the network 
compares, the more accurate it becomes, given that the neurons “learn” from all 
previous comparisons they have done. 
 
If this concept is applied to the securities market, a deep learning network would 
be able to estimate patterns from all the information provided by the HFT 
strategies mentioned above, especially by liquidity detection. However, what 
would be the benefits of combining these technologies? More importantly, how 
the European regulatory framework would address them? 
 
2.2.2. Machine Learning and HFT applied in trading models 
HFT allows the reconstruction of the exchange order book given that it captures 
microstructure data detailing order placements, execution, and cancellation. All 
that raw data serve as an input to the machine learning network and, after 
exchanging historically and real-time data at a large scale, the network might be 
able to interpret the market under analysis and even build predictive models.28  
In this section, this paper will describe two applications of this interaction between 
HFT and machine to identify beneficial or malicious characteristics that will be 
discussed under the regulatory perspective in Chapter 3, namely the Optimized 
Trade Execution via Reinforcement Learning and the Predicting Price Movement 
from Order Book State. 
A. 
Optimized Trade Execution via Reinforcement Learning  
This solution aims to reduce agency and brokerage costs in pre-determined 
trading strategies at a particular market, volume and time horizon. Assessing the 
brokerage cost of transactions is essential to determine the profitability of a 
trading strategy, given that HFT entails placing a massive quantity of orders, and 
each order has its own brokerage cost. Therefore, the cost of executing HFT 
might neutralize a strategy that appears to be profitable or even make it 
unprofitable if it is not well assessed at the beginning of its planning.

In order to assess the real profitability of a certain strategy, the Optimized Trade 
Execution algorithm analyzes the microstructure data to reconstruct the order 
book, then makes comparisons among the different variable conditions that would 
result in multiple scenarios (called “states”) related to the brokerage expenditure 
of the desired trading strategy. These variable conditions might be: a) the bid-ask 
spread, b) the bid-ask volume imbalance, c) the recent volume of transactions, 
and d) the immediate market order cost. Here, machine learning is used to 
identify the probabilistic impact of the variable conditions in each given state, then 
mapping the optimal actions for each state to reduce the trading cost.29 
B. 
Predicting Price Movement from Order Book State  
This mechanism is used to identify predictive models of trading strategies that, 
when combined, would result in a profitable spread and define the adequate 
moment to execute such strategies in the least costly way. In this model, the 
algorithm needs to identify the direction movement of the market by analyzing 
variants, some examples are: a) price, b) the bid-ask spread, c) the variation of 
bid-ask average price related to their inverse volume, d) the bid-ask volume 
imbalance, e) the recent volume of transactions, and f) the trade sign of where 
other investors have crossed the spread in recent executions. The machine 
learning method applied is similar to the Optimized Trade Execution model. 
However, it has additional variables to consider and returns scenarios of 
profitable trading opportunities.30  
Furthermore, a study performed by FLETCHER showed that machine learning 
techniques could make compelling predictions of price movement in currencies 
into the future with an accuracy ranging from 90% in 5 seconds to 53% in 200 
seconds. Since HFT operates at milliseconds, such a time range would easily 
allow profitable trading margins to the investor.31 
2.3. Preliminary Conclusions 
This section studied two applications of machine learning and HFT in organized 
securities markets. In summary, the first application (optimized trade execution) 
targets to reduce costs to a pre-determined trade strategy, while the second 
(predicting price movement) attempts to create profitable trading strategies 
considering market fluctuation and brokerage costs. According to KEARNS & 
NEVMYVAKA, these methods concluded that “learning consistently produces 
policies that are profitable on the test set, and that those policies are broadly 
similar across stocks”. Moreover, the authors considered these technologies 
“powerful and scalable” and even “necessary in the presence of microstructure

3. Legal aspects of HFT and machine learning in capital markets 
This part will discuss the negative side of HFT and machine learning in capital 
markets, the regulatory responses financial authorities have taken to repress it, 
and the recent considerations for this subject in the European Union. 
3.1. Market disruption incidents and systemic risk  
3.1.1. Market Manipulation 
The US “Flash Crash” on 6 May 2010 is one of the most famous incidents of 
market disruption caused by HFT. On this occasion, an automated sell order in 
E-mini S&P 500 stock index futures (“Sell Algorithm”) caused the biggest decline 
ever in the Dow Jones Industrial Average. More than 300 securities were affected 
and, in less than 20 minutes, 20.000 transactions were executed under a 
devaluation of 40% in comparison to the start of the period. 34 Then, it all returned 
to normality after a few minutes.  
According to a joint report of the US Securities and Exchange Commission 
(“SEC”) and the US Commodities and Future Trading Commission (“CFTC”), 
HFTs took part in the Flash Crash by absorbing the initial batch of orders 
submitted by the Sell Algorithm and negotiating thousands of E-mini contracts 
under the motivation of that first order that ended up causing the price changes 
by a chain reaction.35

In the event of the Flash Crash, the US SEC considered the big Sell Algorithm as 
a form of spoofing “to create the appearance of substantial false supply and 
demand and to induce other market participants to trade E-minis at prices, 
quantities, and/or times that would not otherwise have been traded”.36 Following 
the definition set by the European Commission, spoofing involves placing 
apparent orders to create a misleading impression of the stock price or liquidity, 
while layering involves placing several smaller orders to improve the price of a 
trade in the opposite direction of the order book. Both strategies cancel the false 
orders that influenced the market in the direction of the desired price, leaving only 
profitable orders to execution.37  
Although the prices have reverted to normality at the occasion, this case opened 
up the discussion on the scalability of chain reactions geared by HFTs and how 
it would affect the efficient market hypothesis on price formation and, ultimately, 
the confidence in the capital markets. 
3.1.2. Market Abuse 
In 2014, LEWIS published an article (known as “Flash Boys”) that described HFTs 
as tools that read the information provided by investors in slower electronic 
trading platforms and take advantage of this knowledge to execute orders before 
those investors in the US securities market (liquidity detection strategy).38 The 
article narrated the findings of Brad Katsuyama, an individual investor, that 
exposed how HFT took advantage of the fragmentation of the US securities 
market to front-run other investors. In the US, large offers end up being layered 
(given their scale) and executed in different stock exchanges throughout the 
market. However, Katsuyama noticed that after the first layer of the offer was 
executed, the price of that contract worsened in other stock exchanges, obliging 
the execution of the rest of the batch at a lower spread. That happened because 
of the interference of HFTs since they foresaw the information of the large batch 
and executed their order beforehand (in other words, they commit front 
running).39 
In the European Union, front running is a prohibited practice classified as a form 
of inside information that comprises the execution of orders “relating to the client’s 
pending orders in financial instruments” under the Regulation (EU) no. 596/2014 
of 16 April 2014 on market abuse (“Market Abuse Regulation”)40. The next section 
will bring more details about the forbiddance of front running in the EU.

3.1.3. The Role of Machine Learning in Market Disruption 
The usage of machine learning techniques to predict price movements might not 
be an abusive practice by itself; however, it could easily become an instrument 
to refine and camouflage market manipulation strategies such as spoofing and 
layering.  
In this scenario, an investor could potentially create a refined spoofing strategy, 
for instance, based on machine learning predictions and implement it in a 
particular market. As discussed in the Flash Crash case, HFTs have the potential 
to trigger chain reactions when combined with other trading strategies in search 
of ELPs and statistical arbitrage. It means that a manipulative strategy might 
succeed in one market but also affect other markets that share some similarity of 
type, product or strategy, which would make this problem even worse. 
Additionally, given the difficulties in identifying what orders are generated by HFT 
in the order book, well-refined market manipulation practices might manage to be 
undetected by the competent authorities. 
As reported by the Financial Stability Board (“FSB”), the machine learning 
algorithms create “black-boxes” that make it difficult for human users at financial 
institutions and regulators to understand how their decision-making process for 
their trading and investing has been formulated.42 In other words, it may be 
problematic to identify the abusive intention of the owner of the algorithm. 
Therefore, the use of machine learning for trading strategies reduces market 
transparency, and there is no definitive control on how these algorithms might be 
applied to malicious and unlawful purposes. 
On the other hand, the FSB highlighted that machine learning might also be used 
for market surveillance by automating macroprudential analysis and data quality 
assurance. In such cases, machine learning could automatically identify 
anomalies in trade repositories while tackling data quality issues, identifying data

3.2. The EU regulatory framework  
All these possibilities of market disruption create the necessity to increase the 
scrutiny from regulators and securities markets authorities in addressing HFT and 
machine learning algorithms. This section will discuss the present characteristics 
of this topic in the European Union regarding the current prohibitions and 
requirements posed by the regime and what challenges regulators still need to 
address.

prohibition against manipulative and deceptive devices.45 The new rule followed 
the provisions set by Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act46 and was the 
basis for the SEC Rule 10b-5 on fraudulent practices such as front running and

3.2.2. The present framework in the EU framework 
Currently, insider dealing and market manipulation are prohibited practices under 
Articles 14 and 15 of the Market Abuse Regulation, respectively.50 This provision 
addresses specifically natural and legal persons charged with the execution of 
orders,51 which leaves other categories of investors for the scope of the MiFID52 
and the Directive 2014/91/EU on the coordination of laws, regulations and 
administrative provisions relating to undertakings for collective investment in 
transferable securities (“UCITS”).53  
The UCITS provides rules to avoid conflict of interest of legal entities in relation 
to their depositary or investment management activities.54 That would include the 
cases of front running committed by investment firms vis-à-vis their own clients.

After almost ten years since the Flash Crash, financial authorities still struggle to 
Regulate HFT and machine learning. In December 2020, the European Securities 
and Markets Authority (“ESMA”) published a consultation paper with suggestions 
to further develop the European regulatory framework on this matter. The 
proposals include: (a) changes on the market-making activity, to make it more 
predictable and subject to a more stringent regulatory framework, (b) the 
establishment of speedbumps in equity markets to decrease latency arbitrage, 
and (c) making public feeds more transparent, consistent and predictable.57 
 
All these provisions and suggestions might improve the prudential enforcement 
on macro and micro levels of the European capital markets and address some of 
the discussed concerns related to algorithmic trading. However, they cannot 
guarantee that the competent authorities will adequately address future market 
disruption attempts. In 2021, the European Banking Institute (“EBI”) recognized 
that, thanks to the versatility provided by machine learning, autonomous trading 
could present unexpected capabilities beyond experts can reasonably expect and 
ultimately subvert the existing prohibitions of market abuse.58 
 
Therefore, despite all the regulatory efforts taken in the past decade, the lack of 
transparency inherent to algorithm trading seems to be an ongoing liability for 
investors and regulators of capital markets whose solution is not yet visible at the 
horizon of market participants. 
4. Conclusion 
The findings presented in this research showed that algorithm trading featuring 
machine learning and HFT strategies are permanently evolving, and there is no 
accurate estimative on how they affect exchange markets. The innovative 
character that surrounds these instruments obliges financial authorities and 
regulators to address market disruption geared by their usage cases palliatively,
