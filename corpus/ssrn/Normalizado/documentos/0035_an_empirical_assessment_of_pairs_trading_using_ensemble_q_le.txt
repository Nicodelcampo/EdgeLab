Abstract
This study considers the application of Walk-Forward Optimization
(WFO) to evaluate the real-world viability of a cointegration-based pairs
trading strategy in the Forex market.
In previous research, we observed that pairs trading benefited from
the use of a cointegration-based filter such that trades on some given
pair would be executed in the test window only when cointegration was
observed in the training window. However, such improved performance
relied on an arbitrary set of fixed parameters that produced decent results
over the entire 17-year period.
In this study, we apply WFO to dynamically recalibrate strategy parameters over time to enable adaptation to evolving market conditions.
Results show that the use of WFO not only did not worsen historical performance, but also reduced maximum drawdowns, thus improving riskadjusted performance with respect to using the fixed parameter set.

Introduction
Pairs trading, a strategy that involves identifying two correlated assets and
exploiting temporary price divergences between them, has proven effective in
different markets, such as equities and commodities, where asset pairs often
display measurable correlations and mispricing opportunities Gatev et al. [7],
Liu et al. [13].

Simple pairs trading relies on short-term price correlations, assuming that asset prices will revert to their historical mean, but it lacks robustness in accounting for long-term trends or relationships. In contrast, cointegration-based pairs
trading identifies pairs with stable long-term equilibrium relationships, ensuring mean reversion even in the presence of non-stationary financial time series,
offering a more reliable framework for pairs trading Gatev et al. [7], Alexander
[6]. Thus, cointegration techniques help identify pairs that, despite short-term
price divergence in price fluctuations, maintain a consistent long-term relationship. This approach has proved to enhance the reliability of trading signals by
filtering out false signals, leading to improved risk-adjusted performance Jiang
and Xu [10].
Our previous research Lemishko et al. [18] explored the application of cointegration in pairs trading within the Forex market. We investigated the profitability of incorporating the cointegration filter by comparing the performance of a
simple pairs trading strategy with a cointegration-based approach. This study
focused on pairs that included combinations of seven most liquid currencies,
and we applied both strategies to a 17-year dataset of historical daily adjusted
closing prices. Our findings revealed that the cointegration-based pairs trading
strategy outperformed the simpler mean reversion strategy, demonstrating superior risk-adjusted performance, as indicated by higher Sharpe, Sortino, and
Calmar ratios.
Building upon these results, this study extends our previous work by incorporating Walk-Forward Optimization (WFO) to further gauge the real-world
viability of the cointegration-based pairs trading strategy. WFO involves dynamically recalibrating model parameters through a series of rolling time windows, providing a more adaptive approach to changing market conditions. This
methodology is expected to improve the robustness and reliability of the cointegrationbased strategy over time, offering more timely and responsive trading decisions.
The aim of this study is to evaluate whether Walk-Forward Optimization
can provide consistent and stable performance for the cointegration-based pairs
trading strategy when applied to the Forex market. By applying this technique
to the same 17-year dataset, we aim to investigate whether ongoing adjustments
to the strategy parameters enable maintaining its performance in real-time trading scenarios.

Literature review
The success of trading strategies is closely tied to the efficiency of the markets in
which they operate. As a result, a thorough understanding of financial market
efficiency is essential for investors aiming to design and implement effective
trading strategies. [5] describes an informationally efficient market as one where
all participants have swift access to relevant information, and this information is
fully incorporated into asset prices. In such markets, security prices accurately
represent their true intrinsic value.
Backtesting is essential for developing systematic investment strategies, but
2

it has many pitfalls that can lead to false findings and strategies that do not
work in real markets. Backtesting provides valuable insights into a strategy’s
performance in past market conditions. However, it is inherently limited in its
ability to predict future market behavior. Traders who have used backtesting
understand that historical performance is not always a reliable indicator of future outcomes, as market dynamics can vary significantly over time. Bailey et al.
[12] explore backtest overfitting, where a strategy optimized on historical data
captures noise rather than genuine patterns, leading to poor performance on new
datasets. Using a simulator with random walk time series, they demonstrate
how ”optimal” strategies derived in-sample often fail out-of-sample, highlighting
the need for rigorous validation techniques in financial modeling. In addition,
the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission announced that its examination
of hedge funds uncovered a number of issues that are examples of “selection
bias”. According to a Wall Street Journal report, the SEC “uncovered marketing and advertising issues, with some firms potentially misleading clients on
past performance by ‘cherry picking’ their results from fund to fund” Ackerman
[11].
To address this issue, it is essential to rigorously evaluate trading strategies
before deploying them. This involves using advanced techniques to ensure that
the strategy is not merely overfitted to historical data but can also perform
reliably in real-world market conditions. One such method for robust evaluation is Walk-Forward Optimization (WFO), which is widely used in financial
forecasting to determine the most effective parameters for a trading strategy
Kirkpatrick and Dahlquist [9].
Walk-Forward Optimization provides critical
insights into the robustness and adaptability of a strategy. By iteratively retraining models or recalibrating indicator parameters on updated market data,
this approach tests how effectively a strategy adjusts to evolving market conditions, thereby improving its reliability in real-world applications.
By utilizing only the information available at each historical point, it mitigates look-ahead bias and provides a realistic simulation of real-time strategy performance. Additionally, this method helps identify whether a strategy’s
profitability and stability are sustainable over time or prone to deterioration.
In practice, Walk-Forward Optimization involves splitting the dataset into sequential periods. For each period, the strategy is optimized on the preceding
data (the ”training” period) and then evaluated on the subsequent data (the
”testing” period).
This rolling approach ensures continuous adaptation and
validation of the strategy’s parameters in line with evolving market conditions.
Walk-Forward Analysis (WFA) was created by Pardo [4] and expanded in
the second edition Pardo [8]. The author emphasizes the importance of testing multiple parameter sets using historical simulations across various market
conditions and timeframes. This iterative optimization process culminates in
Walk-Forward Analysis, a robust methodology for assessing a strategy’s statistical significance and long-term viability. WFA enables traders to determine
whether a strategy can maintain its effectiveness on unseen, out-of-sample data,
enhancing its adaptability in dynamic market environments.
For instance, Carta et al. [15] employed a Walk-Forward Optimization strat3

egy, where they divided the in-sample (IS) time series into several segments,
each consisting of training and testing sets, to optimize ensemble hyperparameters and intrinsic parameters, using performance on the testing data to identify
the best parameters for the strategy across different segments. This approach
is part of a broader, fully automated, and optimized ensemble strategy introduced in their work, which combines optimized feature selection with a set of
classifiers whose intrinsic and hyper-parameters are learned specifically for each
market under consideration. The strategy adapts to the chosen market, addressing challenges such as data irregularity and high unpredictability, and has
been demonstrated to outperform both the Buy and Hold baseline and several
state-of-the-art methods in real-world futures market experiments.
Moreover Arian et al. [17] highlight Walk-Forward Optimization as a traditional method for financial model validation but notes its limitations, including
sensitivity to window size, poor handling of temporal dependencies, and vulnerability to non-stationarity in financial data. It also struggles with false discovery
prevention and overfitting in dynamic market conditions.
While WFO preserves chronological data order, its rigidity and computational intensity make
it less robust compared to advanced methods like Combinatorial Purged CrossValidation (CPCV), which better address biases, adapt to regime shifts, and
offer more reliable performance assessments through diverse train-test splits.

3.2
Returns of Individual Currency Pairs generated by
the Cointegration-Based Pairs Trading Strategy
In our previous study Lemishko et al. [18], we proposed a cointegration-based
pairs trading strategy applied to 42 unique currency pair combinations derived
from 7 selected currencies. To implement the strategy, we used a training window of 257 days (approximately one year) and a testing window of 21 days
(roughly one month). The strategy involved an iterative procedure over 17years period of study to ensure consistent evaluation: after each testing period,
a new training and testing cycle was initiated.
During each training period, we performed cointegration analysis on the
currency pairs using the Engle-Granger test to identify pairs with a long-term
statistical relationship. The first step of the Engle-Granger test involves conducting the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test on each of the two time series
variables to determine whether each variable is non-stationary. If both variables
in the pair were individually non-stationary, we proceeded to the second step,
which involved performing a regression analysis where one variable is regressed
on the other. The regression equation takes the form:
yt = α + β · xt + ϵt
where yt is the dependent variable, xt is the independent variable, α is the
intercept, β is the slope coefficient, and ϵt is the residual term. The residuals,
ˆϵt = ˆyt −ˆβ · xt −ˆα, represent the deviation of the pair from the long-term
equilibrium.
The second step of the Engle-Granger test is to perform an ADF test on the
residuals of the regression. If the residuals are found to be non-stationary, it
implies that there is no evidence of cointegration.
In simple terms, if both yt and xt are non-stationary, but the residuals ˆϵt
are stationary, then yt and xt are said to be cointegrated. This implies that,
despite the individual trends in the asset prices, a stable long-term relationship
exists between them.
The residuals, ˆϵt, are considered as the so-called synthetic spread, which captures the deviation of the two asset prices from their
equilibrium relationship. The slope coefficient ˆβ is an important measure, as it
represents the sensitivity of yt to changes in xt, or in other words, how much yt
responds to fluctuations in xt.
Specifically, the synthetic spread, ˆϵt, was calculated as the difference between
the log prices of the paired currencies, adjusted by the regression coefficient β:
ˆϵt = log(priceofcurrency1) −β · log(priceofcurrency2),
where ˆϵt represents the synthetic spread.

difference between the current spread value and the mean of the synthetic spread

observed during the training period:

where µ(ˆϵt) and σ(ˆϵt) are the mean and standard deviation of the synthetic
spread during the training period. A threshold for the z-score was set at ±1,
and trading signals were generated when the z-score exceeded this threshold.
Specifically, a long position was taken when the z-score fell below the negative
threshold, indicating a potential buy opportunity, and a short position was
taken when the z-score exceeded the positive threshold, signaling a potential
sell opportunity.
To simulate real-world trading conditions, a two-day lag was applied between
the generation of the signal and the execution of the trade. This means that if a
trading signal was generated at time t, the trade would be executed at time t+2,
allowing for a more realistic simulation of the time required to act on the signal
and account for practical constraints such as order execution delays or market
frictions. The return for the strategy on any given day was then calculated as
the product of the signal at t −2 and the price change at time t, as shown in
the formula:
StrategyReturn(t) = Signal(t −2) × ∆Price(t).
This approach ensures that the strategy accounts for the inherent delays and
complexities involved in real trading environments.
This process was repeated across all testing periods, generating a time series
of returns for each of the 42 currency pairs.
3.3
Walk-Forward Optimization
In this study, we propose a similar approach but, instead of selecting the pairs
yielding ex post a positive Sharpe ratio over the entire 17-year period of study,
we dynamically evaluate the Sharpe ratio of the strategy for each pair on a walkforward basis. Specifically, we compute the rolling Sharpe ratio for each pair
over a rolling training window (t−δtrain, t), and execute trades on a rolling test
window (t, t+δtest) only when the Sharpe ratio for a given pair is positive in the
corresponding training window (t−δtrain, t). This approach more closely mirrors
the real-world scenario faced by practitioners, who need to make decisions at
time t′ based on information available at time t, where t < t′.
In this study, we implemented Walk-Forward Optimization using various
rolling training windows δtrain of 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months
to evaluate the performance of an optimized cointegration-based pairs trading
strategy. At each window, we repeated the procedure described below.

First, we converted the optimization windows from months into days, taking
into account that there are approximately 21 days in one month of trading in
the Forex market.
To assess the risk-adjusted returns of each currency pair, we computed the
rolling Sharpe ratio over a window of N days. The rolling Sharpe ratio is calculated as:
S = Rmean(N) × 257

257
where Rmean(N) represents the rolling mean of returns over the N-days
window, and Rstd(N) represents the rolling standard deviation. The factor of
257 corresponds to the average number of trading days in a year in the Forex
market. This calculation provides a dynamic view of each pair’s risk-adjusted
performance, updating over time.
Next, we define a mask to filter out the currency pairs with a positive rolling
Sharpe ratio. The mask is created by selecting those pairs where the rolling
Sharpe ratio exceeds zero, with a shift applied by one day to avoid look-ahead
bias. This mask effectively selects only the pairs that have shown positive performance in the immediate past in terms of their risk-adjusted returns, assuming
the hypothesis that they will continue to perform positively in the near future.
This study aims to test whether this hypothesis holds. For each day in the
dataset, we count the number of currency pairs that meet the positive Sharpe
ratio threshold. This is done by summing the ‘True‘ values across the mask.
This step ensures that we have an accurate count of the pairs selected for each
rolling window.
Finally, the aggregate returns of the portfolio are calculated by averaging
the returns of the selected pairs at each point in time. This calculation normalizes the returns by the number of selected pairs, ensuring that the portfolio
performance reflects the varying number of pairs included in each period.
By applying this Walk-Forward Optimization methodology, we ensure that
the strategy is adaptive and responsive to changing market conditions, with a
robust performance evaluation across different time windows.
In order to compare the performance of the walk-forward optimized cointegrationbased strategy with dynamic pair selection over time and the strategy applied
to the entire study period (we will simply refer to it as the fixed pair selection
strategy), we first excluded the initial period used in optimization from the time
series of returns obtained for the fixed pair selection strategy applied to the entire study period, ensuring that both return series start and end at the same
dates. We then adjusted their leverage to achieve an arbitrary target annualized
return of 10% for both. Specifically, we applied a leverage factor of 21.71 to the
dynamic pair selection strategy and 26.40 to the fixed pair selection strategy.
This adjustment ensures that both strategies deliver the same arbitrary target
return of 10%, enabling a direct comparison of their respective risk given the
same unit of return.

3.4
Evaluation
To evaluate whether Walk-Forward Optimization can provide consistent and

Impact of Optimization Window on Trading Strategy

these windows may not achieve the highest Sharpe or Sortino ratios, they provide steadier annualized returns, lower volatility, and more manageable drawdowns. The robustness of these windows lies in their relatively lower sensitivity
to small changes in the window size, which makes the resulting strategy more
reliable and adaptable to different market environments. These longer windows
achieve a balance between performance and stability, avoiding the pitfalls of
overfitting while maintaining competitive results.

Conclusions
This study builds upon our previous work by incorporating Walk-Forward Optimization (WFO) to enhance the fixed pair selection (ex post) cointegrationbased pairs trading strategy’s real-world viability through dynamic pair selection (walk-forward). WFO recalibrates model parameters through rolling time
windows, enabling the strategy to adapt to changing market conditions. Using
a 17-year Forex dataset, this study evaluates whether WFO can deliver consistent and stable performance by maintaining robustness, thereby supporting
more timely and responsive trading decisions.
We analyzed the effects of varying optimization windows and found that
medium-to-long optimization windows (24 to 60 months) are less sensitive to
parameter changes, delivering more consistent performance.
To illustrate the comparison between fixed pair selection (ex post) and dynamic pair selection (walk-forward), we used a 36-month optimization window
and scaled both strategies to the same arbitrarily chosen annualized target return of 10%. The analysis of the results revealed that - while the annualized
returns and overall volatility of the two strategies are nearly identical - their
behavior differs in terms of extreme risk and drawdown.
The Sharpe ratios
are similar, indicating comparable risk-adjusted returns overall, but dynamic
pair selection (walk-forward) exhibits significant improvements in its maximum
drawdown and - consequently - Calmar ratio.
The improved Calmar ratio
highlights better risk-adjusted returns relative to drawdowns, and the significantly reduced maximum drawdown.
These improvements suggest that the
optimization process effectively reduces the magnitude and duration of drawdowns, thereby enhancing the stability and reliability of the strategy.
By aligning the strategies to achieve the same returns, it becomes clear
that dynamic pair selection through walk-forward optimization not only did
not worsen risk-adjusted performance, but also mitigated tail risk as well as
drawdowns.
The relative consistency in the performance of the fixed pair selection (ex
post) versus dynamic pair selection (walk-forward) observed in this study shows
that a cointegration-based pairs trading strategy applied to foreign exchange
markets could be well-suited for real-world trading scenarios.
Future research could extend the cointegration-based pairs trading strategy
to other asset classes, such as equities, commodities, and cryptocurrencies, to assess its robustness across different markets with distinct dynamics and volatility
patterns. In addition, investigating the effects of market regimes and volatility
shifts on the performance of the strategy would provide further insights into its
stability and effectiveness across various market environments.
