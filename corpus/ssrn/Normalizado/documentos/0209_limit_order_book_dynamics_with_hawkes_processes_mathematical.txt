Limit Order Book Dynamics with Hawkes Processes:
Mathematical Foundations, Microstructure Modeling,
and Simulation
Maheep Sharma
December 10, 2025
Abstract
We study limit order book (LOB) dynamics using linear and multivariate Hawkes
processes. Hawkes models capture empirically observed clustering of orders, self- and
cross-excitation of buy/sell flows, and volatility bursts at high frequency. We provide a
self-contained mathematical treatment of Hawkes processes, including intensity-based
definitions, branching representations, stability conditions, and explicit second-order
structure for exponential kernels. We then introduce Hawkes-based models for market
order arrivals and limit order book queues, and propose a simple order-flow-driven
price model. Python code for simulation and basic maximum likelihood estimation
is included. This paper is intended as a mathematically oriented introduction that
bridges theory, microstructure modeling, and numerical experimentation for aspiring
quantitative researchers.
1
Introduction
Modern electronic markets are organized as limit order books (LOBs), where buy and sell
limit orders queue at discrete price levels. Market orders, cancellations, and new limit orders
1

drive a complex sequence of events at millisecond or microsecond resolution.
Empirically, high-frequency order flow exhibits:
• clustering of trades and quote updates,
• self-excitation (an order arrival increases short-term probability of more orders),
• cross-excitation between buy and sell flows,
• long memory in trade sign and volatility.
Classical models based on Poisson processes do not account for such temporal dependence. Linear Hawkes processes provide a natural extension: the intensity of future events
depends linearly on past events via a kernel.
The goals of this paper are:
1. to present a rigorous yet accessible introduction to Hawkes processes;
2. to connect Hawkes theory to LOB and microstructure modeling;
3. to provide simulation and basic calibration tools in Python.
We assume familiarity with basic probability and stochastic processes at the level of a
first graduate course.
2
Brief Literature Overview
Hawkes processes were introduced by Hawkes (1971) to model self-exciting point processes
in contexts such as seismology and epidemiology. The branching (cluster) representation of
Hawkes processes, due to Hawkes and Oakes (1974), plays a central role in their probabilistic
analysis.
In finance, Hawkes processes have been applied to:
• model arrival times of market orders and trades,
2

• capture clustering of volatility and market activity,
• study price impact and order-flow imbalance.
Bacry, Mastromatteo, and Muzy (2015) survey many applications of Hawkes models to
high-frequency finance and market microstructure. Other important contributions include
Embrechts et al. on multivariate Hawkes processes and Bouchaud et al. on long-memory in
order-flow and impact.
Our contribution is pedagogical: we assemble core mathematical results, connect them
explicitly to LOB modeling, and provide the simulation and basic estimation code that an
independent researcher can directly run and modify.

Near-Critical Hawkes Processes and Rough Limits
One of the main motivations for using Hawkes processes in finance is their ability to generate
long-range dependence and volatility clustering when operated in a near-critical regime. In
this section we describe the basic intuition and a simplified version of scaling limits that lead
to rough or fractional-like behavior.

5.3
Power-Law Kernels and Fractional Limits
Long memory arises when the kernel g decays slowly, e.g. like a power law:
g(t) ∼c t−(1+γ),
t →∞,
10

for some γ ∈(0, 1). In this case, the impact of distant past events decays slowly, and clusters
spread over long time scales.
Under suitable conditions (beyond our scope to prove rigorously here), one can show that
for an appropriate choice of scaling exponent a, the centered, rescaled process converges in
distribution to a process with fractional or rough characteristics. Typical limit objects are:
• fractional Brownian motion,
• fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes,
• rough volatility processes driven by fractional noise.
5.4
Qualitative Interpretation for Markets
From a financial perspective, the near-critical Hawkes regime captures the idea that most
trades are triggered by past trades rather than by external news. A large market order can
initiate a long cascade of child events (copycat trades, liquidity replenishment, cancellations),
which spreads across multiple time scales. This leads to:
• persistent order-flow imbalance,
• clustering of volatility,
• slowly decaying autocorrelation in squared returns.
In empirical calibration, one often finds branching ratios ˆη very close to one (e.g. 0.9–
0.99), and kernels best approximated by sums of many exponentials, mimicking power-law
behavior. This is consistent with the near-critical, multiscale picture.

5.6
Implications for LOB Modeling
In the context of LOB modeling, near-critical multiscale Hawkes processes imply that:
• order arrivals at the best quotes have long-range dependence,
• queue sizes exhibit periods of slow drift punctuated by rapid bursts,
• midprice dynamics driven by such order-flow will inherit roughness and volatility clustering.
Even a simplified LOB model driven by multiscale Hawkes intensities can reproduce qualitatively realistic patterns seen in real high-frequency data.

6.1
Ogata Thinning Algorithm for Univariate Hawkes
We simulate a univariate Hawkes process with exponential kernel using Ogata’s thinning
method.
Listing 1: Ogata thinning for univariate Hawkes
import numpy as np
def simulate_hawkes(mu, alpha, beta, T):
"""
Simulate a univariate Hawkes process with exponential kernel
phi(t) = alpha * exp(-beta * t) on [0, T].
Returns
-------
events : np.ndarray
Sorted array of event times.
"""
t = 0.0
events = []
lambda_t = mu
while t < T:
# Upper bound on future intensity
lambda_bar = lambda_t + alpha
# Propose next event via exponential( lambda_bar )
u = np.random.random()
w = -np.log(u) / lambda_bar
t = t + w
if t >= T:

break
# Update intensity at proposed time

dt = t - np.array(events)

# Accept or reject

def autocorrelation(x, max_lag):
x = np.asarray(x)
x = x - x.mean()
n = len(x)
ac = np.zeros(max_lag+1)
var = np.dot(x, x) / n
for lag in range(max_lag+1):
if lag == 0:
ac[lag] = 1.0
else:
ac[lag] = np.dot(x[:-lag], x[lag:]) / ((n-lag) * var)

events = np.asarray(events)

n = len(events)

return -mu * T
lambdas = np.zeros(n)
for k in range(n):

past = events[:k]
kernel_sum = 0.0

def autocorrelation(x, max_lag):
x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
x = x - x.mean()
n = len(x)
ac = np.zeros(max_lag+1)
var = np.dot(x, x) / n
for lag in range(max_lag+1):
if lag == 0:
ac[lag] = 1.0
else:
ac[lag] = np.dot(x[:-lag], x[lag:]) / ((n-lag) * var)
return ac

Conclusion
Hawkes processes provide a flexible and mathematically tractable framework for modeling
high-frequency order flow and limit order book dynamics. Their branching representation
and stability conditions give clear intuition: markets with near-critical excitation ratios are
highly endogenous and exhibit clustered volatility. When combined with simple queue and
price dynamics, Hawkes models can be used to simulate realistic LOB environments for
research and model testing.
Future directions include:
• nonparametric estimation of kernels from LOB data,
• multiscale kernels approximating power-law decay,
• rough volatility limits of nearly critical Hawkes processes,
• integration with optimal execution and market-making strategies.
The code examples in this paper provide a starting point for implementing and experimenting with Hawkes-based microstructure models.
