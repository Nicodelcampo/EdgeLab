A Comparative Study of Active Algorithmic Trading and 
Passive Investing 
Kris Kraack, College of Computing, Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, GA, USA. 
Abstract — This study compares an active algorithmic trading strategy, a machine learning model 
comprising both quantitative and sentiment components, with a traditional passive buy-and-hold 
investment strategy through simulated trading using historical stock data under real-world market 
conditions. The findings demonstrate that the algorithmic strategy outperforms passive investment 
consistently on a set of benchmark stocks in different markets. 
Keywords —Algorithmic Trading, Active Trading, Trading Agents, Quantitative Finance, Trading 
Strategies, Investment Strategies, Machine Learning, Passive Investing, Risk-adjusted Portfolio 
Management. 
1. Introduction 
In algorithmic trading, machine learning techniques are leveraged to make active trading 
decisions (Grudniewicz, & Ślepaczuk, 2023). Hedge funds, such as Renaissance Technologies' 
Medallion Fund have demonstrated that algorithmic strategies can achieve sustained high returns 
(Zuckerman, 2019). This study evaluates a machine learning-based algorithmic trading strategy, and 
compares its performance to a passive buy-and-hold investment strategy on the following benchmarks: 
the S&P 500 Index (SPY), Apple (AAPL), Coca-Cola (KO), and JPMorgan Chase & Co. (JPM). 
Trading simulations using historical stock market data and real-world market conditions are conducted 
to evaluate portfolio performance in terms of returns and volatility. The objective is to provide insight 
into the potential for an active algorithmic trading strategy's potential to consistently outperform a buyand-hold strategy in terms of both returns and risk-adjusted performance. The results will contribute to 
the conversation on the theoretical effectiveness of machine learning-based agents in stock trading. 
Hypothesis. An algorithmic machine learning–based trading strategy, integrating quantitative 
and sentiment analysis, can consistently outperform a passive buy-and-hold investing strategy across 
benchmark stocks in different market sectors, while also achieving better risk-adjusted returns. 
2. Model Architecture  
The model designed for this study is a fusion model architecture that combines quantitative and 
sentiment-based components. The quantitative component consists of several models that contribute 
through a voting mechanism to generate trading signals. The sentiment-based component uses natural 
language processing techniques, based on previous work (Kraack, 2024), as a conceptual foundation for 
analyzing textual data to capture market sentiment. These components work synergistically to generate 
trading decisions. Furthermore, the model adapts over time by learning from prior trades by storing and 
retrieving key-trades in a knowledge base in memory.  
3. Experiment Design 
The trading simulation initializes two portfolios with a bankroll equivalent to the price of one 
share of the chosen benchmark. The passive strategy immediately purchases the stock at market open 
and holds it until the simulation concludes. The algorithmic strategy either buys, sells, or holds the stock 
based on its predictions. The simulation exclusively trades one share per transaction, even if cumulative 
returns would permit buying more or less. This is to maintain consistency and comparability while 

avoiding potential compounding that could skew the returns. A broker commission of at least three 
euros or 0.10% per trade is applied to mimic real-world transaction costs.  
To facilitate comparison across different sectors, the following benchmarks are included: the 
S&P 500 (Index), Apple (Information Technology), Coca-Cola (Consumer Staples), and JPMorgan 
Chase & Co. (Financials). The training and prediction process follows a walk-forward validation 
approach. Initially, the model is trained using ten years of historical stock market data, with greater 
weight applied to more recent data. The model continuously predicts the next day’s trading action (buy, 
sell, or hold) and is retrained daily while retaining the ten-year rolling window. This cycle repeats to 
generate daily predictions throughout the chosen backtesting period.  
5. Experiment Result 
Table 1 provides a classification report showing the predictive accuracy of the model for each 
benchmark stock. The model does not employ walk-forward validation, daily retraining, or a knowledge 
base. Instead, the model was trained using consecutive historical stock data from 2012 to 2022, with 
20% of the data reserved for validation. At first glance, the results demonstrate good prediction 
capability, with an overall prediction rate of approximately 81%. However, the 19% error rate is 
significant and could lead to substantial losses or missed opportunities. These results highlight the 
importance of retraining and evaluating the model using walk-forward validation, as this approach more 
accurately captures the temporal dependencies of sequential historical stock data.

5.1. Portfolio Comparison 
The performance of the algorithmic trading model is evaluated against the passive approach by 
comparing the cumulative returns over time for each benchmark portfolio. Comparisons are made 
annually from 2020 to 2024, as well as for the first half of 2025. Figures 1, 2, 3, and 4 show the 
cumulative return comparisons for each benchmark portfolio with vertical green and red lines indicating 
buy and sell transactions, respectively. For each benchmark comparison, the algorithmic strategy 
consistently outperforms the passive investment. The passive strategy only performed better than the 
algorithmic strategy for JPMorgan Chase & Co. (JPM) in the first quarter of 2024 and 2025. Otherwise, 
the algorithmic approach provided better returns. Notably, periods of uncertainty with high volatility 
often lead to successful trading opportunities for the algorithmic approach.

Conclusion 
The experiment concluded that the active algorithmic trading strategy consistently achieves 
higher portfolio  returns than a passive investment approach across all benchmark stocks. It also 
demonstrated higher Sharpe ratio values across all benchmarks, indicating better risk-adjusted returns. 
Therefore, the hypothesis is confirmed. The model effectively captured market dynamics across 
different sectors, demonstrating its robust predictive capability under realistic simulated trading 
constraints. 
The fixed position size of one share per trade was deliberately chosen to eliminate compounding 
to ensure comparability of the strategies across benchmarks. The experiments conducted only simulated 
one year at a time without reinvesting earnings. Incorporating compounding would potentially provide 
further support for the hypothesis.  
Future work involves deploying the model in real-world live market trading to further validate 
its effectiveness beyond theoretical simulations using historical stock data.
