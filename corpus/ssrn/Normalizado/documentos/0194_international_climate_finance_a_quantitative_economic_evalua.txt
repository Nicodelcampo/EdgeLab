Economic Evaluation

Abstract
We quantitatively analyze the effectiveness and optimal design of international
climate finance, which is a critical pillar of international climate cooperation.
Our
multi-county-multi-sector structural trade model incorporates two key climate action
channels—clean energy transition and agricultural adaptation to climate change. Using 2017 data, we find that international climate finance of USD 11.8 billion generates
substantial returns, increasing global welfare by USD 71.8 billion while reducing carbon
emissions by 264 million tonnes. We establish principles to improve the efficiency of
both mitigation and adaptation finance allocation within regions, while also providing
guidance on the distribution of funds between the two types of finance. Our analysis
reveals that international climate finance warrants significant scaling up from current
levels.

international climate finance; adaptation; mitigation; quantitative
trade; trade and environment

Introduction
International climate cooperation is vital for combating climate change. Developing countries, accounting for 63% of global carbon emissions, require trillions of dollars annually
to reduce emissions—costs they cannot bear alone and that pose significant challenges for
developed countries.1 A critical pillar of international climate cooperation is the protocol
of international climate finance, which reached USD 116 billion in 2022 (Figure 1).2
At
COP29 in 2024, developed nations pledged USD 300 billion annually to developing countries
by 2035. While substantial, this figure remains below the USD 1.3 trillion requested, and
detailed implementation plans are yet to be outlined.
To justify the immense scale of proposed international climate finance and bridge the
gap between commitments and practical outcomes, the following key questions must be
addressed: How effective is the current international climate finance system? Should funding
be increased, and if so, by how much? How should the funding contributions be determined
among potential providers? Finally, how should funds be distributed—both among recipient
nations and between mitigation and adaptation priorities? In this work, we aim to answer
these questions by offering a microfounded quantitative evaluation, which in turn elucidates
the design of international climate finance.
Specifically, we build a multicountry-multisector structural trade model with a detailed
micro foundation related to international climate finance. With respect to mitigation finance,
we focus on its impacts on the clean energy transition. We distinguish between clean and
dirty energy—the former involves renewables, wind and solar energy, whereas the latter
involves fossil-fuel-based energy and generates carbon emissions. Clean energy production
requires capital as a necessary input, with which mitigation finance can help. This modeling
choice is rooted in the practice of international mitigation finance, as stated by G-7 leaders as
follows:“The group of rich nations will pledge to funnel financing to developing countries to
help them build capital and accelerate a shift to renewable energy” (Colchester, 2023). With
respect to adaptation finance, we focus mainly on its impact on agriculture, which heavily
depends on weather and is the most affected by climate change. We model adaptation finance
such that it can help farmers adjust their production to those productivity changes induced

Based on the theoretical framework, we estimate key parameters using real data. In
addition to the common parameters used in the quantitative trade literature, the quantitative
predictions of our model hinge on two key parameters—the parameter deciding the renewable
potential ζi and the adaptive conversion elasticity βi. We estimate the first parameter using
clean energy investment data from the International Energy Agency (IEA) and clean energy
generation and capacity data from the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA),
and the second parameter is estimated by matching with agricultural labor intensity.
Armed with the critical parameters, we calibrate our model to 2017 and implement four
sets of counterfactual exercises. First, we evaluate the effectiveness of international climate
finance by comparing the outcome in the real-world scenario with that in the counterfactual scenario, without any international climate finance flow. We find that international
climate finance of USD 11.8 billion in the clean energy transition and agricultural production adaptation generates substantial returns, increasing global welfare by USD 71.8 billion
while reducing carbon emissions by 264 million tonnes.
Of these aggregate effects, the
USD 10.9 billion of international mitigation finance in the clean energy transition increases
global welfare by USD 60.7 billion, a sixfold return, and contributes to all carbon emission
reductions. On the other hand, the USD 0.87 billion international adaptation finance in
agriculture yields a nearly thirteen-fold increase in global welfare, reaching USD 11.1 billion.
Moreover, there are significant variations in carbon emission reduction effects and welfare
impacts across recipient countries, suggesting that substantial improvements can be achieved
through reallocation among recipients.
The second set of counterfactual exercises searches for the optimal allocation of international climate finance among recipients and among providers. We first investigate the
optimal recipient allocation of mitigation finance. As mitigation finance is intended to reduce carbon emissions, we aim to maximize global carbon emission reduction. We find that
national marginal mitigation benefits are jointly determined by a series of economic and
environmental fundamentals, including the price, carbon emission coefficient, renewable potential, and share of clean energy in the energy mix, which vary widely across countries.
Compared with the 2017 equilibrium, the optimal recipient distribution could additionally
abate 28 million tonnes of carbon emissions and increase global welfare by USD 21 billion. We
also study the optimal recipient allocation of adaptation finance, by equalizing the marginal
welfare effect of adaptation finance across recipient countries. We find that the marginal
welfare effect is affected by factors such as price, initial utility, changes in land rent share,
3

adaptation share, and adaptive conversion elasticity. We calculate the optimal allocation
and find that it could increase global welfare by USD 15 billion. The analysis also reveals
that the marginal effectiveness of both types of finance diminishes with increasing funding
levels, suggesting that when resources are limited, they should be concentrated in specific
regions, whereas abundant funds can be more widely distributed.
We then turn to the optimal allocation of providers, in which we consider two scenarios.
First, we maximize global welfare with all people assigned equal weight. As international
climate finance is a form of nominal transfer in our model, the optimum would be achieved
trivially by allocating funding obligations to countries with higher price levels and greater
carbon damage. With the second, we ask people from all the providing countries to share
this burden equally, i.e., we let the providers have the same welfare change after providing
finance. Interestingly, while the results from the first scenario align modestly with the 2017
data, those from the second scenario deviate significantly from both, which concords with
the fact that countries with higher price levels (i.e., richer) and greater carbon damage bear
more funding responsibilities than do larger countries such as the US.
In the third set of counterfactual exercises, we investigate the allocation of finance
between adaptation and mitigation. One fundamental difference between the two types of
climate finance is that mitigation finance reduces carbon emissions that are directly beneficial
to provider countries. We choose overall global welfare as the optimization objective, which
requires equal marginal welfare returns of the two types of funds as a necessary condition for
optimality. We first look into our benchmark equilibrium and find that under the current
allocation, the marginal welfare returns of adaptation finance are much higher than those
of mitigation finance, indicating non-optimality. We then look for the optimal allocation by
equating the two returns. By keeping each country’s climate-based transfers constant, we
find that the optimal share of adaptation finance in total international climate finance is
14%, which is 1.9 times the size in reality. A shift to this ratio would provide a welfare gain
of 1 billion USD.
Finally, we look into how much international climate finance is needed. In this section,
we aim to maximize overall provider or global welfare. Given the current recipient distribution, provider distribution, and ratio of adaptation finance, we find that the optimal amount
of international climate finance to maximize provider welfare is 220 billion USD, which is
18.7 times the real-world amount. This allocation would reduce carbon emissions by 3.9
billion tonnes and increase provider welfare by USD 92.2 billion. The optimal amount of
international climate finance to maximize global welfare is 240 billion USD, which is 20.4
times the real-world amount. This amount would reduce carbon emissions by 4.1 billion
tonnes and increase global welfare by USD 737 billion.

Our work contributes to several streams of literature. First, despite the rapid growth
in the number of publications on international climate finance, studies that evaluate its effectiveness remain limited. The literature focuses primarily on accounting methodologies, with
(Toetzke et al., 2022) identifying discrepancies in reported flows using a machine-learningbased inventory.
Feasibility studies, such as (Kotchen, 2020), examine the alignment of
financial transfers with net benefits from avoided climate damage to facilitate international
agreements. Determinants of allocation are a central theme, with research highlighting the
influence of donor trade interests (Bayramoglu et al., 2023), the importance of vulnerability
indicators (Betzold and Weiler, 2017), the challenges faced by small island developing states
(SIDSs) in accessing adaptation funding (Robinson and Dornan, 2017), and the tradeoffs
between equity and cost-effectiveness in multilateral adaptation finance (Stadelmann et al.,
2014). Additional studies model international climate finance as a type of income transfer
to promote sustainable development (Soergel et al., 2021a), alleviate poverty exacerbated
by climate policies (Soergel et al., 2021b), or explore its impacts as a type of carbon emission trading finance (Bowen et al., 2015). To our knowledge, we are the first to conduct
quantitative analyses on the effectiveness of international climate finance, its empirical decomposition, and strategies for its improved design.
Second, our analysis relates to the emerging literature that uses modern quantitative
trade models.
The methodology in this paper follows the “structural gravity” quantitative trade literature. The trade block of the model is rooted in Eaton and Kortum (2002)
and Caliendo and Parro (2015), whereas we benefit from Costinot and Donaldson (2016)
in modeling how agriculture responds to climate change. The main difference between our
paper and Costinot and Donaldson (2016) is that we introduce adaptation finance and derive a first-order approximation for welfare change in a multicountry-multisector model,
whereas Costinot and Donaldson (2016) work within a quasi-linear setting.
As summarized in Costinot and Rodr´ıguez-Clare (2014), quantitative trade frameworks benefit from
merits such as appealing micro-theoretical foundations, tighter connections between theory
and data, and greater emphasis on transparency. More generally, this research paradigm
has also been applied to trade-and-environment topics (Shapiro, 2016; Larch and Wanner,
2017; Shapiro, 2021). We, therefore, contribute to the literature by applying trade models
to investigate the effects and optimal allocation of international climate finance.
Third, we contribute to the literature on international climate cooperation by investigating the effects of international climate finance, a crucial global initiative to combat climate
change. One strand examines international climate agreements (ICAs), highlighting challenges with climate and intergenerational externalities (Maggi and Staiger, 2022), the welfare
effects of carbon taxes and subsidies under lobbying and ICAs (Kotchen and Maggi, 2024),
5

and how bargaining frameworks influence emissions and technology investments (Harstad,
2023). Recommendations include stronger intellectual property rights or subsidies for shortterm treaties (Harstad, 2016) and higher carbon taxes to meet Paris Agreement targets
(Cruz and Rossi-Hansberg, 2022). A second strand focuses on trade penalties to address
free-riding. Nordhaus (2015) shows that a climate club with weak trade penalties can create
stable coalitions with high-level abatement. Farrokhi and Lashkaripour (2021) finds that
a climate club can achieve up to 69% optimal emission reductions if led by major powers.
Hsiao (2021) demonstrates that coordinated import tariffs reduce emissions effectively, with
domestic export taxes being equally viable. A third strand examines carbon taxes. Conte et
al. (2022) show that carbon taxes in the EU or US can boost global welfare by concentrating production and incentivizing immigration. Furthermore, Kortum and Weisbach (2021)
propose an optimal climate policy using fossil fuel taxes, import tariffs, and export subsidies
to reduce the degree of leakage and promote efficiency.
Finally, we contribute to the general literature on climate change. A first group of
papers examines the effects of climate change. Studies such as Burke et al. (2023) show
that future climate damage far exceeds past damage, whereas Burgess et al. (2017) and
Carleton et al. (2022) find that climate change increases mortality risk, with significant
economic losses. Hsiang et al. (2017) estimates that U.S. climate damage will increase by
1.2% of GDP per +1°C, and Barrage (2023) shows that healthcare costs and tax changes
increase the social cost of carbon. Dingel et al. (2019) and Hsiao et al. (2024) highlight
that climate change exacerbates inequality and worsens welfare, especially in agriculture. A
second group of papers examines the role of adaptation in climate change impacts. Bilal and
Rossi-Hansberg (2023) finds that migration reduces local impacts but has a smaller effect on
overall U.S. welfare losses, amplified by capital depreciation. Cruz and Rossi-Hansberg (2024)
highlights spatial disparities in warming impacts, emphasizing the effectiveness of carbon
taxes combined with adaptation mechanisms such as migration and innovation. A third
group examines global attitudes toward climate change through surveys, with Dechezleprˆetre
et al. (2022) finding that policy effectiveness and impacts on low-income households drive
support and that educational videos explaining policies increase support.

2
Model
We construct a multicountry-multisector quantitative trade model with a detailed description
of international climate finance. The trade block of our paper resembles those of Eaton and
Kortum (2002) and Caliendo and Parro (2015), and in addition, we further incorporate the
clean energy transition and agricultural production adaptation into the model, which we
adopt according to Acemoglu et al. (2012) and Costinot et al. (2016) respectively. Based
on such a comprehensive model, we further introduce mitigation finance as an instrument
through which to increase clean energy production capacity, and grant adaptation finance
the power to improve farmers’ ability to adjust to climate change. In summary, we include
rich elements of international climate finance so that we can implement a thorough and
well-micro-founded examination.
We assume perfect competitiveness for goods and factor markets. The following two
production factors exist: labor, which is used in all sectors, and land, which is used only in
the agricultural sector. Labor is mobile across sectors but immobile across countries.

Mitigation Finance and the Energy Sector

energy in that it generates carbon emissions, whereas the production of clean energy requires
capital, which mitigation finance can help. Clean and dirty energy are combined to produce
composite energy, which is consumed by households or used as an intermediate input in
production.

2.3
Adaptation Finance and the Agricultural Sector
We classify agricultural workers, or farmers, into two groups—adapted and non-adapted.
Adapted farmers can adjust their production to productivity changes induced by climate
change (Costinot et al., 2016), whereas non-adapted farmers cannot. Adaptation finance can
convert non-adapted farmers into adapted farmers. Thus, adaptation finance can determine
the share of agriculture workers to adapt to climate change.
Adaptation Finance and Adaptation Share
In our model, we incorporate an agricultural capacity-building process.4 Adaptation finance funds the training of farmers, who
change their farming practices according to changing climate conditions. Training includes
helping farmers obtain seeds to adapt to climate change, acquire more climate information,
etc.5

Decomposition of the Impacts of International Climate Finance
In this section, we provide first-order decompositions of the effects of international climate
finance. By doing so, we can understand the channel through which to impact, and we also
evaluate the quantitative strength of these channels in later sections. In addition, the optimal
allocation of mitigation finance requires directing funds to regions with the highest marginal
reductions in carbon emissions. Similarly, the optimal allocation of adaptation finance prioritizes regions with the greatest marginal welfare gains. Using first-order decomposition,
we can also study the elements that fundamentally shape the optimal design of funds.

6
Counterfactual Exercises
In this section, we implement four sets of counterfactual exercises. First, we evaluate the
impacts of international climate finance in 2017 by eliminating it and comparing the outcomes
with those of the real world. We then search for the optimal allocation of international
climate finance among recipients and among providers. Moreover, we search for the optimal
allocation of funds between adaptation and mitigation. Finally, we look into the optimal
amount of international climate finance.
6.1
Effects of International Climate Finance
To assess the carbon emission reduction and welfare impacts of international mitigation
finance, as well as the welfare impacts of international adaptation finance, we simulate a
scenario in which these financial flows are excluded from the model.
We then compare
the resulting outputs to the scenario that includes these financial contributions. We begin
by analyzing the impacts of international mitigation finance, followed by an analysis of
international adaptation finance.
6.1.1
International Mitigation Finance
This part generates a counterfactual that eliminates international mitigation finance, allowing us to investigate its carbon emission reduction and welfare effects.
International
mitigation finance can build clean energy capital and extend its capacity constraint.
In
this way, greater clean energy production can substitute for dirty energy and decrease the
corresponding carbon emissions.
The welfare effects of international mitigation finance include two components—real
consumption and carbon damage. We calculate the real consumption change and carbon
damage change as (RCT −1)×GDP and (CBT −1)×GDP, respectively, following Shapiro
(2016), where RCT is the real consumption change in percentage terms, and CBT is the
carbon damage change in percentage terms. The welfare change is then calculated as the sum
of the real consumption change and the carbon damage change. Real consumption change,
carbon damage change and welfare change represent US 2017$ billions. Our analysis suggests
that USD 10.9 billion in international mitigation finance for the transition to clean energy
29

mitigates carbon emissions by 263.9 million tons.
Thus, the carbon emission reduction
benefit is equal to USD 49 billion. This reduction contributes to a global real consumption
increase of USD 11.7 billion, resulting in a net global welfare benefit of USD 60.7 billion.
Mitigation finance is intended to reduce the carbon emissions of developing countries.
The carbon emission reduction of each recipient region is shown in Figure 2. Each node
represents either a provider or a recipient, with the region’s abbreviation displayed near the
node. Coordinates are plotted on a logarithmic scale, with the X-axis showing the mitigation
finance received by each recipient region and the Y-axis representing the quantity of carbon
mitigated. Each point corresponds to a specific recipient region. The positive correlation
between carbon emission reduction and mitigation finance increases is self-evident. Besides,
there are very heterogeneous carbon emission reduction effects across recipient countries,
with some countries experiencing dramatic carbon emission reduction effects. We explore
the reasons for such effects in Section 6.2.1.
We present the welfare effects of mitigation finance for each provider and recipient
region in Figure 3.
Panel (a) shows that while large portion of providers benefit from
providing mitigation finance, there are net losers as well. Countries such as Canada (CAN),
Australia (AUS), and the Netherlands (NZL) experience limited impacts from climate change
and, therefore, do not derive substantial benefits from mitigation finance.
Furthermore,
there is no clear correlation between welfare benefits and international mitigation finance
contributions from providers. This finding indicates that the provision of funds reflects a
measure of altruism, highlighting the collaborative nature of this international effort aimed
at the greater good. The good news is that the total benefit of all providers increases by 11.6
billion dollars; therefore, overall funding provision is still beneficial to providers. Panel (b)
shows that all recipients benefit from receiving mitigation finance. For both graphs, there is
no strong correlation between the size of mitigation finance and welfare change, indicating
that there are many additional underlying and unseen mechanisms, which we explore later.
6.1.2
International Adaptation Finance
In this subsection, we execute a counterfactual that eliminates both international adaptation
finance and mitigation finance. Our intention is twofold: First, by comparing the results in
the last subsection, we can identify the impacts of international adaptation finance. Second,
we can assess the overall effects of international climate finance.
Our results suggest that the 0.87 billion USD in international adaptation finance has
increased global welfare by 11.08 billion USD. Providers have experienced a welfare loss of
USD 1 billion, whereas recipients have gained USD 12.07 billion.
Figure 4 illustrates the welfare effects of adaptation finance for both providers and
30

recipients. In Panel (a), the Y-axis represents the welfare change by providers, whereas in
Panel (b), the Y-axis represents the welfare gained by recipients. Panel (a) reveals that
although some providers experienced a positive production spillover effect from providing
adaptation finance, others incurred a welfare loss. Panel (b) indicates that all recipients
benefit from receiving adaptation finance. The results in this subsection help explain why
adaptation finance is not popular in the current developed world.
In summary, USD 11.8 billion in international climate finance in 2017 led to a USD 71.8
billion increase in global welfare, with providers benefiting by USD 10.6 billion. Additionally,
such finance is projected to reduce CO2 emissions by 264 million tonnes.

6.3
The Optimal Ratio: Adaptation Finance vs. Mitigation Finance
This section discusses the optimal allocation of adaptation finance as a proportion of total
climate finance, where “optimal” refers to maximizing global welfare. To achieve this goal,
keeping each country’s climate-based transfers constant, we calculate the change in global
welfare for varying adaptation finance ratios, ranging from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.01,
compared with a baseline scenario without international climate finance. The ratio that
yields the greatest increase in global welfare is identified as the optimal level. Our analysis
indicates that the optimal ratio is 0.14, which is 1.9 times higher than the 2017 level, resulting
in a global welfare increase of USD 1 billion relative to the baseline. Although our analysis
focuses on a subset of international climate finance, an approximate comparison using the
current 21% of adaptation accounts for public climate finance suggests that the optimal ratio
to maximize global welfare is approximately 40% (21% × 1.9).
The optimal ratio is determined by equating the marginal welfare change from additional mitigation finance to the marginal welfare change from additional adaptation finance.
Figure 10(a) illustrates how these marginal changes evolve as the share of adaptation finance
37

increases. Specifically, as the share of adaptation finance increases, the marginal welfare gain
from additional adaptation finance diminishes, whereas the marginal welfare gain from additional mitigation finance increases. The point where these two curves intersect represents
the optimal ratio, identified as 0.14.
Figure 10(b) illustrates changes in welfare for the world, providers, and recipients as
the share of adaptation finance increases, relative to a scenario with no international climate
finance. Global welfare initially increases but then decreases as the share of adaptation finance grows. For providers, welfare consistently decreases as the share of adaptation finance
increases. This finding highlights their preference for mitigation finance over adaptation
finance, as mitigation efforts in developing countries produce positive externalities for developed countries. As the share of adaptation finance increases, welfare for recipients initially
increases but eventually decreases. This finding suggests that there is an optimal balance for
adaptation finance and that allocating excessively high shares may not be beneficial, even for
recipients. While both adaptation and mitigation finance benefit recipients, their marginal
welfare contributions diminish with increased amounts. Initially, adaptation finance provides
greater welfare gains for recipients. However, as its share grows, mitigation finance becomes
more effective at enhancing their welfare.
6.4
Optimal Amount
This section examines the optimal amount of international climate finance, the most important topic at the 2024 UN Climate Change Conference. The issue is critical, as developing
countries are demanding increased financial support in exchange for adopting more ambitious mitigation targets sought by developed countries. To address this issure, we analyzed
welfare changes across varying levels of climate finance—ranging from 0 to 350 billion USD
in 5-billion-dollar increments—while maintaining the existing regional distribution of funds
and adaptation finance ratio. The results are compared against a baseline scenario with no
international climate finance.
Our analysis reveals that current levels of international climate finance are significantly
below the optimal level. The amount of climate finance needed to maximize the welfare of
providers is USD 220 billion, which is 18.7 times greater than the 2017 level. Compared
with no international climate finance, this allocation improves providers’ welfare by USD
92.2 billion, increases global welfare by USD 716 billion, and reduces carbon emissions by 3.9
billion tonnes. Moreover, the amount required to maximize global welfare is USD 240 billion,
which is 20.4 times greater than the 2017 level, resulting in a USD-737-billion gain in global
welfare and a USD-91.6-billion improvement for providers while reducing carbon emissions by
38

4.1 billion tonnes. Although our analysis focuses on a subset of international climate finance,
an approximate comparison using the total USD 71.6 billion in 2017 international climate
finance suggests that the optimal amount to maximize provider welfare is approximately
USD 1.34 trillion (71.6 × 18.7), whereas the optimal amount to maximize global welfare is
approximately USD 1.46 trillion (71.6 × 20.4).
Figure 11(a) illustrates the global welfare changes as well as those for providers and
recipients as the amount of climate finance increases. While welfare initially increases with
increased climate finance, it eventually decreases due to diminishing marginal benefits. This
finding highlights the existence of an optimal level of climate finance, indicating that excessively high amounts may also not be efficient.
Figure 11(b) illustrates the reasons underlying the optimal amount of international
climate finance. We plot three key curves—the provider’s demand curve, which reflects the
marginal benefit of providing climate finance; the social demand curve, or social marginal
benefit curve, which exceeds the provider’s demand curve due to the positive externalities
that climate finance generates for recipients; and the provider’s supply curve, representing
the marginal cost of providing climate finance, which is calculated as the current welfare
loss caused by reduced income when funds are allocated to recipients. Both the provider’s
demand curve and the social demand curve decline as the amount of climate finance increases,
reflecting the diminishing marginal benefits of international climate finance. The optimal
amount for providers is determined by the intersection of their demand and supply curves.
In addition, the global optimal amount is found at the intersection of the social demand
curve and the provider’s supply curve.
7
Conclusion
This study intends to fill the literature gap of investigating the impact and optimal allocation
of international climate finance. To do this, we nest the clean energy transition and agricultural production adaptation into a multicountry-multisector quantitative trade model.
Based on the theoretical framework, we execute a series of quantitative exercises to evaluate
and improve the design of international climate finance.
Our results highlight the critical importance and significant benefits of international
climate finance in humanity’s fight against global warming. The impact of such finance,
however, could be further amplified through improvements in its design across multiple
dimensions. Specifically, global carbon emission reductions can be optimized by directing
mitigation finance to regions with lower price indices, higher carbon emission coefficients,
greater renewable energy potential, higher clean energy distribution parameters, and lower
39

quantity share of clean energy in their energy mix.
Similarly, global welfare can be maximized by allocating adaptation finance to regions
with lower price indices, higher initial utility, more significant changes in land rent shares,
and lower adaptation share and adaptive conversion elasticities. Additionally, we find that
the marginal effectiveness of both mitigation and adaptation finance diminishes as the level
of finance increases. As such, when funding is limited, resources should be concentrated in
a single region, while abundant funding can be distributed across multiple countries.
Our findings also emphasize the urgent need to increase the share of international
adaptation finance within the overall climate finance framework, as the current marginal
welfare effect of adaptation finance is significantly greater than that of mitigation finance.
Finally, our findings underscore the need to scale up the size of climate finance, which
currently falls far short of its optimal level. This vital topic warrants further research to
deepen and broaden our overall understanding.

Global Multi-Region Multi-Sector Input-Output Table
We merge the 76 sectors in the GTAP database into 17 sectors (shown in Table 2). Except
for the clean energy, dirty energy and agricultural plants sector, other sectors concords with
the industry classification of Shapiro (2016). These 17 sectors are the clean energy, dirty
energy, agricultural plants, other agricultural, mining, food, textiles, clothes, wood, paper,
mineral, chemical, metals, electrical, transportation, other manufactures, and non-tradable
sectors. The clean energy sector in the model corresponds to the electricity sector in the data,
whereas the dirty energy sector corresponds to the combination of coal, oil, gas, petroleum,
gas manufacturing and distribution.
We take full advantage of SAM in our parameterization process.18 The details in the
SAM greater than are those in the model and include on imported commodities, domestic
commodities, activities, factors, regional households, private households, trade taxes, import
sales taxes, domestic sales taxes, factor taxes, production taxes, direct taxes, government,
capital, import margins, export margins and rest of world. To ensure that the data concord
with the model, we make the following adjustments to the SAM.
We merged domestic commodities with activities (since activities are used to produce
domestic commodities); merged private household and government into regional households;
merged factor into regional households; adjusted export margins so that each sector’s world
import margin equals its world export margin;19 merged import margins and export margins
into the rest of the world;20 merged regional household incomes with taxes (specifically,
factor use tax, production tax, factor income tax, trade tax, import sales tax, and domestic
sales tax);21 Import duties are deducted from import commodities, being part of government
