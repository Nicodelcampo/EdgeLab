Pairs Trading with Costly Short-Selling ∗
Jing Xu †
Peiquan Yang ‡
Abstract
We study an optimal pairs trading model with costly short-selling. When the investor has
logarithm utility function, we derive the solution in closed form, which shows that: the
optimal allocation functions are piece-wise linear in the pair’s relative price; stock borrowing
fees asymmetrically reduce the optimal size of the long/short position; and for risk-hedging
purpose, it can be optimal to short sell even when the stock borrowing fees outweigh the
expected return earned from short selling. When the investor has power utility function, we
propose analytical allocation functions adopting which only causes a small utility loss. When
the investor is constrained from borrowing funds, the margin requirement for short selling
can signiﬁcantly aﬀect the trading strategy. Empirically, we demonstrate the importance of
incorporating short selling costs when trading pairs in China’s stock market and verify the
model-implied relation between short selling costs and proﬁtability of pairs trading.
Keywords: portfolio choice; pairs trading; risky arbitrage; short selling cost.
JEL Classiﬁcation: C32, G11, G12.

Introduction
As a popular statistical arbitrage method, pairs trading aims to exploit relative mispricing
of two highly correlated assets.
When the prices of two such assets exhibit temporary
divergence, an arbitrageur can simultaneously take a long position on the undervalued asset
and a short position on the overvalued one, speculating on future convergence of their prices.
Although optimal pairs trading has been extensively studied in the literature, existing models
often neglect the costs incurred by short selling in pairs trading. This assumption may not
be consequential in stock markets where the costs of short selling are relatively low (e.g.,
the US stock market); it is, however, inappropriate for markets where short selling is highly
costly (e.g., the China’s stock market).1
In fact, by studying trading performance in 34
international stock markets, Jacobs and Weber (2015) show that short selling costs are an
important driver of pairs trading performance, highlighting the necessity of incorporating
short selling costs in the context of optimal pairs trading. This study ﬁlls the above gap by
examining the impact of various aspects of short selling costs, including stock borrowing fees
and margin requirement, on optimal pairs trading. Moreover, by performing an empirical
analysis in China’s mainland stock market, we empirically demonstrate the importance of
optimally incorporating short selling costs in pairs trading and verify the model’s main
implications.
Speciﬁcally, we study an optimal portfolio choice problem of an investor who dynamically allocates wealth between a risk-free asset (bond) and two risky assets (stocks). After
de-trending, each stock has an extra positive (or negative) expected return when it is undervalued (or overvalued) relative to the other one, and the diﬀerence in their de-trended

value of the borrowed stocks.

log-prices (“relative price” hereafter) is reverting towards a long-run mean of zero, implying
a tendency of convergence of the de-trended prices. Diﬀerent from prior studies, we assume
short selling any of these two stocks is costly, requiring the investor to maintain suﬃcient
margins and pay stock borrowing fees. Consistent with the practice in China’s stock market,
we assume constant stock borrowing fees rate for both stocks. The investor’s objective is
to maximize her expected constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) utility derived from the
wealth level at a ﬁnite horizon.2
In the absence of short selling costs, similar to Liu and Timmermann (2013), the optimal
stock allocations are linear functions of the relative price. In the presence of short selling
costs and when the investor’s utility function is of logarithm type, we derive the optimal
allocations in closed form.
The optimal allocations are piecewise linear functions of the
relative price. Speciﬁcally, when one stock is only slightly overpriced relative to the other, it
is optimal to take a long position (instead of a short position) on the overvalued stock to earn
its positive instantaneous risk premium. When the degree of overpricing becomes greater
but is still below a critical threshold, it is optimal to take a null position on the overvalued
stock as this avoids incurring the short selling costs. When the degree of overpricing becomes
suﬃciently large, it is optimal to short sell the overvalued stock because its expected return
becomes suﬃciently negative.
In our model, short selling is driven by two incentives: (i) to capitalize on the negative
expected return of the overpriced stock (speculation) and (ii) to hedge the risk of holding
the undervalued stock (risk hedging). This fact has two major implications. First, it can
be optimal to short sell the overpriced stock even when the stock borrowing fees outweigh

When a stock is deeply undervalued, the investor takes a long position with a large size on
this stock to speculate on its high expected return, generating a large risk exposure and
creating a strong demand for hedging the risk. In such a case, if the stock borrowing fees
only marginally outweigh the expected return earned from short selling the overvalued stock,
the demand for hedging risk can dominate and make it optimal to short sell. Second, the
short stock borrowing costs simultaneously but asymmetrically reduce the optimal size of
the long and of the short position. The impact on the short position is due to the lower
after-fee return earned from short selling. The reduction in the short position’s size reduces
the investor’s risk exposure, weakening her risk-hedging demand and leading to a smaller
optimal size of the long position. Because the expected return earned from the long position
is not aﬀected by the stock borrowing fees, the impact on the long position is smaller in
magnitude.
When the investor’s utility function is of power type, closed-form solution is no longer
available. We ﬁrst demonstrate numerically that the optimal allocation functions in this
case share many similarities with those in the logarithm utility function case. For example,
the optimal allocation functions are close to be piecewise linear in the relative price; and
it is optimal to short sell only when the degree of overpricing exceeds a threshold level.
Unlike those in the logarithm utility function case, however, the optimal allocation functions
and threshold levels in the power utility function case are time-dependent. Based on these
observations, we analytically derive a near-optimal allocation rule and we show that adopting
it only causes a fairly small utility loss for the investor.
In China’s stock market, borrowing stock for short selling is subject to high margin
requirement (see footnote 1).
To study the impact of such high margin requirement on
optimal pairs trading, we add a constraint on the investor’s ability to borrow funds. With
3

this additional constraint, the margin requirement can have a signiﬁcant impact on the
optimal stock allocations. For instance, when one stock is deeply overpriced, if this stock’s
expected return is suﬃciently negative, then it is optimal to take a large short position
on this stock; in this case, the investor may need to reduce the size of her long position
on the undervalued stock so that she has suﬃcient funds to maintain the margin required
for short selling. With the no-fund-borrowing constraint, we decompose the total costs of
short selling into two components: the direct stock borrowing fees payment and the liquidity
constraint cost of maintaining margins; and we show that the second cost component can
also be signiﬁcant.
To make a direct comparison with Liu and Timmermann (2013), which study optimal
convergence trade without short selling costs, we further examine an extension of the baseline
model. In the extended model, we allow the investor to trade in a market portfolio, which
is costly to short sell, and assume the paired stocks are priced according to the CAPM
conditional on zero relative price level. With zero stock borrowing fee, we replicate Liu and
Timmermann (2013)’s result that the optimal allocations to the paired stocks have opposite
signs near the zero relative price level when the two stocks’ prices are mutually convergent.
With positive stock borrowing fees, the investor changes the short positions to null positions
near the zero relative price level since the abnormal returns earned from short selling are too
low compared to the stock borrowing fees. Thus, diﬀerent from those in the baseline model,
the optimal allocations to the paired stocks in this extension do not have the same sign near
zero relative price level. Moreover, we show that stock borrowing costs can reduce the size
of the position on the market portfolio even when it is a long position. This is because stock
borrowing costs reduce wealth allocations to the paired stocks, leading to smaller systematic
risk exposure to hedge by trading the market portfolio.
Lastly, we perform an empirical analysis in China’s mainland stock market and the
4

objective is twofold. First, we demonstrate the importance of optimally incorporating stock
borrowing costs for pairs trading in China’s stock market. For this objective, we investigate
the empirical performance of the optimal trading strategy and of the cost-naive trading
strategy which is obtained under the zero stock borrowing fee assumption, respectively.
Second, we verify our model’s major implication that the higher the stock borrowing fees, the
higher the value of adopting the optimal trading strategy. For this objective, we exploit the
time-series variations in the stock borrowing fees rate quoted by a major Chinese securities
company, namely Huafu Securities. During the testing period, Huafu Securities’ quote of the
stock borrowing fees rate experiences one single signiﬁcant change on 16 March 2015, when
it drops from 10.6% to 8.35%. Such variation enables us to demonstrate our main points in
the clearest manner.
By examining the performance of trading the top 15 pairs formed using the shortable
Chinese stocks, we show that the cost-naive strategy substantially underperforms the optimal
strategy in terms of risk-adjusted returns. When adopting the cost-naive strategy, the investor trades on the relative mispricing too aggressively, leading to excessive stock borrowing
fees payment and resulting in considerably lower Sharpe ratios. Speciﬁcally, when trading
in an initially equal-weighted portfolio of the sample pairs, the Sharpe ratio achieved by the
optimal strategy is 0.3 higher than that achieved by the cost-naive strategy during the full
testing period. In addition, the performance advantage of the optimal strategy is larger during the subperiod with higher stock borrowing fees. For example, for the above-mentioned
pairs portfolio, the improvement in the Sharpe ratio equals 0.27 (0.21 resp.) during the
high-fees (low-fees resp.) subperiod. These results are consistent with our model’s main
implications.

Literature Review
There is a large body of literature on optimal pairs trading.
Generally speaking, pairs
trading models can be classiﬁed into two categories: the spread-based approach and the
cointegration-based approach.3
The spread-based approach directly models the spread between the prices of two or more
target stocks and designs trading strategies accordingly. The development of this approach is
mainly reﬂected by the ever-growing sophistication of the spread modeling, starting from the
simple mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process model (e.g., Elliott et al. (2005)
and Zeng and Lee (2014)) to more complicated models such as the regime-switching model
(Bai and Wu (2018)), the L´evy OU process model (Wu et al. (2020)), the general state space
model (Zhang (2021)), and the fractional OU process model (Xiang et al. (2022)). G¨onc¨u
and Akyildirim (2016) study the application of the spread-based approach to pairs trading
in the commodity markets.
Diﬀerent from the spread-based approach, the cointegration-based approach models the
price of each stock separately, but permitting a cointegration relation between diﬀerent stocks’ prices.4 This approach allows the researchers to cast optimal pairs trading problems
in the classic portfolio choice framework (see, e.g., Tourin and Yan (2013) and Liu and Tim3Some researchers have recently explored the possibility of applying machine learning techniques in pairs
trading. For instance, Flori and Regoli (2021) use the long/short-term memory networks to reveal pairs
trading opportunities; Han et al. (2023) examine the use of unsupervised learning technique in pairs trading.
4It is worth mentioning that Huck and Afawubo (2015) argue for the superiority of the cointegration-based

6

mermann (2013)). The recent advances of the cointegration-based approach include optimal
pairs trading with parameter uncertainty (Chiu and Wong (2018)), partial cointegration (Clegg and Krauss (2018)), or delayed cointegration (Yan et al. (2022)). Lintilhac and Tourin
(2017) apply the cointegration-based method to optimal pairs trading in the Bitcoin markets.
Since short selling costs are only incurred by the short leg, we need to model the long and
the short leg in pairs trading separately. Thus, the cointegration-based approach becomes
the ideal choice for our purpose.
Our study is closely related to the studies of optimal pairs trading with market frictions,
such as transaction costs (e.g., Song and Zhang (2013) and Lei and Xu (2015)) or price
impact (Cartea et al. (2019)). An important diﬀerence between these studies and ours is
that transaction costs or price impact are incurred only at the time of trading, while the
stock borrowing fees are charged continuously when the stocks are borrowed for short selling
(D’avolio (2002)). Our study is also related to the studies of optimal arbitrage strategies,
including Brennan and Schwartz (1990) which study optimal arbitrage with index futures
by modeling the spread between two similar futures’ prices as a Brownian bridge process;
Dai et al. (2011) which extend Brennan and Schwartz (1990) by allowing the arbitrageur to
directly reverse positions at a lower cost; and Liu and Longstaﬀ(2004) which examine the
impact of collateral requirement on optimal arbitrage trading.
The empirical performance of pairs trading strategies has been examined in many ﬁnancial markets, including the US stock market (Gatev et al. (2006), Schultz and Shive (2010),
Rinne and Suominen (2017), and Chen et al. (2019)), the US REITs market (Mori and
Ziobrowski (2011)), the international commodities markets (Fernandez-Perez et al. (2020)),
and the international stock markets (Jacobs and Weber (2015)). Our empirical analysis
contributes to this ﬁeld of study by demonstrating the importance of optimally controlling
short selling costs when conducting pairs trading in China’s stock market.

3
The Model
Our purpose is to study the impact of costly short-selling on optimal pairs trading. Hence,
our model needs to capture some important stylized facts about short selling in markets with
high short selling costs. We choose China’s mainland stock market as the main playing ﬁeld
for two reasons. First, it has become the second largest stock market in the world; thus,
studying optimal trading strategies in this market is economically meaningful. Second, short
selling in China’s stock market is subject to high costs, making it particularly important to
optimally reduce short selling costs to enhance the proﬁtability of pairs trading.5
3.1
Costs of Short Selling Stocks in China
We begin with discussing some stylized facts about short selling in China’s mainland stock
market.6 In this market, short selling becomes feasible only after the launch of the pilot
program in March 2010, when qualiﬁed investors are allowed to short sell the stocks included
in the list of Designated Securities Eligible for Short Selling (“the list” for short). Afterwards,
the list is expanded gradually over time. Qualiﬁed investors can short sell stocks using the
stock borrowing service provided by securities companies, which charge stock borrowing fees.
A unique feature of the stock borrowing fees rate in China’s stock market is that diﬀerent
securities companies may quote diﬀerent rates; but for a given securities company, the rate
typically does not vary across shortable stocks. This is very diﬀerent from the US market
where stock borrowing fees exhibit signiﬁcant cross-stock variations (D’avolio (2002)).

Model Implications
In this section, we analyze the model’s primary implications.
4.1
Pair Selection and Parameter Estimates
We use the historical daily returns of a pair of stocks traded in China’s mainland market
to calibrate the stock-related parameters. To select the pair, we carry out the following
procedures.
First, we identify stocks that are shortable during the period from 29 July
2010 to 31 October 2023. Next, following Gatev et al. (2006), we construct a total return
14

index for each of these stocks, incorporating dividend distributions and share splits. Then,
we determine the pairwise distance of these stocks by calculating the Euclidean distance
between the time series of their total return indexes. We ﬁnd that the stock of China CITIC
Bank Co., Ltd. and the stock of Bank of Communications Co., Ltd. have the minimum
distance. Accordingly, we pair them together to calibrate model parameters.
To deal with diﬀerential nominal price levels of the two stocks, we estimate a matching
ratio k so that we can regard k shares of the China CITIC Bank as stock 1 and 1 share of the
Bank of Communications as stock 2 in the model. The optimal matching ratio k together with
other stock-related parameters are estimated using the quasi-maximum likelihood estimation
(QMLE) method detailed in Appendix A.1. Using this approach, we obtain the following

component is that it can be optimal to short sell the overvalued stock even if the short
selling costs exceed the expected return earned from short selling. Note that by short selling

5.2
Objective and Testing Strategy
The main objective of our empirical analysis is twofold. First, we demonstrate the importance
of optimally incorporating stock borrowing costs for pairs trading in China’s stock market.
For this objective, we investigate the empirical performance of the optimal trading strategy
and of the cost-naive trading strategy which is obtained under the zero stock borrowing fee
assumption, respectively. Second, we verify our model’s main implication that the higher
the short selling costs, the more important to adopt the optimal trading strategy. For this
objective, we exploit the time-series variations in the stock borrowing fees rate quoted by
a major Chinese securities company, namely Huafu Securities. We consider this securities
company for the following two reasons. First, Huafu Securities’ quote is available from 13 July
2012 to present, leaving us with a relatively long testing period. Second, Huafu Securities’
quote experiences one single signiﬁcant drop during the testing period.21 In particular, the
quoted stock borrowing fees rate is 10.6% during the period from 13 July 2012 to 15 March
21By comparison, the quotes of some other securities companies typical exhibit multiple small changes
during the sample period, making it harder to cleanly demonstrate our main points.
41

2015 (subperiod 1), and it decreases to 8.35% during the period from 16 March 2015 to 31
October 2023 (subperiod 2). Such variation enables us to demonstrate our main points in
the clearest manner.
Suppose that an investor with a relative risk aversion coeﬃcient of 5 trades the 15 pairs
using the stock borrowing service provided by Huafu Securities.22 We investigate her trading performance in the full testing period, as well as in subperiod 1 and in subperiod 2

subperiod 1 when the stock borrowing fees rate is higher. For example, the portfolio-level

at the pair level for 10 out of the 15 pairs (the cases indicated with asterisk). This indicates
a positive relation between the performance advantage of the optimal trading strategy and
the magnitude of short selling costs, consistent with our model’s main implication.
Intuitively, by adopting the cost-naive strategy, the investor will incur excessive stock
borrowing costs, which can drag down the performance. To verify this intuition, we calculate
the investor’s average daily stock borrowing costs–wealth ratio and report the results in Table
6. This ratio is uniformly greater when adopting the cost-naive strategy than when adopting
the optimal strategy. Moreover, as is shown in Exhibits 2 and 3, the gap in the realized
short selling costs is wider during subperiod 1 when the stock borrowing fees rate is higher.
