The Microstructure of NFT Markets:
Evidence from 410 Million Orderbook Events
Tim Baumgartner∗
March 15, 2026
How do markets create liquidity when every asset is unique?
Using
over 410 million orderbook events across six major NFT collections, we
document a two-tier orderbook in which collection-level bids – offers to
purchase any token of a collection – rather than token-specific orders,
supply the dominant source of buy-side liquidity. Median quoted spreads
of 48–80% far exceed equity benchmarks, yet effective trading costs are
only 2–5% for liquid collections, as most transactions execute against
collection-level bids priced near the collection floor. Wide quoted spreads
thus reflect the two-tier structure, not illiquidity, with implications for
any market where heterogeneous assets trade under a common category.

Introduction
How do you make a market for something that exists only once?
In equity markets,
continuous competition among buyers and sellers of identical shares produces bid-ask
spreads of a few basis points. But when every asset is unique – as with non-fungible
tokens (NFTs) – there is no identical share, no fungible counterpart, and no obvious
mechanism for price discovery. The standard microstructure framework – from bid-ask
spreads to market maker models – takes fungibility as given. How liquidity is created and
priced when every asset differs remains largely an open empirical question.
NFT markets have generated tens of billions of dollars in cumulative trading volume,
with daily volume regularly exceeding $300 million at the 2022 peak – yet fundamental
questions about their microstructure remain unanswered. How liquid are these markets?
Who provides liquidity when there is no designated market maker? What determines the
variation in market quality across collections, time periods, and market structures?
These questions extend beyond the NFT market. Microstructure theory is built on
fungibility – yet markets for heterogeneous assets, from real estate to art to intellectual
property, must create liquidity without it. As traditional finance explores the tokenization of such assets, large-scale empirical evidence on how markets function in the absence
of fungibility remains scarce; existing evidence on unique-asset markets such as art (Korteweg et al., 2016) relies on sparse auction data. NFT markets provide a unique laboratory: years of continuous trading, hundreds of millions of recorded orderbook events, and
observable participant identities offer a granularity unavailable in any other market for
heterogeneous assets. They reveal, at the order level, how markets endogenously create
fungibility to generate liquidity – and the adverse selection costs this entails.
We address these questions using transaction-level orderbook data, comprising more
than 410 million events across six major Ethereum-based NFT collections, supplemented
by on-chain event data for extended sample coverage and robustness. Unlike prior work
that relies exclusively on completed transactions recorded on the blockchain, our data
capture bids, asks, sales, and transfers with pseudonymous trader identities, timestamps,
and marketplace attribution. We first develop a conceptual framework for the two-tier
1

orderbook (Section 4) and then organize the empirical analysis around three sections:
How should liquidity be measured when assets are unique (Section 5)? Does professional
liquidity provision emerge (Section 6)? What determines the variation in market quality
(Section 7)?
Our first set of results establishes the baseline: NFT markets are illiquid by any
conventional standard, with median quoted spreads of 48–80% (approaching 200% for
the least liquid collections) and bid-to-trade ratios reaching up to 50 000. These quoted
spreads, computed from the 1–7% of snapshots with two-sided quotes1, represent upper
bounds on true trading costs; a floor-benchmark spread that covers nearly all sales yields
costs of only 2–5% for the most liquid collections, indicating that the wide quoted spreads
partly reflect the structural properties of the two-tier orderbook rather than round-trip
transaction costs alone.
Standard measures miss a key institutional feature: A large
share of bidding activity takes the form of collection-level floor bids, offers to purchase
any token in a collection at a stated price. These floor bids constitute the effective best
bid in the vast majority of orderbook snapshots and reduce measured spreads by more
than one-third. We call this structure the two-tier orderbook: token-specific orders in the
upper tier and collection-level floor bids in the lower.
Our second set of results characterizes liquidity provision: A small number of algorithmic market makers (5 to 38 per collection) supply the bulk of buy-side liquidity, with
reaction times as low as one second after completed sales. Yet these market makers bear
substantial costs: Market-adjusted post-purchase returns are negative at all horizons,
reaching −3% to −7% at ten days, reflecting both adverse selection (a token-level rarity
test confirms a modest quality differential) and inventory risk from market-wide price declines. The dominant channel appears to be market timing rather than token-level quality
selection: A bid-type decomposition shows that collection-level bidders are, if anything,
less exposed to subsequent market movements than token-specific purchasers, consistent
with conservative bid shading concentrating their purchases on post-decline dates. We

Our third set of results examines the determinants of market quality: Collection identity accounts for the largest share of spread variation in panel regressions, absorbing over
30 percentage points of explained variation, while token rarity contributes no additional
explanatory power despite its central role in NFT valuation. An institutional comparison sharpens this result: CryptoPunks and WrappedCryptoPunks represent the same
10 000 underlying assets under two market structures. Examining these data reveals that
the native bilateral market achieves tighter quoted spreads (54% vs. 111%) despite generating far fewer bids, though transaction-based price impact is substantially lower in
the standardized market – suggesting that the two-tier orderbook structure inflates measured quoted spreads without necessarily raising execution costs. A subperiod analysis
documents secular improvement in bid infrastructure alongside declining trading activity,
and wash trading detection reveals that suspicious activity is concentrated on platforms
offering volume-based incentive programs.
When viewed as a whole, these findings document how markets function when the
core microstructure assumption of fungibility is absent – dynamics that are, by definition,
unobservable in equity data. They contribute to three strands of the literature. First,
we introduce the two-tier orderbook as a conceptual and analytical framework for NFT
liquidity, providing the first comprehensive empirical documentation of NFT market microstructure using bid-side data. The academic NFT literature has grown rapidly since
2021 but remains focused on returns (Kong and Lin, 2021; Dowling, 2022; Mazur, 2021),
bubble dynamics (Barbon and Ranaldo, 2023), and trading networks (Nadini et al., 2021);
surveys confirm this emphasis (Kr¨aussl and Tugnetti, 2024). Work on NFT microstructure is limited: Mukhopadhyay and Ghosh (2021) discuss market structure conceptually,
and Wilkoff and Yildiz (2023) examine illiquidity using the Amihud (2002) measure but
rely on transaction data alone. No prior study analyzes bid-side data at scale.
The bid-ask spread, analyzed as the cost of immediacy by Demsetz (1968) and subsequently decomposed into adverse selection (Copeland and Galai, 1983; Glosten and

costs. In NFT markets, however, token-level spreads are computable for fewer than 7%
of orderbook snapshots because most tokens have one-sided quotes. The coexistence of
token-specific orders and collection-level floor bids has no counterpart in traditional securities markets. We decompose token values into a common collection component and
an idiosyncratic premium, derive an adverse selection mechanism in which collection-level
bidders systematically acquire below-average tokens, and find patterns consistent with the
resulting predictions in the data. Lommers et al. (2022) model the option-like character of
NFT floor prices theoretically, but ours is the first study to formalize the adverse selection
inherent in collection-level bidding and to document how floor bids interact with tokenspecific orders, reshape measured spreads, and contribute to price discovery. We apply the
full microstructure toolkit – spreads, depth, price impact, market maker identification,
adverse selection, commonality, and panel regressions – to a dataset of over 410 million
events, in the spirit of Makarov and Schoar (2020) for cryptocurrency markets. More
broadly, the two-tier structure is not specific to NFTs: It arises wherever buyers can bid
on categories of heterogeneous assets rather than individual items. NFT markets, with
hundreds of millions of observable orderbook events, provide the first empirical setting in
which this structure can be documented and analyzed at the tick level.
Second, we identify and characterize NFT market makers and document a form of
adverse selection specific to heterogeneous-asset markets: a which-asset problem in which
bidders face uncertainty not about fundamental value but about which asset they will
receive.
This adverse selection channel – uncertainty about which asset is delivered
rather than about its value – cannot arise in fungible-asset markets and has therefore
remained unobserved in the empirical literature. The algorithmic, cross-collection bidding
we observe shares structural similarities with high-frequency market making in equities
(Menkveld, 2013; Hagstr¨omer and Nord´en, 2013), where heterogeneous intermediaries operate across multiple venues and securities. We test for liquidity commonality following
Chordia et al. (2000) and Hasbrouck and Seppi (2001), finding that when common market makers reduce their activity, liquidity deteriorates simultaneously across collections –

illiquidity contagion mechanism of Cespa and Foucault (2014), and the model of financially constrained intermediaries operating across multiple assets in Gromb and Vayanos

Third, the CryptoPunks/WrappedCryptoPunks comparison – identical assets under
two co-existing market structures – provides evidence on how market design shapes liquidity for unique assets. The native CryptoPunks marketplace, where buyers and sellers
post token-specific quotes bilaterally, resembles the decentralized search markets modeled
by Duffie et al. (2005): Traders must find counterparties, and matching frictions generate
endogenous bid-ask spreads. The standardized ERC-721 infrastructure, by contrast, centralizes liquidity through collection-level floor bids – effectively replacing bilateral search
with a pooled limit orderbook. Our comparison thus connects to the broader literature on
trading frictions in markets for heterogeneous durable goods (Gavazza, 2011). How market
structure affects trading costs is a central question in microstructure research (Madhavan, 2000; Biais et al., 2005); O’Hara (2003) argues that liquidity is a property of market
design, Amihud and Mendelson (1986) show that trading costs affect asset prices, and
Hendershott et al. (2011) use NYSE automation as a natural experiment to demonstrate
that algorithmic trading narrows spreads. In decentralized finance, Lehar and Parlour
(2025) show that liquidity providers on automated market makers (AMMs) face adverse
selection costs structurally similar to those of traditional intermediaries, and Hasbrouck
et al. (2024) demonstrate that fee design shapes the composition of informed and uninformed trading. Importantly, collection-level floor bids share a functional similarity with
AMM liquidity pools: Both aggregate capital across heterogeneous trading opportunities,
and both expose providers to adverse selection from better-informed counterparties. Our
setting offers a descriptive comparison of the same assets under two market structures operating simultaneously on the same blockchain; the observation that the native bilateral
mechanism achieves tighter quoted spreads than the standardized two-tier infrastructure
– though transaction-based price impact tells the opposite story – suggests that market
design choices – not merely tokenization – drive trading costs. And this consideration is
directly relevant to the broader agenda of tokenizing real-world assets (see Section 7.2).

Institutional Background
NFTs are unique digital assets recorded on a blockchain – a shared, tamper-resistant ledger
maintained by a decentralized network of computers. Unlike cryptocurrencies such as
Bitcoin or Ether, where every unit is interchangeable, each NFT is distinct and represents
ownership of a specific digital item, typically an image but also music, video, or virtual
land. This section provides the institutional context needed to interpret our empirical
analysis; readers familiar with blockchain-based markets may proceed to Section 3.
Ethereum and NFT standards.
Ethereum is the dominant blockchain for NFT issuance and trading. In 2018, the Ethereum community adopted ERC-721, a standard interface for creating, transferring, and trading non-fungible tokens. The standard ensures
interoperability: Any ERC-721 token can be listed, bid on, and traded on any compatible
marketplace (OpenSea, Blur, LooksRare, and others) without marketplace-specific integration. Because all tokens in a collection share the same smart contract, marketplaces
can offer buyers the option to place a single bid for any token in a collection, rather than
requiring bids on individual tokens – the foundation of the two-tier orderbook structure
we document.
NFT marketplaces.
Trading occurs on specialized platforms that maintain off-chain
orderbooks – databases of bids and asks hosted on the marketplace’s servers – while
settling completed transactions on the Ethereum blockchain. This avoids a blockchain
transaction for every order placed or canceled while preserving on-chain settlement for
actual trades.
Sample collections.
Our sample comprises six collections – five distinct projects plus
WrappedCryptoPunks, a standardized wrapper for CryptoPunks discussed below – that
span the major categories of the NFT market. Four are “profile-picture” (PFP) collections – series of algorithmically generated avatar images whose owners typically use them
6

as social-media profile pictures. Bored Ape Yacht Club (BAYC, 10 000 tokens, launched
April 2021) and Mutant Ape Yacht Club (MAYC, 20 000 tokens, August 2021) are issued by Yuga Labs and together represent the most actively traded PFP brand. Azuki
(10 000 tokens, January 2022) is an independent anime-inspired PFP project. CryptoPunks (10 000 tokens, June 2017) is the earliest major PFP collection and predates the
ERC-721 standard, as discussed below. Otherside (100 000 tokens, April 2022), also by
Yuga Labs, differs: Its tokens represent virtual land parcels in a metaverse environment
rather than profile pictures, resulting in a substantially larger supply and lower median
price. This category variation – four PFP collections of 10 000–20 000 tokens alongside one
virtual-land collection of 100 000 tokens – allows us to examine whether microstructure
patterns are specific to PFP markets or generalize across NFT asset types.
CryptoPunks: a pre-standard NFT.
CryptoPunks is a collection of 10 000 algorithmically generated pixel-art characters that was launched in June 2017, before the
adoption of the ERC-721 standard. Its smart contract implements a proprietary marketplace with token-specific bids and asks – a bilateral structure in which each order
references a specific token, and buyers and sellers must find counterparties individually.
Because this proprietary contract does not conform to ERC-721, CryptoPunks cannot be
traded on standard NFT marketplaces. The WrappedCryptoPunks contract addresses
this limitation: Owners deposit their CryptoPunk into a wrapper contract that issues an
ERC-721 token in return, enabling trading on any compatible marketplace. The process
is permissionless and reversible. The co-existence of these two market structures – native
bilateral trading and standardized two-tier trading – for the same 10 000 underlying assets
provides a natural experiment for examining how market design affects liquidity, which
we exploit in Section 7.2.

3.2
Sample Construction
We clean the raw API output by removing duplicate events, identified through their
unique combination of order identifier, event type, and timestamp. We further exclude
tokens and collections flagged as spam by the Reservoir API and normalize all records to
a common schema.
We then reconstruct token-level orderbooks by processing events sequentially for each
token within a collection. At each point in time, we track the best bid (highest active buy
order) and best ask (lowest active sell listing), yielding a time series of orderbook snapshots. Because the Reservoir API does not report order expiration or nonce invalidation
events, we apply a heuristic staleness filter that removes orders older than seven days from
the active book. A sensitivity analysis varying the cutoff (7, 14, 30 days, and no filter)
shows that the seven-day filter reduces the incidence of negative spreads – mechanically
arising from stale bids exceeding the current ask – from 4.7% to 1.4% for BAYC, while results for other collections are largely insensitive to the choice of cutoff. Between 28% and
51% of all bid events carry no token identifier across four collections (and nearly 100% for
WrappedCryptoPunks), indicating collection-level offers that apply to any token rather
than a specific one.6 Because the API does not report an order.criteria field, we cannot distinguish trait-filtered criteria orders from true collection-wide bids; our floor bid
measure therefore represents an upper bound on unconditional collection-wide liquidity
(see footnote 6). These collection-level bids are excluded from the token-level orderbook
but form the lower tier of what we call the two-tier orderbook, formalized in Section 4.
We refer to the highest such collection-level buy order as the floor bid – the best available
collection-level buy price, representing the pooled buy-side demand that sellers can access
without attracting a token-specific bid. (This is distinct from the conventional NFT floor
price, which denotes the lowest active ask, i.e., the cheapest token available for purchase.)
6Some bids without a token identifier may be criteria orders – offers restricted to tokens with specific
traits (e.g., a particular background color) rather than any token in the collection. The Reservoir API
activity export does not include an order.criteria field, so we cannot distinguish trait-filtered orders
from true collection-wide bids. If a share of these bids is trait-restricted rather than unconditional, our
floor bid measure may overstate true collection-wide liquidity. However, the core finding – that the floor
bid constitutes the effective best bid in the vast majority of snapshots – is conservative in this respect:
Trait-restricted bids apply to subsets of tokens, so treating them as collection-wide bids, if anything,
overstates competition for less common tokens and understates it for common ones, without affecting
the aggregate dominance of the lower tier.

A Framework for the Two-Tier Orderbook
This section develops a framework for the two-tier orderbook – a market structure that
arises when buyers can place bids applicable to any asset within a category rather than
targeting individual items. NFT marketplaces support two types of buy orders: tokenspecific bids targeting individual tokens and collection-level bids applicable to any token
in a collection (see Section 2). This structure creates endogenous fungibility: collectionlevel bids treat heterogeneous tokens as interchangeable at the collection floor, pooling
buy-side liquidity but generating an adverse selection problem that has no direct analogue
in standard microstructure settings. We formalize this environment and derive testable
predictions that guide the empirical analysis in Sections 5 to 7.
Setup.
Consider a collection of N tokens indexed i = 1, . . . , N. Each token has
fundamental value
vi = vC + εi,

where vC > 0 is the common value – the value of collection membership shared by all
tokens – and εi ≥0 is an idiosyncratic premium reflecting individual token attributes
such as rare visual traits or notable provenance. The non-negativity assumption reflects
the institutional structure: In principle, any token can be sold to the collection-level bid
at price pC, establishing vC as an approximate lower bound on traded values; tokens
worth less than the collection floor would not participate in the market. In practice,
the constraint is an approximation: For some tokens, attributes such as widely disliked
visual traits may depress value below vC, yielding εi < 0 in economic terms. Relaxing
non-negativity strengthens the adverse selection prediction, as negative-premium tokens
are the first to be tendered into collection-level bids. The data are consistent with this
possibility: in MAYC and Otherside, the median collection-level bid exceeds the median sale price (Table 3, Panel A), and crossed quotes – where the floor bid exceeds the
token-specific ask – affect 55–93% of two-sided snapshots (Panel B), suggesting that a
substantial share of tokens carry negative idiosyncratic premia. The premiums are drawn
independently from a distribution F with mean µε and variance σ2
ε; the latter measures
within-collection heterogeneity.
Two types of buy orders co-exist: Token-specific bidders observe the full value vi of
a particular token and submit bids for that token alone. Collection-level bidders observe
only vC and submit a single bid pC applicable to any token in the collection; pC corresponds
to the empirically observed collection-level floor bid.
The seller of token i faces both
types of offers and accepts the highest available bid. Collection-level bidding is rational
when evaluating individual tokens is costly: With N = 10,000 tokens, estimating each εi
requires per-token due diligence on visual traits, provenance, and recent comparable sales.
A bidder who forgoes this screening saves evaluation costs at the expense of the adverse
selection discount derived below.
Bidder entry.
Let c > 0 denote the per-token evaluation cost. A bidder who pays c
learns εi for a particular token and can bid its full value vi, earning zero expected trading profit in a competitive market. A collection-level bidder avoids this cost but earns
expected profit
πC = vC + E[εi | seller accepts pC] −pC,

which is negative whenever pC does not fully reflect the adverse selection discount –
that is, whenever the bidder pays more than the adversely selected token is worth. In
equilibrium, the bidder chooses collection-level bidding when the saved evaluation cost
exceeds the expected adverse selection loss: c > µε −E[εi | accept]. The adverse selection
cost µε −E[εi | accept] is increasing in σ2
ε – greater within-collection heterogeneity widens
the gap between the average token and the adversely selected token. The entry condition
thus predicts that collections with high heterogeneity (e.g., those with rare one-of-a-kind
attributes) should attract proportionally more token-specific bidding, while homogeneous
collections should be dominated by collection-level bids. Our data confirm that the most
active market makers – those placing thousands of bids per day – bid almost exclusively
at the collection level (see Section 6.3), consistent with this trade-off.
Adverse selection.
The collection-level bidder faces a selection problem similar to
the lemons market (Akerlof, 1970): A seller accepts the collection bid pC only when it
exceeds all competing token-specific bids for that seller’s token. Tokens with high εi are
more likely to attract token-specific bids above pC, so the tokens actually delivered to
collection-level bidders are drawn disproportionately from the lower tail of the εi distribution:
E[εi | seller accepts pC] < µε.
(3)
Unlike the canonical settings of Glosten and Milgrom (1985) and Easley and O’Hara
(1987), where adverse selection stems from uncertainty about a single asset’s payoff, the
information asymmetry here concerns which asset the bidder will receive. Informed sellers,
not informed buyers, drive the selection problem. This yields three predictions which we
test in Sections 5.2 and 6.3:
Prediction 1 (Adverse selection costs). Equation (3) implies that tokens sold into
collection-level bids should be of below-average quality – less rare items that fail to attract
token-specific bids above pC. This cross-sectional selection, together with the markettiming exposure inherent in standing uniform-price offers, should produce negative postpurchase returns on average. Rational bidders anticipate these costs and shade their bids
below E[vi]; the discount should increase with within-collection heterogeneity σ2
ε, since
greater dispersion in εi amplifies the lemons problem.
14

Prediction 2 (Spread reduction). Because collection-level bids pool demand across
all tokens, they create a common bid floor that narrows spreads relative to the fragmented
token-specific orderbook. The effect should be largest where token-specific bidding is thin.
Prediction 3 (Attenuation through token-specific bidding). Where token-specific
bids are prevalent, sellers of high-εi tokens can bypass the collection bid, weakening the
lemons mechanism. Adverse selection costs should therefore be lower in collections with
active token-level competition.
Limitations.
The framework is static – while the entry condition endogenizes the
choice between collection-level and token-specific bidding, it abstracts from strategic bidupdating, dynamic learning, and competition among bidders of the same type. Its purpose
is to isolate the adverse selection mechanism – uncertainty about which token will be
delivered – and to discipline the empirical analysis that follows.

The Two-Tier Orderbook

The two-tier orderbook has no direct analogue in traditional equity markets. The
closest concept is the relationship between sector ETFs and their constituent stocks: Just
as an ETF market maker provides a single price for a basket of heterogeneous stocks,
a collection-level bidder offers a single price for any token in a heterogeneous collection.
However, in equity markets the ETF and its constituents trade on separate exchanges
with formal creation-redemption mechanisms that enforce price convergence, whereas in
NFT markets the two tiers coexist within a single marketplace infrastructure and no arbitrage mechanism links the collection floor to individual token prices. In effect, the lower
tier constitutes a standard limit-order market for a synthetic fungible asset – amenable
to classical spread decomposition and adverse selection analysis – while the upper tier
remains a fragmented search market where standard measures break down due to thin,
non-continuous trading. Having established how the two-tier orderbook reshapes measured spreads, we next assess whether the various liquidity measures yield consistent
signals.

6.3
Adverse Selection
These behavioral patterns characterize how market makers operate, and the natural next
question is what costs their collection-level bids impose. Collection-level bidding creates
a lemons problem (as formalized in Section 4, Equation (3)). Characteristically, the floor
bid is a uniform offer for any token in a collection, yet individual tokens differ in quality
and value.
Since bids are binding signed orders, the MM cannot screen or reject an
incoming sale once a seller accepts – the seller retains full discretion over which token to
sell. A seller who accepts such a bid is therefore likely selling a below-average token, one
whose value is at or below the floor bid price (Prediction 1).
We proxy for the costs of adverse selection through post-purchase price impact, i.e.,
the change in the daily median sale price index at horizons of 1, 5, and 10 calendar days
following each MM purchase.16 If MMs bear adverse selection costs, prices should decline
after their purchases – indicating that MMs tend to buy just before the market moves
against them. Note that this collection-level return measure captures both true adverse
selection (receiving below-average tokens) and inventory risk (buying into market-wide
declines); the token-level rarity test below isolates the former channel.

6.4
Liquidity Commonality
The overlap of market makers across collections raises a natural question: Do their shared
activities generate correlated liquidity fluctuations? In equity markets, such liquidity commonality is well documented. Chordia et al. (2000) show that individual stock liquidity
co-moves with market-wide and industry-wide liquidity, suggesting common supply-side
factors; Chordia et al. (2001) link aggregate liquidity to trading activity and volatility,
Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) provide a theoretical underpinning through funding
constraints, and Karolyi et al. (2012) extend the analysis globally. Whether such commonality extends to NFT markets, where the cross-section is small and a handful of shared
participants dominate liquidity provision, is an open question.

though the comparison is imperfect: Their measures (quoted spreads and depth) differ
from ours, their sample covers a single centralized exchange, and the leave-one-out market
average is computed over hundreds of stocks rather than our five collections, where each
leave-one-out market proxy averages over only four peers – making it far less diversified
and more sensitive to idiosyncratic shocks in any single collection. The three remaining
liquidity measures show weaker commonality (R2 below 1%), indicating that co-movement
is concentrated in the participation channel rather than in aggregate bidding or trading
activity. This pattern is in line with the mechanism suggested by the cross-collection MM
overlap documented in Section 6.1: A small number of shared market makers transmit
activity shocks across collections, but the transmission is narrow, affecting who bids rather
28

than how much or at what price.
Three of the four liquidity measures – bid count,
bid value, and active bidders – are bid-based and therefore largely unaffected by wash
trading, which manifests on the sales side (Section 7.4). Sale count is the exception, but
its commonality is the weakest of the four (R2 < 1%) and does not drive any of the main
conclusions.
Professional market makers thus exist in NFT markets – concentrated, fast, and algorithmic. Yet they face adverse selection because the collection-level bid, their primary tool,
cannot discriminate between high- and low-value tokens within a collection. The tension
between the fungible component of value (collection membership) and the non-fungible
component (individual token characteristics) shapes both the structure of liquidity provision and the costs it entails. That market makers persist despite these costs implies that
the bid-ask spread – widened by the bid shading documented in Section 5.2 – provides sufficient compensation on average, just as in equity markets where the spread compensates
designated market makers for adverse selection risk (Glosten and Milgrom, 1985).

What explains the variation in market quality across collections, time, and market structures? and a robustness check on market integrity through wash trading detection (Section 7.4).

7.4
Market Integrity: Wash Trading
Wash trading – simultaneously acting as buyer and seller to inflate apparent activity – is a
documented concern in cryptocurrency markets (Gandal et al., 2018; Cong et al., 2023).
In NFT markets, where marketplace incentives such as token airdrops reward trading
volume, the potential is evident. We apply heuristic-based detection methods following
Oh (2024) – building on earlier NFT wash trading research by Wachter et al. (2023) – to
assess the prevalence and marketplace-driven nature of wash trading in our sample. This
analysis serves as a robustness check rather than a causal investigation: The heuristics
flag potentially suspicious trades but cannot determine intent.
We apply four heuristics. H1 identifies self-trades: transactions where the buyer and
seller are the same address. H2 flags round-trip trades: cases where address A sells to
address B and B sells back to A within seven days. H3 flags high-volume pairs: buyer-seller
pairs with five or more transactions between them. H4 identifies same-block round-trips:
trades completed within 60 seconds or fewer. A sale is flagged if it triggers any of the four
heuristics.
37

Flagging rates can be seen in Panel A of Table 11 and range widely: from 3.2% (Otherside) to 57.0% (WrappedCryptoPunks), with MAYC (47.5%), Azuki (44.5%), and BAYC
(17.3%) in between and CryptoPunks at 10.0%. H3 (high-volume pairs) is the dominant
heuristic for most collections: Flagged activity concentrates among repeat trader pairs
rather than same-address or same-block transactions. Self-trades (H1) are rare (39 for
BAYC, zero for CryptoPunks and Otherside), and same-block round-trips (H4) are nearzero across all collections (32 flagged trades total), indicating that the most mechanically
obvious form of wash trading is uncommon in practice. The USD volume share from
flagged trades closely tracks the flagged sales share, from 3.1% (CryptoPunks) to 59.3%
(WrappedCryptoPunks).
Panel B disaggregates by marketplace, and Figure 8 visualizes the cross-marketplace
contrast, plotting flagged sales and volume shares side by side for each venue. For four of
five collections, Blur flagging rates exceed 39%, reaching 58.9% for WrappedCryptoPunks.
OpenSea rates are consistently below 4% (MAYC: 52.0% on Blur vs. 2.1% on OpenSea;
BAYC: 39.7% vs. 1.1%; Azuki: 46.1% vs. 2.5%). The exception is Otherside, where only
3.5% of Blur sales are flagged, presumably because the low average token value reduces
the profitability of airdrop-driven wash trading. CryptoPunks, which trades exclusively
on its native marketplace, shows a 10.0% flagging rate.
The marketplace-driven pattern points to incentive-based wash trading rather than
individual manipulation. During our sample period, Blur distributed governance tokens
(the BLUR airdrop) proportional to users’ trading activity, creating a direct financial
incentive to inflate volume. This dynamic reflects the two-sided platform logic analyzed by
Rochet and Tirole (2003): By subsidizing one side through token rewards, the marketplace
attracts activity, but a substantial share proves artificial. The concentration of flagged
trades on Blur across most collections, combined with near-zero flagging on OpenSea
(which lacked a comparable volume-based incentive), identifies the airdrop program as
the primary structural driver.
Next, we consider Blur’s marketplace entry as a natural experiment. For BAYC and
WrappedCryptoPunks, our sample spans both pre- and post-Blur periods, enabling a before/after comparison. Table A11 reports the results. Before Blur’s launch in October

false negatives (sophisticated wash traders using multiple addresses). Flagged trades are

of identified market maker purchases overlap with wash trade flags across collections, and

Conclusion
This paper provides the first comprehensive analysis of NFT market microstructure using bid-side orderbook data. The central finding is that NFT markets have developed
a two-tier orderbook in which collection-level floor bids co-exist with token-specific orders and constitute the dominant source of buy-side liquidity – a structure absent from
traditional securities markets. Because collection-level bids cannot discriminate between
high- and low-value tokens, the market makers who supply them face adverse selection:
Post-purchase price impact ranges from −3% to −7% at ten days, driven primarily by
market timing rather than token-level quality differences. Collection identity accounts for
the largest share of spread variation in panel regressions, absorbing over 30 percentage
points of R2.
These findings carry practical implications. Platform incentive design has first-order
consequences for market integrity: Volume-based airdrop programs coincide with wash
trading rates of 40–59% on Blur versus 1–4% on OpenSea.
For investors, token-level
quoted spreads of 48–80% (up to 200% for the least liquid collections) – though floorbenchmark spreads are substantially lower at 2–5% for the most liquid collections – represent a material friction largely invisible to retail participants accustomed to equitymarket spreads of 0.1%, and improving the visibility of realized transaction costs on NFT
marketplaces could reduce this information gap.
Our results also contribute to the broader agenda of tokenizing heterogeneous realworld assets. A natural experiment comparing CryptoPunks (native marketplace, tokenspecific bidding) with WrappedCryptoPunks (standardized ERC-721 infrastructure) shows
40

that the two-tier structure inflates measured quoted spreads while generating far greater
bidding activity and substantially lower transaction-based price impact. The form of bidding may matter as much as its volume: Blockchain-based trading infrastructure alone
does not resolve the illiquidity inherent to heterogeneous assets.
From a wider perspective, our findings address a question that traditional securities
data cannot answer: how markets create liquidity when every asset is unique. The mechanism we document – endogenous creation of fungibility through category-level bidding,
generating pooled liquidity at the cost of adverse selection – is invisible in equity markets,
where fungibility is given by construction. The finding that collection identity dominates
all other spread determinants suggests that, for heterogeneous assets, the relevant unit of
liquidity may be the category rather than the individual asset – a result that standard
microstructure models, built for fungible securities, do not predict.
There are some limitations: Our sample covers six high-value Ethereum-based profilepicture collections; generalizability to other NFT categories or blockchains remains an
open question. The spread determinant regressions rely on contemporaneous regressors
and should be interpreted as conditional correlations, and Newey-West standard errors
correct for serial correlation but not for cross-sectional dependence. The data provider
does not distinguish trait-filtered criteria orders from true collection-wide bids (Footnote 6), so our floor bid measure represents an upper bound on unconditional collectionwide liquidity.
These limitations suggest directions for future work: A formal causal analysis exploiting the timing of marketplace incentive changes could identify the precise mechanisms
through which platform design drives wash trading. Extending the two-tier orderbook
framework to art, gaming, and music NFTs – or to non-Ethereum blockchains – would
test its generality. Ultimately, the two-tier orderbook, in which market makers price categories rather than individual assets, may represent the equilibrium market structure for
any asset class characterized by within-category heterogeneity, from tokenized real estate
to collectible markets.
