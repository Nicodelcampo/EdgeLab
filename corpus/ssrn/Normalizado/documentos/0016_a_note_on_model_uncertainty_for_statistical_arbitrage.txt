A note on model uncertainty for statistical
arbitrage
Daisuke Yoshikawa∗
January 5, 2023
Abstract
This study considers an optimal stopping problem that addresses
model uncertainty.
Model uncertainty is the uncertainty affecting
model assumptions, e.g., the assumed form of the probability distribution and the parameters embedded in the probability distribution. The results presented in this study show the explicit form of the
boundary indicating the optimal stopping time, assuming the portfolio value as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Furthermore, the boundary can be regulated depending on the ambiguity of the estimated
model. Thus, the boundary is more robust than is a boundary derived without considering model uncertainty. In this sense, applying
our method might make statistical arbitrage more robust because the
trading code for statistical arbitrage often depends on the statistical
test, which might lead to incorrect estimation. We also show that the
value function for the optimal stopping problem that addresses model
uncertainty has a structure consistent with the certainty equivalent,
by which we derive the risk premium in the context of expected utility
with risk rather than model uncertainty.
Keywords
Kullback-Leibler divergence, relative entropy, optimal stopping,
robustness, certainty equivalent, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process

Introduction
This study presents an optimal boundary for the optimal stopping problem
that accounts for model uncertainty. Model uncertainty is the uncertainty
affecting model assumptions, e.g., the assumed form of the probability distribution and the parameters embedded in the probability distribution. We
can use this boundary to make a statistical arbitrage strategy more robust.
According to [18], the term “statistical arbitrage” was first used in the
1990s as a sobriquet elaborated from pairs trading, developed by Nunzio
Tartaglia’s trading group at Morgan Stanley in the 1980s. The essence of
statistical arbitrage is the pursuit of stable profits. Indeed, pairs trading,
the simplest form of statical arbitrage, is executed by taking long and short
positions of stocks with similar movements. If one stock depreciates, then the
appreciation of another stock makes the portfolio stable. Statistical arbitrage
inherits this spirit.
While statistical arbitrage pursues stable profits, the implication of “stability” is not necessarily clear. In response to this situation, [13] provides a
formal definition of statistical arbitrage; 1 i.e., there is an opportunity for
statistical arbitrage in the market if a profit can be gained without facing a
loss for a sufficiently long, possibly infinite, time horizon. For example, if an
individual bets on a coin flip, where the odds of it coming up heads are 51%
and the odds of it coming up tails are 49%, then he or she can keep betting
on heads for an infinite number of entries and not lose. The above example
may provide an intuition of a statistical arbitrage opportunity.
Of course, in practice, the coin flip cannot be infinitely continued, and
the odds of it coming up heads or tails might be incorrectly recognized. Even
though it is possible to find a flip game with a biased coin, sometimes, this
game must come to an end. If the odds of the coin flip coming up heads
or tails is incorrectly recognized, then this recognition could lead to a result
opposite of that expected.
A similar problem appears in actual financial
practice.
According to [11, 12, 6, 5], if the portfolio value is stationary, then it
is possible to pursue statistical arbitrage.
The probability of coming up
with heads or tails in a coin flip corresponds to the model parameters of
stationary portfolio processes in financial practice. Thus, we have to consider

model parameters’ misspecification to construct a trading code for statistical
arbitrage. Furthermore, we must construct this trading code so that it works
well in a finite time horizon. In summary, our issues are model uncertainty
and finite time horizon.
As we describe in Section 3, the above trading code consists of the meanreversion point of the stationary portfolio process and the most diverted
point from the mean-reversion point. We usually derive the former point by
calibrating the model parameters, which might lead to misspecification. We
can derive the latter point by solving the optimal stopping problem using
the calibrated model parameters. As in previous works, [20] formulates the
optimal stopping problem in the infinite time horizon, taking into account
model uncertainty,2 and [15] identifies the latter point by solving the optimal stopping problem in the finite time horizon without considering model
uncertainty.
Our contribution in this study is to present the most diverted point from
the mean-reversion point by solving the optimal stopping problem in a finite
time horizon, taking into account model uncertainty.
More precisely, we utilize the method of [3], who discuss it in the context
of convex risk measures. Specifically, we replace the estimated model with
a model similar to but different from the estimated model. If we are sure
about the estimation, then we adopt a model that is very similar to the
estimated model. If not, then we adopt a very different scenario incorporating
the worst-case scenario, thus reducing profit.
This policy may make the
trading code robust. In this formulation, we use Kullback–Leibler divergence
to describe the similarity between models and incorporate the minimizing
principle related to robustness.
As a result, we can obtain the explicit form of the solution to the boundary
implying the maximum expected value of the portfolio, which can be used as
a benchmark to take a position. Furthermore, we show that the implication
of this type of problem is consistent with the certainty equivalent, which
leads to the risk premium in the context of expected utility with risk. This
property might induce a clear connection between the contexts of risk and
uncertainty.

Implications
Let us consider an optimal stopping problem ess supτ∈St,T EQ[Yτ|Ft] for probability Q. If we know probability P, then we can apply Q = P and derive
the optimal stopping time. If we do not know the true probability, then we
estimate P. Then, if we believe that the estimate is certain, we again apply
Q = P. However, if we are not necessarily convinced of our estimate, then
we might hesitate to immediately apply Q = P. Then, how would we choose
Q for which we have numerous candidates? This sort of uncertainty is often
called model uncertainty. One criterion is to choose the Q that minimizes
EQ[Yτ|Ft], where Q is equivalent to P because, even if the estimation of P is
incorrect, the probability of Q generating a worst-case scenario may relieve
the impact of the misspecification of P. That is, these criteria lead to the robust selection of Q. Although we are not perfectly certain of our estimate of
P, we are willing to partially utilize the estimate depending on our confidence
in it; i.e., if we are highly confident in our estimate of P, then we choose a
Q relatively similar to P, simultaneously minimizing EQ[Yτ|Ft]. In contrast,
5
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4110785

if we are less confident in our estimate, then we choose a Q relatively less
similar to P, simultaneously minimizing EQ[Yτ|Ft].
The problem setting of (1) reflects the above situation. Here, EQ[log(dQ/dP)|Ft]
is called Kullback–Leibler divergence or relative entropy. Kullback–Leibler
divergence is always positive and is zero if and only if Q = P (Theorem 1.4.1
of [14]). Due to this property, Kullback–Leibler divergence is considered to
express the similarity between probability measures.
As we state above, if the agents believe that their estimate is correct, then
they choose a Q similar to P, and vice versa. The constant λ > 0 represents
the degree of agents’ confidence in their estimation. Indeed, if λ is close to
zero, then in problem (1), the weight of the second term EQ[log(dQ/dP)|Ft]/λ
increases relative to the first term EQ[Yτ|Ft]. This situation induces the selection of a Q similar to P to minimize Kullback–Leibler divergence. In contrast,
a larger λ value decreases the weight of the second term, thus inducing the
robust selection of Q minimizing EQ[Yτ|Ft], which might be less similar to P.
As a form of financial practice, this problem provides a trading code
for statistical arbitrage. Following [13]’s formulation of statistical arbitrage,
if the portfolio value is stationary, then statistical arbitrage is possible, as
shown by several authors, such as [12, 11, 6, 5]. Indeed, if the portfolio value
is stationary, then it moves around the mean-reversion point. Thus, we can
construct a strategy to pursue statistical arbitrage by specifying the meanreversion point and the most divergent point from the mean-reversion point.
For example, we may take a position when the portfolio value touches the
most divergent point from the mean-reversion point and liquidate it when
the portfolio value converges to the mean-reversion point.
In this study, we apply the Onsenstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process for (2),
similar to [9] and [2]. Thus, X is stationary. We can estimate the meanreversion point by regarding X as the portfolio value.
The main results indicate the most divergent point. Since Vλ(t, x) of (1)
is essentially the expected value of the portfolio, if Vλ(t, x) were higher than
the current portfolio value of Xt, then the portfolio value would increase if
the investor waits. In other words, Vλ(t, x) = x implies the time to attain the
expected maximum value of X. The boundary bx(t) given in (4) identifies the
maximum value since Vλ(t, bx(t)) = bx(t). Thus, bx(t) characterizes the most
divergent point.

estimate the model parameters. A larger λ value implies anxiety regarding
the agent’s misspecification of the parameters. The minimum value λ = 0.0
means that the agent is perfectly confident in his or her estimation.

5
Concluding remarks
In this study, we show the explicit form of the optimal boundary while addressing model uncertainty. This boundary may represent a relevant factor
for statistical arbitrage. The optimal boundary presented in this study might
be the robust trigger for taking a position on which to conduct statistical arbitrage. The value function given by the optimal stopping problem has a
similar structure to the certainty equivalent. This structure may clarify the
discussion of ambiguity by utilizing the concept of risk.
Although we identify the boundary while accounting for model uncertainty, we have yet to show the actual performance of the trading code presented in this study. To accomplish this, we have a future research project:
i.e., we execute an empirical study using actual market data. We also need to
account for transaction costs in the empirical study. Due to the properties of
statistical arbitrage, transaction costs tend to increase with each transaction.
Indeed, pairs trading, the minimum scale of statistical arbitrage, requires taking a long and a short position to make a stable but relatively small profit.
Since statistical arbitrage often has a larger scale than does pairs trading,
the former generates higher transaction costs, which cancel out the profits of
statistical arbitrage. The robust trading code presented in this work plays
an essential role in the empirical study accounting for transaction costs.
