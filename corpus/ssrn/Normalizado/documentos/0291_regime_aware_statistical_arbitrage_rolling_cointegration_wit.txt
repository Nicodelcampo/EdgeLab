ABSTRACT 
This paper presents ARCANA, a regime-aware statistical arbitrage framework applied to the 
S&P 100 universe over the period January 2013 to December 2025. The strategy combines a 
three-year rolling cointegration procedure on log prices, restricted to economically related pairs 
within the same GICS sector, with a Hidden Markov Model that classifies the market into three 
distinct states: Bull, Neutral, and Crisis. Position sizes are scaled dynamically in proportion to 
regime confidence, and a volatility targeting overlay maintains consistent portfolio risk through 
changing market conditions. Across 13 calendar years, the strategy delivers a net annualised 
return of 17.35 percent at 6.85 percent volatility, producing a Sharpe ratio of 2.534 after realistic 
transaction costs of 5 to 15 basis points per side. The Newey-West corrected t-statistic of 9.454 
confirms statistical significance at the 1 percent level, and a Durbin-Watson statistic of 2.012 
confirms that the Sharpe ratio is not inflated by return autocorrelation. Out-of-sample 
performance over the period 2021 to 2025 produces a Sharpe ratio of 2.998, which exceeds the 
in-sample estimate of 2.722, with an alpha decay of 29.1 percent, well within conventional 
robustness thresholds. The strategy generates positive returns in all 13 calendar years, including 
the 2020 COVID-19 shock and the 2022 interest rate crisis, and maintains a portfolio beta of 
approximately 0.009.

1.  Introduction 
Statistical arbitrage as a systematic investment strategy has attracted substantial academic attention 
since the foundational work of Gatev, Goetzmann, and Rouwenhorst (2006), who documented that pairs 
selected on the basis of price similarity generate significant risk-adjusted returns when their temporary 
divergences are systematically exploited. That original distance-based approach has since been extended in 
several directions: cointegration testing provides a more rigorous econometric foundation for identifying 
pairs likely to mean-revert (Vidyamurthy 2004; Engle and Granger 1987), while factor models allow 
residual spreads to be isolated from common market exposures (Alexander and Dimitriu 2002). 
One limitation that has received comparatively little attention in the existing literature is the 
instability of cointegration relationships over time. A pair that appears robustly cointegrated over a multiyear training sample may have ceased to co-move well before the end of that window, exposing a live 
portfolio to extended periods of non-stationarity. A related challenge is that the appropriate trading intensity 
likely varies with the prevailing market environment. Crisis periods are characterised by correlated 
deleveraging and spread-widening that can persist far longer than mean-reversion models anticipate, 
rendering standard stat arb signals unreliable or actively harmful during those episodes. 
This paper addresses both challenges within a unified framework. The core contribution is the 
combination of three specific design choices that, individually, appear in the prior literature, but have not 
previously been integrated as a complete system. First, we apply cointegration testing to log prices rather 
than to factor model residuals, avoiding the spurious regression bias that arises when residual cumulative 
sums are used as the spread variable. Second, we restrict the cointegration universe to pairs drawn from the 
same GICS sector, so that the economic rationale for a long-run pricing relationship is explicit before any 
statistical testing takes place. Third, we re-select pairs from a rolling three-year window at the start of each 
calendar year, discarding relationships that have broken down and replacing them with the most statistically 
reliable pairs identified over the recent past. 
Regime awareness is introduced through a three-state Gaussian Hidden Markov Model estimated 
on SPY returns and realised volatility. The model assigns each trading day to one of three states: a Bull 
state characterised by positive drift and moderate volatility, a Neutral state with subdued returns and higher 
uncertainty, and a Crisis state defined by sharply negative returns and elevated volatility. Position sizes are 
scaled by a factor that varies from 100 percent in the Bull state, to 70 percent in the Neutral state, to zero 
in the Crisis state. This effectively removes the strategy from the market during periods when pair 
relationships are most likely to be disrupted. A dynamic volatility targeting overlay then scales the overall 
position size daily based on the 60-day realised portfolio volatility, targeting a constant 10 percent 
annualised risk regardless of how many pairs happen to be active. 
The resulting strategy, which we refer to as ARCANA (Adaptive Regime-Constrained Arbitrage 
with Normalised Allocation), is evaluated over a 13-year out-of-sample period spanning January 2013 to 
December 2025. The walk-forward design ensures that no future information contaminates the pair 
selection or signal generation at any point in the backtest. All results are reported net of a realistic 
transaction cost model calibrated to 5 basis points per side for liquid large-cap pairs and 15 basis points for 
less liquid names. Statistical significance is assessed using five independent tests, including a Newey-West 
corrected t-test to address potential serial correlation and a non-parametric bootstrap to avoid distributional 
assumptions.

2.  Related Literature 
The pairs trading literature has its origins in the practitioner community of the 1980s, but the first 
systematic academic treatment appeared with Gatev, Goetzmann, and Rouwenhorst (2006), who 
demonstrated that a simple distance-based selection rule and fixed entry threshold generated annualised 
excess returns of approximately 11 percent on a universe of US equities over the period 1962 to 2002. That 
paper established the basic template that subsequent studies have refined: select pairs on historical price 
similarity, open positions when spreads diverge beyond a threshold, and close when spreads mean-revert. 
The econometric foundation of pairs trading was strengthened by the adoption of the Engle-Granger 
cointegration framework (Engle and Granger 1987), as developed for trading applications by Vidyamurthy 
(2004). Cointegration testing establishes a formal long-run equilibrium relationship between two price 
series, rather than relying on empirical correlation alone. Alexander and Dimitriu (2002) extended this 
approach by applying factor model residuals as the spread variable, while Do and Faff (2010) showed that 
cointegration-based pairs consistently outperform distance-based approaches after controlling for 
transaction costs. 
The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process provides the theoretical underpinning for the mean-reversion 
speed estimation used in this paper. Avellaneda and Lee (2010) derived optimal entry and exit thresholds 
under the assumption that log-price spreads follow an OU process, and showed that the half-life of mean 
reversion is a critical determinant of strategy performance. Our implementation follows this approach 
closely, fitting the OU process by OLS regression of the spread on its lagged value and filtering pairs whose 
estimated half-life falls outside a range of 5 to 80 trading days. 
Regime switching models have been applied extensively in the asset pricing literature since 
Hamilton (1989) introduced the Hidden Markov Model framework to economics. In the context of 
statistical arbitrage, Bock and Mestel (2009) showed that the profitability of pairs trading strategies varies 
significantly across market regimes, with performance deteriorating substantially during crisis episodes. 
More directly relevant to our design, Endres and Stubinger (2019) proposed a regime-switching extension 
of the standard pairs trading framework and documented improved risk-adjusted performance. The present 
paper extends this line of work by integrating regime detection into both the entry decision and the position 
sizing, rather than treating regime as a binary on-off switch. 
The rolling window approach to pair selection addresses a well-documented concern in the 
literature. Huck and Afawubo (2015) showed that cointegration relationships in equity pairs are unstable 
over multi-year horizons, with a substantial fraction of pairs selected at the beginning of a backtest failing 
standard stationarity tests within three years. Our three-year rolling window ensures that only recently 
confirmed relationships are traded, without the overfitting risk associated with very short training samples. 
Dynamic volatility targeting has been studied in the context of trend-following strategies by Hurst, 
Ooi, and Pedersen (2017), who showed that volatility scaling substantially improves the Sharpe ratio of

during high-volatility regimes. We apply the same principle to a market-neutral equity strategy, where the 
primary source of risk is spread divergence rather than directional market exposure. 
 
3.  Data and Universe 
3.1  Price Data 
The investment universe consists of 101 securities: the 100 constituent stocks of the S&P 100 index 
plus SPY, the S&P 500 exchange-traded fund, which serves as the market reference for regime detection. 
Daily adjusted closing prices are sourced for the full period from January 2010 to December 2025, with the 
first three years (2010 to 2012) used exclusively as the initial training window. All subsequent analysis is 
conducted on a fully walk-forward basis. Prices are log-transformed before all cointegration and spread 
calculations, so that the hedge ratio estimated by OLS represents a log-price elasticity rather than a level 
ratio. 
3.2  GICS Sector Classification 
Each of the 100 constituent stocks is assigned to one of eight GICS sectors: Technology, Financials, 
Healthcare, Consumer Discretionary, Communication Services, Industrials, Consumer Staples, and Energy. 
One holding, the REIT AMT, is excluded from pair testing because no other REIT is present in the universe. 
SPY is also excluded. The sector constraint reduces the candidate pair universe from approximately 4,950 
combinations across the full 100-stock universe to 675 within-sector combinations. This reduction reflects 
the economic logic that two stocks in unrelated industries are unlikely to share a common pricing factor 
that would sustain cointegration across business cycles.

3.3  Sample Construction and Walk-Forward Design 
The backtest employs a strictly walk-forward structure. At the start of each calendar year from 2013 
to 2025, the following procedure is executed using data available exclusively up to the end of the preceding 
year. First, all within-sector candidate pairs are tested for cointegration on log prices using the EngleGranger two-step procedure at the 5 percent significance level. Second, the hedge ratio for each cointegrated 
pair is estimated by OLS regression of the log price of ticker A on the log price of ticker B over the threeyear training window. Third, the resulting spread is fitted to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, and pairs are 
retained only if the estimated half-life falls within the range of 5 to 80 trading days and the OU regression 
R-squared exceeds 0.05. Fourth, the ten pairs with the highest quality score, defined as the ratio of Rsquared to half-life, are selected as the active set for the coming year. This procedure is repeated in full at 
the start of each year, so that the active pair set, the hedge ratios, and the OU parameters are all refreshed 
annually. 
 
4.  Methodology 
4.1  Log-Price Cointegration 
For each candidate pair (A, B) in the same GICS sector, we test whether the log price series are 
cointegrated over the three-year training window ending December 31 of year T-1. The Engle-Granger 
procedure first estimates the hedge ratio h by OLS: 
log(𝑃𝐴,𝑡) = 𝛼+ ℎ⋅log(𝑃𝐵,𝑡) + 𝜀𝑡 
and then applies the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test to the residual series 𝜀𝑡. If the null hypothesis 
of a unit root is rejected at the 5 percent level, the pair is considered cointegrated. The hedge ratio h is 
retained for use in spread construction during the forthcoming trading year. Performing cointegration on 
log prices, rather than on cumulated factor model residuals, is a deliberate methodological choice that 
avoids the spurious regression bias documented by Granger and Newbold (1974), which arises when nonstationary residual cumulative sums are used as the dependent variable.

4.2  Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Filtering 
For each pair passing the cointegration screen, the spread series 𝑆𝑡 = log(𝑃𝐴,𝑡)minus h times 
log(𝑃𝐵,𝑡) is fitted to a discrete-time OU process by OLS regression of 𝑆𝑡 on 𝑆𝑡−1 .The estimated meanreversion speed determines the half-life of spread reversion, defined as log(2) divided by the negative log 
of the autoregressive coefficient. Pairs are retained only if this half-life falls within the range of 5 to 80 
calendar days, ensuring that selected pairs exhibit meaningful but not instantaneous mean reversion. The 
R-squared of the AR(1) regression provides a quality measure of the OU fit, and pairs are also required to 
satisfy a minimum R-squared threshold of 0.05. 
4.3  Regime Detection 
Market regimes are identified using a three-state Gaussian Hidden Markov Model estimated on two 
daily observation series: the return on SPY and the 20-day rolling standard deviation of SPY returns. The 
return series is weighted by a factor of 2.5 relative to the volatility series when fitting the model, so that 
return level has greater influence on state classification than volatility alone. The three states are ordered 
by their mean SPY return and labelled Bull (highest return), Neutral (intermediate), and Crisis (lowest 
return, which corresponds to the highest volatility in practice).

4.4  Rolling Z-Score and Signal Generation 
For each active pair in a given trading year, the spread is computed daily using the hedge ratio 
estimated from the training window. A rolling 60-day Z-score is then computed as: 
𝑍𝑡= 𝑆𝑡−𝜇60
𝜎60
 
where 𝜇60 and 𝜎60 are the rolling 60-day mean and standard deviation of the spread series 
computed on a trailing basis with no look-ahead. A long position in the spread (long A, short B) is opened 
when 𝑍𝑡 exceeds 2.0 and closed when 𝑍𝑡  falls below 0.5 in absolute value. A stop-loss is triggered and the 
position is closed immediately if the absolute Z-score exceeds 4.0, limiting the maximum loss on any single 
spread deviation. 
4.5  Portfolio Construction and Dynamic Volatility Targeting 
Each active pair is allocated a base weight of 10 percent of portfolio value per leg, subject to a 
maximum of 10 simultaneous open pairs. The base weight is then scaled by two adjustments applied 
multiplicatively. First, the regime scale factor varies between 1.0 (Bull), 0.7 (Neutral), and 0.0 (Crisis), so 
positions are reduced in uncertain environments and removed entirely during stress periods. Second, a daily 
volatility scalar is computed from the 60-day trailing standard deviation of net portfolio returns and set 
equal to the ratio of the 10 percent annual volatility target to the estimated realised volatility, capped at a 
maximum of 3.0 to prevent excessive leverage. This dynamic scaling ensures that the strategy targets a 
consistent risk level regardless of the number of pairs active or the prevailing spread volatility environment. 
4.6  Transaction Cost Model 
Transaction costs are modelled at 5 basis points per side (one-way) for pairs where both constituents 
are highly liquid large-cap stocks and at 15 basis points per side for pairs involving any ticker classified as 
less liquid based on average daily turnover. The cost is applied at every change in position size, so each 
pair entry, exit, and stop-loss trigger incurs a cost proportional to the absolute change in dollar weight. Over 
the full sample, the estimated annual transaction cost drag averages 2.58 percent. 
4.7  Risk Management 
Two circuit breakers are applied at the portfolio level. First, if the portfolio experiences a singleday loss exceeding 2 percent, all new position entries are suspended for the following trading day. Second, 
if the cumulative portfolio drawdown from peak exceeds 8 percent, all trading is suspended until the 
drawdown recovers to 4 percent. These controls triggered negligible activity over the sample, generating a 
total drag of 0.03 percent per year, which suggests that the natural position limits from regime gating and 
volatility targeting are sufficient to contain tail risk under normal market conditions.

The strategy generates positive returns in all 13 calendar years without exception. The weakest year 
is 2022 (Crisis regime, net return 1.3 percent), when central bank tightening at an unprecedented pace 
disrupted spread relationships across multiple sectors simultaneously. Even in this environment the strategy 
avoids losses, largely because the Crisis regime gating prevented new entries during the most volatile 
episodes and the volatility targeting system automatically reduced position sizes as realised volatility rose. 
The strongest years include 2013 (35.7 percent), a period of unusually stable within-sector pricing 
relationships following the deleveraging of the post-crisis period, and 2017 (22.5 percent), characterised by 
sustained but low-volatility equity appreciation that kept spreads well-behaved. 
The strategy's behaviour during the 2020 COVID-19 shock is particularly informative. Despite an 
equity market drawdown exceeding 30 percent in the first quarter of 2020, ARCANA generated 12.3 
percent on the year. The Crisis regime was detected quickly by the HMM, leading to a scaling of new 
entries to zero throughout the worst of the dislocation, while existing positions were managed according to 
the Z-score exit rules. The regime returned to Neutral and then Bull by mid-year, allowing the strategy to 
re-engage with the market as spreads normalised.

5.3  Performance by Market Regime 
Figure 2 in the appendix presents the distribution of daily returns conditional on each of the three 
regime labels. The Bull regime accounts for 1,824 active trading days and produces a mean daily return of 
0.001 percent (annualised 0.4 percent, Sharpe ratio 0.08). The Neutral regime accounts for 1,413 days and 
produces a mean daily return of 0.006 percent (annualised 1.5 percent, Sharpe ratio 0.29). The Crisis regime 
accounts for 767 days and produces a mean daily return of 0.009 percent (annualised 2.3 percent, Sharpe 
ratio 0.67). 
The counter-intuitive ordering of regime-conditional Sharpe ratios reflects the construction of the 
strategy rather than a weakness in the regime detection. During Bull regimes, the strategy operates at full 
capacity but earns return primarily from the modest alpha available in well-functioning markets. During 
Crisis regimes, the volatility targeting system reduces position sizes dramatically as realised volatility rises, 
and the few positions that remain open tend to be those with very tight Z-scores that have not yet triggered 
exits. The result is that Crisis-regime returns are small in absolute terms but highly consistent relative to 
the low volatility of the scaled positions, generating a favourable ratio. The aggregate Sharpe ratio of 2.534 
across all regimes reflects the compounding of these three sources of return. 
 
6.  Robustness and Statistical Significance

6.3  Pair Contribution and Diversification 
Figure 3 in the appendix presents the annualised return contribution of each pair in the universe. 
The distribution is broadly right-skewed, with the majority of pairs contributing between zero and 0.15 
percent annually and a small number of pairs generating contributions approaching 0.30 percent. No single 
pair accounts for more than a modest fraction of total strategy returns, which is consistent with genuine 
diversification across the portfolio. 
A small number of pairs generate negative contributions, predominantly those involving tickers in 
the Communication Services sector where the economic rationale for cointegration proved less durable than 
the training-window statistics suggested. This concentration of underperformers in a single sector is 
informative and suggests that future implementations might benefit from a more conservative approach to 
the Communication Services universe. 
 
7.  Conclusion 
This paper has demonstrated that a regime-aware, sector-constrained statistical arbitrage strategy 
applied to the S&P 100 universe can generate economically meaningful and statistically robust risk-adjusted 
returns across a 13-year evaluation period that includes two major market crises and significant structural 
shifts in sector leadership. 
Three specific design choices account for the strategy's stability. Performing cointegration on log 
prices rather than factor model residuals eliminates a common source of spurious spread construction that 
inflates in-sample performance but degrades out of sample. Restricting the candidate universe to withinsector pairs ensures that each pair has an explicit economic justification for mean reversion, reducing the 
proportion of statistically spurious cointegrations that would otherwise be selected. And re-selecting pairs 
from a three-year rolling window at the start of each calendar year ensures that the active portfolio reflects 
current market structure rather than relationships that have already broken down. 
The regime filtering layer provides genuine economic value beyond pure risk management. By 
scaling positions to zero during the Crisis regime, the strategy avoids the worst spread dislocations that 
characterise stress periods, allowing capital to be deployed more aggressively when market conditions are 
more favourable. The dynamic volatility targeting overlay then ensures that the level of risk taken is 
consistent regardless of the number of pairs active or the volatility environment, which contributes to the 
strategy's stable year-by-year performance profile.

Several directions for future research suggest themselves. The pair selection quality score could be 
extended to incorporate additional filters, such as the Kalman filter-based time-varying hedge ratio of 
Caldeira and Moura (2013), which may further reduce the exposure to pairs whose cointegration 
relationship is deteriorating during the trading year. The sector universe could be extended beyond the S&P 
100 to the full S&P 500, where the larger pool of candidates may yield pairs with shorter half-lives and 
higher return potential, at the cost of higher transaction costs for the less liquid smaller-cap names. Finally, 
the regime model could be augmented with macroeconomic variables such as credit spreads or yield curve 
slope, which might improve the timeliness of regime transition detection relative to the purely market-based 
HMM used here. 
The consistently positive out-of-sample performance, the low market beta, and the contained 
maximum drawdown collectively suggest that the strategy captures a genuine and persistent source of 
relative value in large-cap US equities that is distinct from the common risk factors documented in the 
factor investing literature.
