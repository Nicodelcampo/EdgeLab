Abstract 
This article synthesizes foundational theories in economics and modern quantitative finance, 
with particular emphasis on how stochastic analysis, market microstructure, and machine 
learning jointly shape contemporary research and practice. We review classic frameworks for 
portfolio choice and asset pricing, the martingale approach to no-arbitrage valuation, and 
liquidity-sensitive models of execution and market making. We then integrate recent 
econometric advances—especially double/debiased machine learning for causal and structural 
estimation—and discuss emerging uses of large language models for measurement and 
prediction tasks under explicit validity conditions. The methodology section proposes a unified 
research-to-deployment pipeline that links theory-driven modeling to disciplined empirical 
evaluation. The analysis highlights implications for market efficiency, risk management, and 
financial stability, and identifies open problems at the intersection of equilibrium modeling, 
strategic interaction, and data-driven inference. We also highlight recent market-structure 
developments—such as the growth of zero-days-to-expiry index options, the transition to T+1 
settlement in U.S. securities, and the expansion of central clearing in U.S. Treasuries—and 
discuss their quantitative implications for liquidity and intraday risk.

General equilibrium; asset pricing; stochastic calculus; stochastic control; market microstructure;

1. Introduction 
Economics and quantitative finance share a common object: decision-making under uncertainty. 
In economics, uncertainty enters through incomplete information, strategic interaction, and 
aggregate shocks; in finance, it appears in asset returns, volatility, liquidity, and counterparty 
risk. Over the past five decades, the field has developed a layered architecture of theories. At the 
most abstract level are equilibrium arguments linking preferences, technology, and information 
to prices. At the operational level are models of trading, hedging, and risk management that must 
confront market frictions such as transaction costs and price impact. More recently, highdimensional data and machine learning (ML) have expanded what is empirically measurable, 
while also raising new concerns about identification, overfitting, and the epistemic reliability of 
automated measurement. 
 
This article provides an integrated view of these developments and argues for a coherent 
methodology: (i) begin with economic primitives and no-arbitrage restrictions; (ii) represent 
trading and strategic behavior as stochastic control or game problems; (iii) estimate and validate 
with econometric procedures designed for high-dimensional nuisance components; and (iv) 
maintain governance practices that preserve interpretability, auditability, and robustness under 
distributional change. The aim is not to replace classical theory with algorithmic prediction, but 
to combine them in ways that remain mathematically and empirically defensible. 
Note: This article is for academic and educational purposes and does not constitute investment advice. 
2. Literature Review 
2.1 Portfolio choice and risk premia: from mean-variance to factor models 
Modern quantitative finance begins with a deceptively simple insight: investors care about both 
expected return and risk. Markowitz’s mean-variance framework formalizes this as an 
optimization problem that trades off expected portfolio return against portfolio variance, laying 
the foundation for risk-return frontiers and diversification logic (Markowitz, 1952). The capital 
asset pricing model (CAPM) embeds this logic into equilibrium, predicting that expected excess 
returns are proportional to systematic risk exposure (Sharpe, 1964). The efficient markets 
hypothesis (EMH) advances a related informational claim: under sufficient competition and 
rationality, prices incorporate available information so that predictable excess returns are 
difficult to extract (Fama, 1970). 
 
Subsequent theory and evidence complicate these canonical models. Multi-factor approaches, 
including arbitrage pricing theory (APT), relax the single-factor structure of CAPM and connect 
pricing to the absence of arbitrage across many assets (Ross, 1976). Empirical asset pricing 
confronts a persistent tension: equilibrium models explain returns through economic 
mechanisms, but expected returns are not directly observed and must be estimated with noisy 

data. In this setting, measurement error and multiple-hypothesis testing become central 
methodological issues—problems that are now being revisited with modern statistical learning 
tools.

2.2 No-arbitrage valuation and stochastic analysis 
The no-arbitrage paradigm provides a second pillar: asset prices must be consistent with the 
absence of costless riskless profits. In continuous time, this logic is naturally expressed through 
stochastic calculus and martingale methods. The Black-Scholes model shows how, under 
frictionless trading and diffusion dynamics, derivative prices satisfy both a risk-neutral 
expectation representation and a partial differential equation (Black and Scholes, 1973). 
Merton’s continuous-time treatment generalizes the approach and clarifies connections between 
dynamic hedging, replication, and equilibrium (Merton, 1973). The monograph by Karatzas and 
Shreve systematizes the mathematical foundations, including semimartingales, equivalent 
martingale measures, and utility-based optimization in incomplete markets (Karatzas and Shreve, 
1998). 
 
Two themes from this literature remain particularly relevant for modern practice. First, pricing 
and risk management are inseparable: any valuation formula implies hedging strategies and 
residual risks when markets are incomplete or frictions are present. Second, optimization under 
uncertainty naturally leads to stochastic control, where value functions satisfy Hamilton-JacobiBellman (HJB) equations and optimal policies can be characterized via verification arguments. 
These tools also underpin modern models of execution and market making, which treat liquidity 
as an endogenous cost of trading rather than an exogenous fee.

2.3 Liquidity, market microstructure, and strategic interaction 
Classical asset pricing abstracts from how trades occur. Yet in practice, trading itself moves 
prices through market impact, bid-ask spreads, queue priority, and adverse selection. The optimal 
execution literature models the tradeoff between execution cost and price risk, producing policies 
that specify how rapidly to liquidate or acquire a position. A widely used benchmark is the 
Almgren-Chriss framework, which yields an explicit efficient frontier between expected trading 
cost and cost variance under stylized impact models (Almgren and Chriss, 2001). In parallel, 
market making models treat liquidity provision as a control problem with inventory risk and 
stochastic order arrivals. Avellaneda and Stoikov derive quoting rules in a limit order book that 
balance expected spread capture against inventory risk (Avellaneda and Stoikov, 2008). 
 
Guéant provides a unified treatment of these models, emphasizing both analytical tractability and 
calibration to market realities, and extending the framework to block trade pricing and liquiditysensitive hedging (Guéant, 2016). A natural next step is to treat liquidity as strategic: the actions 
of one large trader affect the opportunities of others. This leads to stochastic games, and, in 
markets with many interacting agents, to mean field game (MFG) limits. The probabilistic 
approach developed by Carmona and Delarue provides a comprehensive theory for mean field 
control and games, including forward-backward SDE characterizations and connections to 
master equations (Carmona and Delarue, 2018).

2.4 Empirical asset pricing and machine learning 
Machine learning has re-entered empirical asset pricing for a pragmatic reason: expected returns 
are difficult to measure, and the predictor set proposed by decades of research is large, 
correlated, and potentially nonlinear. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu provide a comparative analysis of ML 
methods for forecasting equity risk premia and document that flexible nonlinear models can 
improve out-of-sample predictive performance relative to traditional linear benchmarks (Gu, 
Kelly, and Xiu, 2020). The central methodological challenge, however, is interpretability and 
economic meaning: predictive success does not automatically identify causal mechanisms or 
equilibrium channels. 
 
This distinction motivates a division of labor. Structural and equilibrium models remain essential 
for explaining why risk premia exist and how they should respond to changes in preferences, 
technology, or constraints. ML is most valuable as a measurement tool: it can approximate 
conditional expectations, summarize high-dimensional states, and produce forecasting signals 
that can be embedded into economically constrained decision problems (for example, portfolio 
optimization with turnover and risk limits). 
2.5 Econometrics for high-dimensional and AI-assisted measurement 
The rise of ML forces econometrics to confront an old issue in a new form: how to conduct valid 
inference on low-dimensional parameters (such as treatment effects or structural elasticities) in 
the presence of high-dimensional nuisance functions. Chernozhukov et al. develop 
double/debiased machine learning (DML), which constructs Neyman-orthogonal score functions 
and uses sample splitting (cross-fitting) to reduce regularization bias, enabling asymptotically 
normal inference in many settings (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). These ideas are directly relevant 
to finance, where one might estimate causal effects of policy changes, execution rules, or 
information disclosures on liquidity and volatility. 
 
Recent work extends DML to settings with additional selection complications. For example, 
Dolgikh and Potanin propose DML estimators for treatment effects in multivariate sample 
selection models and study their finite-sample properties (Dolgikh and Potanin, 2025). Such 
extensions matter because financial datasets often reflect endogenous participation: trades are 
observed only when agents choose to transact, and market data can be truncated by reporting 
thresholds or platform rules.

2.6 Large language models as econometric instruments for text and unstructured data 
Large language models (LLMs) introduce a new kind of measurement automation: they can label 
and summarize text, extract structured variables from disclosures, and generate features from 
qualitative data. Ludwig, Mullainathan, and Rambachan propose an applied econometric 
framework that distinguishes prediction tasks from estimation (measurement) tasks and 
emphasizes two validity requirements: avoiding training-data leakage for prediction and 
collecting validation data to quantify measurement error for estimation (Ludwig, Mullainathan, 
and Rambachan, 2025). In finance, such tools could reduce the cost of constructing sentiment 
indices, classifying news by topic, or extracting risk disclosures from filings, but only if their 
error structure is explicitly modeled and audited. 
 
From a methodological standpoint, LLM-based variables should be treated like any other noisy 
proxy: they can be useful, but they must be validated, and their errors must be propagated into 
uncertainty quantification. This perspective aligns naturally with the econometric emphasis on 
measurement error, and it also complements the risk-management culture of financial 
institutions, where model risk and governance are increasingly central. 
3. Methodology: a unified research-to-deployment pipeline 
We now outline a methodological template for integrating economic theory, mathematical 
finance, market microstructure, and data-driven inference. The template is intended as a practical 
blueprint rather than a single model. It emphasizes three principles: (i) explicit economic 
constraints (no-arbitrage, budget, risk limits); (ii) mathematically characterized decision rules 
(stochastic control and games); and (iii) empirical validation protocols that protect against 
overfitting, leakage, and distributional shift. 
3.1 Modeling primitives and identification targets 
The first step is to define primitives and targets. Primitives include state variables (prices, order 
book features, macro signals), controls (trading rates, quotes, portfolio weights), and a 
probabilistic law for state evolution (diffusions, point processes, or regime-switching models). 
Targets are the objects one intends to estimate or compute, such as (i) a pricing functional for 
derivatives, (ii) an optimal policy for execution, (iii) equilibrium price impact and spreads, or 
(iv) causal effects of a rule change. 
 
Identification must be specified at the outset: prediction problems (forecasting returns or order 
arrivals) require stable out-of-sample performance, whereas causal or structural parameters 
require assumptions that justify counterfactual comparisons. DML can be used when the causal 
parameter admits an orthogonal score representation with high-dimensional nuisance functions, 
while structural estimation may impose equilibrium restrictions from no-arbitrage or agent 
optimization. 

3.2 No-arbitrage constraints and valuation 
For valuation problems, the no-arbitrage condition implies the existence of a pricing kernel or an 
equivalent martingale measure under which discounted asset prices are martingales (Karatzas 
and Shreve, 1998). Practically, this provides a disciplined way to combine statistical estimation 
with financial consistency: a forecasting model for drifts may be useful for portfolio choice, but 
it is not a pricing model unless it is linked to a stochastic discount factor. Conversely, derivative 
pricing models must specify hedging strategies and residual risks in incomplete markets. 
3.3 Optimization under frictions: control formulations 
Many real problems are naturally expressed as stochastic control: maximize expected utility of 
terminal wealth, minimize execution cost subject to risk, or maximize market-making P&L 
subject to inventory constraints. The control formulation clarifies what the system is optimizing 
and what constraints it respects. In execution, the canonical objective is a mean-risk tradeoff in 
liquidation cost; in market making, it is a tradeoff between spread capture and inventory risk. 
These objectives can often be solved analytically in stylized settings, which is valuable because 
it yields interpretable comparative statics (how the policy changes with volatility, risk aversion, 
or impact parameters). In more realistic settings, numerical methods (dynamic programming, 
PDE solvers, reinforcement learning with constraints) are used, but the interpretation remains 
anchored by the control objective.

3.4 Strategic interaction: games and mean field limits 
When multiple agents interact, control problems become games. Strategic interaction is central in 
microstructure: liquidity providers quote prices anticipating adverse selection; large traders 
choose execution schedules anticipating price impact; and regulators set rules that alter 
incentives and equilibrium outcomes. Mean field games offer a tractable approximation when the 
number of agents is large and each agent’s influence is small, but the aggregate distribution of 
states affects incentives. The Carmona-Delarue framework enables probabilistic characterization 
and numerical approximations for such models, making them relevant for studying systemic 
liquidity, crowding, and feedback loops (Carmona and Delarue, 2018). 
3.5 Empirical discipline: evaluation protocols and model risk controls 
To translate models into practice, empirical evaluation must match the decision environment. For 
prediction-based components (return forecasts, volatility forecasts, order arrival intensities), 
standard cross-validation is insufficient when data are autocorrelated and when labels overlap 
across time. Practitioners therefore rely on walk-forward evaluation, embargo periods, and 
backtesting protocols that prevent information leakage. Moreover, any performance claim must 
incorporate transaction costs, slippage, and capacity effects, which often dominate paper profits. 
 
Model risk controls are equally important. In quantitative finance, a useful distinction is between 
(i) statistical risk (estimation error under a fixed model) and (ii) structural risk (model 
misspecification and regime change). Robustness checks include stress testing, parameter 

perturbations, and scenario analysis. Coherent risk measures and expected shortfall provide a 
mathematically principled language for tail risk aggregation (Artzner et al., 1999).

4. Analysis and Synthesis 
4.1 Market efficiency, predictability, and the role of measurement 
The EMH (Fama, 1970) can be read less as a claim of perfect markets and more as a warning 
about signal-to-noise ratios: if markets are competitive, most predictable patterns are quickly 
arbitraged away or are compensation for risk. The practical implication is that improved 
prediction is best viewed as improved measurement rather than proof of a free lunch. In this 
spirit, ML-driven return forecasts are valuable insofar as they reduce measurement error in 
conditional expectations, enabling sharper tests of economic mechanisms and better calibrated 
decision rules. 
 
Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) emphasize that ML can materially improve risk-premium 
measurement, but they also caution that better measurement does not, by itself, identify 
economic structure. Bridging prediction to explanation requires embedding forecasts into models 
that respect equilibrium restrictions and that generate testable implications about how risk premia 
should respond to changes in constraints or shocks. 

4.2 Liquidity as an economic variable 
Liquidity is simultaneously a technological and strategic phenomenon: it depends on market 
design, the distribution of agent objectives, and the information environment. Execution costs 
and spreads can therefore be interpreted as prices for immediacy and risk-bearing. The execution 
and market making literatures provide operational definitions of liquidity through measurable 
objects: impact functions, fill probabilities, and inventory risk. In turn, these micro-level frictions 
can aggregate into macro-level effects by changing the cost of capital, amplifying deleveraging, 
or generating endogenous volatility. 
 
Guéant’s framework highlights that liquidity is not merely a nuisance: it enters pricing and 
hedging through additional state variables and through nonlinear feedback between trading and 
prices (Guéant, 2016). In this setting, control and game methods are not optional—they are the 
natural language for describing optimal behavior under endogenous trading costs. 
4.3 Strategic execution and market making: why games matter 
The core insight of Almgren-Chriss is that a large order transforms a static investment problem 
into a dynamic tradeoff between cost and risk. Avellaneda-Stoikov adds a complementary 
insight: liquidity provision can be framed as utility maximization under inventory constraints 
with stochastic order arrivals. Both models are analytically tractable in stylized regimes, which is 
precisely why they remain influential: they reveal comparative statics and provide baselines for 
more complex simulation environments. 
 
However, modern markets involve many participants who respond to the same signals and 
constraints. This creates crowding and feedback effects that cannot be captured by single-agent 
models. Mean field games provide a tractable approximation and suggest that equilibrium 
liquidity and volatility depend on the distribution of inventories and risk tolerances. The 
Carmona-Delarue theory makes these links mathematically precise and supplies tools for 
numerical computation of equilibria (Carmona and Delarue, 2018). 
4.4 Causal inference and policy evaluation in finance 
Many economically important questions in finance are causal: how do disclosure rules affect 
liquidity? how do execution restrictions change market quality? what is the effect of monetary 
policy surprises on risk-taking? These questions require counterfactual reasoning. In highdimensional settings, DML provides a principled approach to combining flexible ML estimation 
of nuisance components with valid inference on low-dimensional causal parameters 
(Chernozhukov et al., 2018). The extension of such methods to selection problems is particularly 
relevant for market data, where the act of trading determines what is observed (Dolgikh and 
Potanin, 2025). 

4.5 AI-assisted measurement: opportunities and governance 
LLMs offer new capabilities for extracting structure from text-intensive financial environments, 
including earnings calls, analyst reports, risk disclosures, and regulatory documents. The 
econometric framework proposed by Ludwig, Mullainathan, and Rambachan underscores that 
these tools must be used with safeguards: prediction tasks require demonstrable absence of 
training-data leakage, and measurement tasks require validation data to quantify error and bias 
(Ludwig, Mullainathan, and Rambachan, 2025). In institutional contexts, this implies that AIassisted variables should enter the same governance pipeline as traditional risk models: 
documentation, validation, stress testing, monitoring, and auditability. 
 
A broader implication is epistemic. As models become more complex, the risk shifts from 
estimating parameters to understanding failure modes. A mature quantitative practice therefore 
treats modeling as an iterative cycle of hypothesis, test, and revision, rather than as a one-time 
optimization exercise. This is consistent with both econometric principles and the practical 
experience that the most damaging losses often arise from untested assumptions, regime changes, 
or unmodeled dependencies. 
4.6 Current market structure and policy developments (2024–2026) 
While the preceding sections emphasize stable theoretical primitives, recent market-structure 
changes have shifted key constraints faced by quantitative strategies. These developments 
motivate explicit modeling of intraday risk transfer, settlement and clearing frictions, and crossasset liquidity resilience. 
4.6.1 Zero-days-to-expiry (0DTE) index options and intraday risk transfer 
Short-dated index options have grown rapidly, altering the intraday distribution of hedging 
demand and potentially the microstructure of volatility. For example, Cboe reports that in Q3 
2025, 0DTE contracts comprised 57% of SPX index options average daily volume. This 
concentration of option expiry risk into the trading day increases the importance of modeling 
gamma-related hedging flows and liquidity conditions at sub-daily horizons. 
4.6.2 Faster settlement (T+1) and funding/FX frictions 
The U.S. transition to T+1 settlement (effective May 28, 2024) reduces counterparty exposure in 
the settlement cycle but tightens operational timelines and can shift demand for intraday liquidity 
and FX funding. For quantitative finance, the implication is that backtests and execution models 
should incorporate settlement-driven constraints (e.g., cut-off times, fails risk, and financing 
costs) rather than treating trades as frictionlessly nettable at end-of-day. 
4.6.3 Expanding central clearing in U.S. Treasuries and balance-sheet constraints 
Policy moves toward broader central clearing in U.S. Treasuries aim to reduce systemic risk, but 
they also change the economics of intermediation via margin requirements, access models, and 
netting sets. The SEC has extended compliance dates for covered clearing-agency standards, 

pushing key deadlines into 2026–2027. This makes it timely to extend microstructure and 
execution models to include clearing and margin as endogenous constraints on market making 
and repo financing. 
4.6.4 Liquidity fragility, fragmentation, and resilience across asset classes 
Recent central-bank research emphasizes that liquidity can deteriorate simultaneously across 
markets, particularly under stress, and that fragmentation and high-frequency activity can 
interact with resilience. A practical synthesis is to treat liquidity not only as a cost parameter but 
also as a state variable with regime dependence and feedback into volatility and funding 
conditions. 
4.6.5 Rate regime and cross-asset correlations 
As of December 2025, the Federal Reserve’s target range for the federal funds rate was 3.50% to 
3.75%. Shifts in the rate regime influence carry, discount rates, and the co-movement structure 
between equities, rates, and volatility. Quantitative strategies therefore require scenario-aware 
evaluation that conditions on policy and term-structure states rather than assuming stable 
correlations. 
5. Conclusion 
General economics and quantitative finance are increasingly intertwined. Equilibrium and noarbitrage theories provide discipline and interpretability; stochastic analysis and control theory 
supply mathematically precise decision rules; microstructure models incorporate liquidity and 
strategic interaction; and modern econometrics and machine learning expand what can be 
measured and predicted. The most productive research agenda is integrative: embed data-driven 
components within economically constrained models, and validate them with evaluation 
protocols that reflect market frictions and model risk. 
 
Open problems remain. On the theoretical side, connecting equilibrium asset pricing to 
microstructure frictions and strategic interaction at scale is still challenging. On the empirical 
side, causal identification in markets with endogenous participation and rapidly evolving 
institutions requires continued methodological innovation. Finally, AI-assisted measurement—
particularly via LLMs—creates opportunities to incorporate rich qualitative information, but it 
also demands rigorous validation and governance. Bridging these domains will be central to the 
next generation of economics and quantitative finance, both for scientific understanding and for 
responsible practice.
