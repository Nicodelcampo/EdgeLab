A Cheat Sheet for Algorithmic Trading of Investment Portfolios 
 
These working notes on algorithmic trading are designed as an introduction to the basic equations that govern the 
day-to-day trading of an investment portfolio to meet a pre-defined strategic asset allocation. The algebraic 
framework applies generally to long only and long/short strategies. It incorporates the use of leverage as well as the 
impact of trading costs on a portfolio. The presentation of the material is based on first principles. As such it assumes 
an elementary knowledge of linear algebra, but no particular expertise in finance or securities trading. 
 
In these notes, we will use the terms ‘portfolio’ and ‘investor account’ interchangeably. Both refer to the pool of 
money that is being traded using an investment strategy managed by an investor or investment advisor. The investor 
account could be a separate account such as a personal brokerage account, or a fund structure such as a hedge fund, a 
mutual fund or a SICAV amongst others.  
This paper is concerned with developing the algebraic framework that defines how a portfolio should be traded based 
on the asset allocation created by a given investment strategy. The output of the strategic allocation consists of a list of 
securities to trade, the target weight of each security in the portfolio post trading, as well as the exposure of each 
security in the portfolio, i.e. whether the positions are held long or short. We do not delve into how a specific 
investment strategy is developed, simply in its implementation in a portfolio structure. 
While trading an account that does not use margin is reasonably straightforward, investment strategies can also take 
advantage of the full buying power of an account in which leverage is allowed. Managing the strategic asset allocation 
of a portfolio in the context of leverage limits, margin calls and target risk limits is not especially complex but requires 
solving different sets of linear equations. In this paper, we derive the sets of equations that provide a complete 
framework to compute the real time situation of such a portfolio.  
The resulting systems of equations give rise to matrices that can be inverted with standard analytical packages. No 
consideration is given to the efficiency of any particular matrix inversion technique, a topic outside the scope of this 
paper. 
Finally, while there are similarities between trading futures and trading securities such as bonds or equities, the 
mechanics and accounting methodology for futures trading are different than what is outlined below. Our notes on an 
equivalent algebraic framework that applies when trading futures will be published in a separate paper.

PART 1: UNDERSTANDING HOW MARGIN ACCOUNTS WORK 
A margin account is brokerage account that is allowed to use margin in order to leverage its investment positions. The 
use of margin and leverage involves additional risk of loss. It must be requested by the account owner and approved by 
the brokerage firm that holds the account. While regulations (such as Regulation T “Reg. T” in the United States) 
control the use of margin for individual accounts, institutional investors will negotiate the amount of leverage they can 
employ with the brokerage firm holding the account.

PART 2:  TRADING TO MEET MAINTENANCE MARGIN REQUIREMENTS (NO TRANSACTION COSTS) 
Assuming that the account in the example above is subject to the standard Reg. T margin limit of 50%, the fall in 
security value has brought the account to 44% margin, which is below the minimum margin allowed. This margin 
violation results in a margin call. The investor is required to act so that the account meets its minimum margin 
requirement.  
Note that a similar need to rebalance a portfolio can arise periodically if the investment strategy is managed with a 
target margin level. As the portfolio margin fluctuates away from its target with moving prices over time, the portfolio 
occasionally needs to be rebalanced for the purpose of bringing its margin level back on target which may either higher 
or lower than its current position. If the margin is lower than target, as is the case when a margin call occurs, securities 
will need to be sold to bring leverage down and margin up. Conversely if the margin is higher than target, more capital 
needs to be borrowed by operating at the lower target margin. Securities need to be purchased using the account’s 
buying power to reduce the margin level.  
While we discuss selling securities to meet a margin call in this section for the sake of simplicity, the same resulting 
equations can be used to increase or decrease portfolio margin towards a target level, by replacing the maintenance 
margin with the target margin.  
Rebalancing a portfolio to meet a margin call can be done either by adding cash to the account, or by selling securities 
to increase the cash position. This section will derive the equations that govern the selling decisions in this situation.

shares. Also available are wrap fee schedules, whereby an investor pays an annual percentage fee, for instance 1% of 
the account value, to the brokerage firm but trades are free.
