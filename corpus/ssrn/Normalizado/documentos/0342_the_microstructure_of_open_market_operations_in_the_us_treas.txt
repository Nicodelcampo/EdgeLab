LEADING IN THOUGHT AND ACTION 
 
 
 
 
The Microstructure of Open Market Operations in the U.S. Treasury Market 
 
A proposal for the Q Group – 2010 Call for Research Proposals 
 
Over the last two decades, many domestic and international financial markets have experienced 
high volatility, price bubbles, sudden, severe (and often deemed “excessive”) downward price 
movements, drying liquidity, rapid reversals of capital flows, and contagious propagation of 
shocks across stocks, bonds, and currencies – as recently as in correspondence with the global 
financial crisis of the last two years. These phenomena are pervasive and difficult to reconcile 
with standard asset pricing theory, as well as of significant economic, financial, and social 
implications. Against this backdrop, our theoretical and empirical research explores how the 
introduction of realistic financial frictions – such as price manipulation, information asymmetry 
and heterogeneity, strategic trading, and endowment shocks – may shed light on these 
phenomena, in particular on the mechanisms through which private and public information are 
impounded into asset prices and on the implications of these mechanisms for market liquidity 
and efficiency. Much of our work on these issues concentrates on the U.S. Treasury bond 
market. The secondary market for U.S. Treasury securities is among the largest, most active, 
and most liquid financial markets. However, the study of the process of price formation in this 
market has not received the same attention reserved by the literature to the stock market. This 
is an important omission since government bond markets are “special,” i.e., many of their 
institutional features differ from those of stock exchanges, where most theoretical and empirical 
research instead occurs. For example, the nature of private information in those markets differs 
markedly from the notion widely used when analyzing equity markets; in addition, those 
markets are populated by rational, possibly better-informed, but possibly non-profit-maximizing 
price manipulators like Central Banks. In short, the understanding of the way government bond 
markets function remains less than complete. 
 
In view of that, our research agenda contributes to fill this gap by identifying a link between the 
price discovery role of order flow in the bond markets (and their liquidity) and their information 
environment (the heterogeneity of investors’ information and the intrinsic quality of 
macroeconomic news releases). For instance, in a paper titled Informed and Strategic Order 
Flow in the Bond Markets,1 we modeled and establish empirically the non-trivial role of 
informed and strategic trading in the U.S. Treasury market, contrary to common 
misconceptions about the nature of trading in that market as being driven uniquely by noninformation motives (e.g. by liquidity considerations). In a related study titled The On-The-Run 
Liquidity Phenomenon,2 we employed these insights to shed light on one of the most 
important, yet not fully understood asset pricing puzzles, the on-the-run phenomenon. In 
particular, we propose a novel theory relating liquidity differentials between similar bonds

Our proposed research project builds upon the aforementioned framework to study the 
important yet not fully understood trading activity by governments in financial markets. Indeed, 
the aggressive response of the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank to the economic 
turmoil of the last few years suggests that monetary authorities around the world are likely to 
play a much more active role in financial markets than they did in the past. As the ensuing 
intense academic and political debate shows (e.g., Jones, 2008; Boehmer et al., 2009), such 
a role raises pressing questions related not only to its motives and effectiveness but also to its 
implications (or externalities) for the quality of the process of price formation in the affected 
markets – e.g., investors' ability to trade promptly and with minimal price impact (liquidity) and 
the extent to which information is incorporated into prices. Answers to these questions are 
relevant not only to academics but also to policy-makers, professional investors, and risk 
managers. 
 
 
Motivated by this debate, our proposed research project investigates, both theoretically and 
empirically, the implications of the presence of an active price manipulator – like the Central 
Bank – in a financial market for that market's liquidity. We do so by studying one market in 
which monetary authorities have long been active via Open Market Operations (OMOs), the 
secondary market for U.S. government bonds. OMOs are uninformative trades in U.S. Treasury 
securities aimed at accomplishing the Federal Reserve's target level of the federal funds rate.3 
To guide our analysis, we develop a parsimonious model of trading in Treasury securities in the 
presence of a stylized Central Bank (attempting to achieve a price target while accounting for 
the cost of the intervention). Our novel theoretical analysis shows that government intervention, 
while effective at its policy, may also improve equilibrium market liquidity – contrary to extant 
studies of informative Central Bank interventions in currency markets – and that both effects 
are sensitive to the market's information environment. We plan to test these implications by 
employing a novel, comprehensive sample of U.S. Treasury bond market transaction-level data 
from BrokerTec (the electronic platform where the majority of such trading migrated since 
2001) and a proprietary dataset of all OMOs conducted by the Federal Reserve between 
January 2001 and March 2009. Our preliminary analysis of this data shows strong support for 
our model’s implications. For instance, we find that bid-ask price spreads for notes and bonds 
nearly uniformly decline (i.e., their liquidity improves) both on days when the Federal Reserve 
executed OMOs in the corresponding maturity bracket. Estimated liquidity improvements are 
both economically and statistically significant: OMO days are characterized by average bid-ask 
spreads that are 10% (for ten-year notes) to 54% (for three-year notes) lower than their sample 
means. We expect to complete the empirical portion of this research by the Spring of 2011.
