Managing Volatility for Profitable Options Trading:
Evidence from Pairs Trading Strategies
Irene Aldridgea,1,∗, Kelvin Jianga,1
aCornell Univeristy ORIE, Financial Engineering, 2 West Loop Rd., New

Abstract
This study investigates the potential of managing volatility to generate profitable returns in options trading. The paper introduces a novel methodology
for modeling pairs trading strategies in options, utilizing underlying asset
volatility and publicly available daily variables to predict volatility. Through
simulation results, we demonstrate the potential for ex-ante profitable options pairs.
Consistent with previous research, we provide evidence that
microstructure factors significantly influence underlying asset volatility and
options pairs performance.
Importantly, we find that microstructure factors exhibit persistence and can be effectively utilized to create profitable
strategies.
Furthermore, we empirically test the performance of the proposed options trading strategies and find that it aligns with our theoretical
predictions. Notably, while AI-based prediction using Neural Networks is
explored, we find that simple OLS linear regressions outperform in the specific application considered. This study contributes to the understanding of
volatility management and provides practical insights for options traders.

1. Introduction
Recent literature has documented abnormal returns associated with asset
volatility. For example, [1] show that volatility-managed portfolios produce
abnormal returns.
In this paper, we propose and test a methodology to
successfully harness the volatility persistence via novel options pair trading
strategies. We use microstructure effects to forecast volatility. Thus, our
paper contributes to the literature on persistence of microstructure effects,
as well as volatility forecasting and explaining informational inefficiencies in
today’s markets.
Pairs trading involves the simultaneous trading of two financial instruments that exhibit a tendency to move in unison. In this strategy, traders
take a long position (buy) in one security and a short position (sell or shortsell) in the other security.The strategy was initially developed by Morgan
Stanley in the 1980s and extensively examined in research studies like [2]
and [3],
Pairs trading strategy is based on temporary dislocations of the prices
away from the Law of One Price. The prices of two financial instruments may
deviate temporarily from their long-term relationship, providing a potential
opportunity for a profitable trade. Traders often rely on the assumption that
these prices will ”mean-revert,” returning to their usual relationship over
time. In pursuit of profit, traders may buy the undervalued asset and sell
the overvalued one, a trade commonly known as arbitrage. It’s important
2

to note that most arbitrage carries some level of risk, as traders make a
bet on the relationship holding or mean-reverting and act accordingly. For a
comprehensive analysis of the differences between risky and riskless arbitrage,
refer to studies such as [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10] and [11]). [12] hypothesized
that even riskless arbitrage entails execution effects that introduce risk into
the investment.
In this research, following [1], we create alternative volatility-managed
portfolios via options pairs trading. Using the seminal Black-Scholes model
([13]), we construct pairwise options portfolios that are only dependent on
market volatility.
The literature on options pairs arbitrage is rather scarce. Research on
options pairs trading in the public domains includes [14], [15] and [16]. [15]
note that most options mispricings disappear once transaction costs are taken
into account. [14] also highlight that option mispricing relative to their theoretical benchmarks may be primarily due to microstructure effects and high
trading costs in the options market. [16] try to construct the index options
pairs, but do not find sustained profitability.
However, [17] show that microstructure effects account for a substantial
portion of asset volatility. In our implementation, we also rely on microstructure effects to successfully estimate short-term volatility realizations.
To simplify estimation, we rely on daily data of underlying assets to estimate relevant microstructure effects, following well-established literature.
For example,[18] discuss how traditional liquidity metrics such as price impact and [19] measures are effective in capturing microstructure effects. [20]
uses Gibbs estimates on daily prices to capture effective trading costs. [21]
3

show that microstructure effects can be successfully proxied using daily Open,
High, Low and Close prices. [22] show that using higher-frequency data in
volatility prediction does not improve the outcome.
In order to assess the impact of execution effects on options arbitrage,
we conduct empirical investigations. Our findings demonstrate that intraday microstructure effects indeed influence volatility spread. These effects
vary depending on the specific pair of securities chosen. Specifically, we observe that intraday price bounce following [23], price dispersion according to
([24]), price impact as described by [19] and [25], as well as realized volatility
following [26], all have a significant impact on volatility estimates. Consequently, these factors must be taken into consideration when engaging in
options arbitrage.
Finally, we test and document the empirical performance of the options
trading strategies using microstructure factors to predict volatility. We find
that our empirical findings are consistent with our theoretical predictions.

pair of the options at close on day t and reversing our position at close on
day t + 1.

Appendix A. Appendix: Neural network predictability of volatility residuals
We considered simple neural networks with three distinct activation functions, ReLu, Sigmoid and Tanh, described in [32]. Figures A.13-A.16 show
realized vs. predicted volatility residual values for:

In a Relu Neural Network, most points are tightly clustered along certain lines parallel to the diagonal, suggesting that the ReLU model tends to
predict a limited range of values for residuals. This could be due to ReLU’s
nature of outputting zeros for negative values, potentially limiting variability
in lower range predictions.
When we applied sigmoid as our activation function, we observed that
points tend to concentrate in more limited ranges compared to the linear
model.
This is because the sigmoid activation function squeezes outputs
into a range between 0 and 1, which reduces the variance of predictions and
potentially causes clustering.
The Tanh function, which outputs values between -1 and 1, also shows
clustering around certain lines. It shares the limited output range characteristic but can handle negative predictions more flexibly than ReLU.
It appears that neural networks, regardless of activation function, predict the residuals with a consistent bias or variance in certain directions.
When comparing these models, linear regression shows a more generalized
performance across different values.
