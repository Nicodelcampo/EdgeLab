Optimal Pairs Trading with Transaction Costs in
Finite Horizon
Chen Ziyi∗
Gu Jia-Wen†
Pun Chi Seng‡
Abstract
We study optimal pairs trading with transaction costs over a finite time horizon.
The investor trades a pair of co-integrated stocks whose price difference follows a
mean-reverting process. A delta-neutral strategy is adopted, involving a long position in one stock and a short position in the other, with an equal number of shares
in each. Trading incurs transaction costs, modeled as being linear in the number
of shares transacted. The objective is to maximize the expected utility of terminal
wealth net of liquidation costs. For comparison, we first derive the explicit optimal strategy and value function in the frictionless benchmark. In the presence of
transaction costs, we show that the frictional value function is a continuous viscosity
solution to a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) variational inequality, and the associated free boundaries can be expressed as functions of time and the spread. We adopt
an asymptotic approach to expand the value function and derive explicit asymptotic
expressions for the free boundaries, and thereby construct a nearly optimal strategy.
To justify the asymptotic expansion, we apply homogenization techniques together
with the theory of viscosity solutions. Finally, we conduct numerical simulations to
demonstrate the importance of incorporating the transaction costs in pairs trading.
Key words: pairs trading, transaction costs, viscosity solutions, asymptotic expansion, free
boundaries, homogenization, HJB inequality

strategy involves taking a long position in the underpriced stock and a simultaneous short
position in the overpriced one, with both positions closed when the spread reverts to its
historical mean (see Vidyamurthy [47] and Ehrman [12]). It embodies the fundamental
principle of ”buy low and sell high”. Since its introduction by Nunzio Tartaglia’s team
on Wall Street in the mid-1980s, the strategy has attracted considerable attention from
both the academic and industry communities.
The essence of pairs trading lies in two key aspects: the identification of suitable stock
pairs and the formulation of effective trading strategies. Two commonly used methods
for pair identification are the distance method and the cointegration method. The distance method selects stock pairs based on the historical similarity of their normalized
price series (e.g., Gatev et al. [16], Krauss [24], Huck [17]). In contrast, the cointegration
method relies on statistical tests to detect a long-term equilibrium relationship between
two non-stationary price series (e.g., Huck [18], Lin et al. [28], Cartea et al. [5]). Once a
pair is identified, the next step is to design a trading strategy that determines when to
enter and exit positions. Most studies adopt a mean-reversion approach: positions are
opened when the spread deviates significantly from its historical or theoretical mean, and
closed when it reverts. Thresholds for entry and exit are often determined empirically,
based on quantiles, standard deviations, or z-scores of the spread.
Apart from simple trading strategies, some researchers focus on the sophisticated
mathematical pairs trading rules. They formulate the pairs trading problem as a stochastic optimal problem and leverage a range of mathematical tools—such as dynamic programming, the BSDE approach, and viscosity solution methods— to derive the optimal
trading strategies. Broadly speaking, this literature can be categorized into two strands:
(i) Continuous rebalancing models. Inspired by the classical continuous-time portfolio selection problem (e.g., Merton [32], Zhou and Li [52]), some studies examine optimal
investment problems involving cointegrated assets in continuous time, with the objective
of determining the optimal portfolio weights or the allocation of wealth across stock pairs,
under a utility-maximization or mean-variance framework. Mudchanatongsuk et al. [34]
consider a utility maximization problem under the constraint that the dollar amounts of
long and short positions are equal. Tourin and Yan [45], Liu and Timmermann [29], and
Xu and Yang [19] relax this constraint by allowing for arbitrary investment amounts in
each stock. In addition, Liu and Timmermann [29] and Xu and Yang [19] further extend
the model. Liu and Timmermann incorporate ’stopped’ cointegrated price processes and
stochastic investment horizons modeled by a Poisson process, while Xu and Yang [19]
investigate the impact of short-selling fees on pairs trading. Chiu and Wong [6, 7], Zhu
et al. [53] and Yu et al. [50] study the problem within a mean-variance framework. To
address the issue of time inconsistency, Zhu et al. [53] and Chiu and Wong [7] derive
an equilibrium strategy, whereas Yu et al. [50] and Chiu and Wong [6] adopt a precommitment approach. A notable limitation is that, due to mathematical complexities,
2

the aforementioned studies neglect transaction costs, and the resulting optimal strategies
require continuous rebalancing, leading to high-frequency trading behavior. As a result,
these strategies become practically infeasible when transaction costs are sufficiently large.
(ii) Optimal switching models. This line of research incorporates transaction costs
and models trading as a sequence of discrete decisions, focusing on the optimal timing
of opening and closing positions. Zhang and Zhang [51] and Leung et al. [27] study the
optimal buying and selling problem under exponential Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) price
dynamics with slippage costs.
As an extension, Song and Zhang [41] and Leung and
Li [26] consider the optimal timing to trade an OU price spread subject to a stop-loss
constraint. Tie et al. [44] and Liu et al. [30] relax the mean-reversion assumption and
instead model asset prices as geometric Brownian motions. Ngo and Pham [36], as well as
Suzuki [42, 43], investigate the optimal switching problem among three regimes (buy, sell,
and flat) under infinite horizon setting. Further, Lei and Xu [25] study the three-regime
switching problem in a finite-horizon setting.
Compared with continuous rebalancing
models, the optimal switching models incorporate the transaction costs but require the
number of traded shares in each transaction to be fixed, which limits flexibility in practical applications.
Taking into account the advantages and limitations of the two modeling approaches in
the existing literature, we study a finite-horizon pairs trading problem with transaction
costs in continuous time and allow the number of shares of paired stocks to vary. We
assume that the investor’s wealth is generated from trading a pair of cointegrated assets,
and that the spread follows an OU process. Similar to Mudchanatongsuk et al. [34], we
consider a delta-neutral strategy that requires taking a long position in one stock and a
short position in the other, with an equal number of shares in both positions. At each
transaction, the cost is proportional to the number of shares traded. Our goal is to maximize the utility of terminal wealth minus the liquidation cost. Such a modeling framework
poses significant challenges for mathematical analysis: (i) Unlike in frictionless cases, the
position in the paired stocks becomes a state variable in the presence of transaction costs,
as it can no longer be adjusted freely and frequently. Instead, the control variables are
modeled as two non-decreasing processes representing the cumulative number of shares
bought and sold, respectively. (ii) A finite time horizon introduces an additional time
dimension to the value function, and the incorporation of transaction costs further renders the value function non-smooth. Both of these factors result in a so-called singular
control problem of high dimensionality. Some advanced techniques have been developed
to address this problem. For example, Shreve and Soner [39] adopt viscosity solution
methods to analyze optimal investment and consumption with transaction costs in the
infinite horizon setting. Building on this, Dai and Yi [9] study the same problem in a
finite-horizon framework and leverage PDE methods to transform the control problem
into a parabolic double obstacle problem. Their optimal strategies are to maintain the
3

position within the no-trade region, and trading occurs only when the position hits the
boundaries of this region. However, the explicit characterization of these boundaries is
impossible, and one must resort to numerical methods (See Tourin and Zariphopoulou
[46], Dai and Zhong [10]). Due to the high nonlinearity of the HJB inequality governing
the value function, solving it numerically requires significant computational effort and can
be too slow for real-time trading applications.
To obtain tractable results, some researchers adopt asymptotic methods and treat
transaction costs as small perturbations of a frictionless benchmark. Janeˇcek and Shreve
[20] employ this approach to study the optimal investment and consumption problem,
while Whalley and Wilmott [48] focus on an optimal hedging model. Both works derive
explicit asymptotic approximations of the free boundaries by expanding the value function in powers of the transaction costs. Further, building on homogenization and viscosity
solution technique, Soner and Touzi [40], as well as Possama¨ı et al. [38], provide rigorous asymptotic expansions for models involving single or multiple assets with transaction
costs. This approach can also be adapted to various extensions of other models (see Altarovici et al. [1], Moreau et al. [33], Melnyk et al. [31], Kallsen [21], among others).
In this paper, we show that the value function is a continuous viscosity solution to an
HJB variational inequality. The HJB variational inequality indicates the state space can
be split into three regions: buying region, selling region and no-trade region. We establish
that the buying boundary (which separates the buying region from the no-trading region)
and the selling boundary (which separates the selling region from the no-trading region)
can both be characterized as functions of time and the spread. In order to derive asymptotically explicit expressions for the free boundaries, we further postulate, via heuristic
arguments, that the value function admits an expansion in powers of a suitably scaled
transaction-cost parameter. By retaining the leading-order term and neglecting higherorder corrections, we obtain the first-order and second-order corrector equations. Solving
these equations yields explicit asymptotic approximations for the free boundaries. Based
on these boundaries, we reformulate the singular control problem as a Skorokhod problem, the solution of which yields a nearly optimal trading strategy. Finally, to rigorously
justify our derivation, we apply homogenization techniques and the theory of viscosity
solutions to verify the validity of the asymptotic expansion. We also conduct numerical
experiments to intuitively illustrate the properties of the nearly optimal strategy in the
presence of transaction costs and explore its performance under different market conditions. The result indicates that the impact of transaction costs on the profitability of
pairs trading cannot be ignored.
In summary, this paper makes contributions from both financial and methodological
perspectives. From the financial perspective, (i) we incorporate transaction costs into the
pairs trading problem under a finite horizon setting and relax the restrictive assumption
of fixed trading shares, thereby making the model more practical and consistent with

4
Asymptotic Analysis
In this section, we adopt asymptotic expansion methods to derive a nearly optimal strategy. The key idea is to treat transaction costs as small perturbations to the frictionless
counterpart. We first analyze the effect of transaction costs on the frictional value function, and then perform an asymptotic expansion of the value function in powers of the
appropriately scaled transaction-cost parameter, retaining terms up to the leading order.
Finally, by matching terms of different orders and applying the principle of smooth fit,
we obtain explicit expressions for the asymptotic free boundaries and asymptotic value
function.
4.1
Heuristic Derivation for Asymptotic Expansion
When the transaction cost K is sufficiently small, the frictional value function is expected
to differ only slightly from its frictionless counterpart. This observation suggests performing an asymptotic expansion. However, the key subtlety lies in identifying a suitable
scaling of the transaction-cost parameter, since the deviation of the frictional value function from its frictionless counterpart typically occurs at a fractional order in K, rather
than at order K itself. To determine the appropriate scaling, we provide a heuristic argument following Janeˇcek and Shreve [20] and Muhle-Karbe et al. [35].
When transaction frictions are present, frequent trading is prohibitively expensive,
since transaction costs significantly erode arbitrage profits. Hence, it is neither rational
nor economically optimal for the investor to maintain the number of units prescribed by
(10). Instead, the investor should maintain Yt within an interval [−∆y/2 + y∗, y∗+ ∆y/2]
where y∗is the optimal position in the absence of costs. Trading should occur only when
Yt reaches the boundaries of this interval. Such behavior leads to two types of losses
relative to the frictionless optimal expected utility: one arises from the deviation from
y∗(the displacement loss), and the other arises from the transaction costs (the cost
loss). The former increases with the width of no-trade region , while the latter decreases.
We need to make a trade-off and choose the width of the no-trade region that minimizes
the total loss.
According to the properties of reflected Brownian motion and its associated local time,
the amount of trading required to maintain the position within the no-trade region is inversely proportional to the width ∆y of that region (for a detailed explanation, see Janeˇcek
and Shreve [20]). Besides, by Lemma 3.1, the frictionless value function dominates its
counterpart with transaction costs, which implies—via the first-order condition—that the

displacement loss is of order (∆y)2. Taking both the displacement loss and cost loss into
account, the total utility loss is proportional to

To minimize it, the width of no-trade region ∆y should be of order K

Conclusion
This paper studies optimal pairs trading with transaction costs under utility maximization framework. The spread between a pair of co-integrated assets is modeled as a meanreverting process, and the investor follows a delta-neutral strategy by simultaneously
taking long and short positions in the two assets while maximizing expected utility of
35

Figure 4: Dynamics of optimal number of units
36

terminal wealth net of liquidation costs.
For comparison, we first investigate the frictionless counterpart and derive the optimal strategy in closed form that indicates trading every time. However, the presence of
transaction costs gives rise to a no-trading region characterized by free boundaries. We
prove that the value function is a continuous viscosity solution and then formulate the
corresponding HJB variational inequality. The structure of the HJB equation naturally
partitions the state space into three regions: the buying region, the selling region, and
the no-trade region. We further show that there exist buying and selling boundaries separating these regions, which can be expressed as functions of time and the spread.
To obtain analytical insights and tractable approximations, we perform an asymptotic expansion of the frictional value function in powers of the appropriately scaled
transaction-cost parameter.
By matching terms across different orders, we derive explicit approximations for the free boundaries and the resulting nearly optimal strategy.
The no-trading region contains the Merton line (frictionless optimal strategy) and shrinks
over time. The nearly optimal strategy maintains the position in no-trade region and
trading only occurs when the position reaches the free boundaries. To rigorously justify
the asymptotic results, we extend homogenization and viscosity-solution techniques and
verify the formal expansion.
Finally, numerical experiments are conducted to illustrate the structure of the optimal
strategy and to quantify the impact of transaction costs on trading behavior and utility.
The results demonstrate that ignoring transaction costs can lead to excessive trading and
significant utility loss, highlighting the importance of incorporating fictions in the design
of pairs trading strategies.
