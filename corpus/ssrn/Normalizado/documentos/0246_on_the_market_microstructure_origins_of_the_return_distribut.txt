Abstract
We develop a reduced-form microstructure model showing that the distribution of market
orders and the shape of the limit order book (LOB) jointly determine return distributions. LOB
imbalance, defined as the ratio of the slopes of the ask and bid sides, drives return mean and
skewness. LOB convexity, the ratio of the slopes of higher and lower levels, shapes variance
and kurtosis, linking liquidity and tail risk. Using ultra-high-frequency LOB data for NYSE
stocks, we provide strong empirical support. Exploiting the Reg SHO pilot program as an
exogenous shock, we establish the causal effect of LOB shape on return distribution.
Key words: Limit Order Book, Imbalance, Convexity, Skewness, Kurtosis, Quantiles, Market
Order Distribution, RegSHO
JEL Classification: G12, G18

Introduction
In order-driven markets, non-marketable limit orders are placed in a queue prioritized by price and
time, forming the limit order book (LOB). Transaction prices are established when market orders
(or marketable limit orders) arrive and execute against the existing limit orders in the book. In a
sense, the LOB acts as a mirror that reflects incoming market orders. Consequently, the distribution
of market orders and the shape of the LOB jointly determine the distribution of transaction prices
and thus returns.
Figure 1 provides an intuitive framework for visualizing the relationship between the distribution of market orders, the shape of the LOB, and price formation. In this simplified framework, we
assume that the shape of both the ask and bid sides of the LOB over a given time interval—say, a
trading day—can be represented by a continuous price impact function centered around a midquote
price (set at $15 in this example), as depicted in the top-right panel labeled “Price Impact Function
P(Q).” Positive values in the market order distribution correspond to market buy orders executed
against the ask side of the LOB, while negative values correspond to market sell orders executed
against the bid side. Furthermore, we assume that, over this time interval, market orders arrive according to a normal distribution, shown in the lower-right panel titled “Quantity PDF” (probability
density function). This implies that there is no market order imbalance—defined as the difference
in volume (in number of shares) between buy and sell orders—yielding a market order distribution
with a mean of zero. As market orders arrive and execute (or, figuratively speaking, are reflected)
against the LOB, transaction prices are observed, as represented in the top-left panel, “Price PDF.”
[Figure 1 about here.]
Panel (a) of Figure 1 illustrates the baseline scenario with a balanced, linear LOB, where the
price impact functions for both bid and ask sides are linear and have identical slopes. In this case,
the return distribution is a scale transformation of the market order distribution, where the scaling
factor is the reciprocal of the LOB slope. Thus, if the market orders follow a normal distribution
with zero mean and some variance, the returns will also exhibit a normal distribution with zero
mean, and the return variance will equal the market order variance multiplied by the slope of the
LOB. This is the well-known fact that higher transaction costs or greater illiquidity lead to greater
return variance, given that the LOB slope can be considered as a measure of transaction costs, or
equivalently, market illiquidity.
1

Panel (b) of Figure 1 illustrates the impact of an imbalanced LOB, where the ask side’s price
impact function has a steeper slope than the bid side, though both remain linear. Here, the LOB
imbalance, defined as the ratio of the ask and bid side slopes, can be considered as an indicator of
buying or selling pressure from limit orders. When the ask side slope exceeds that of the bid side,
as in this example, there is relatively more liquidity on the bid side, creating a buying pressure
from limit orders. Consequently, a positive LOB imbalance produces a positively skewed return
distribution in the absence of market order (MO) imbalance, suggesting a strong link between
return skewness and LOB imbalance.
Panel (c) of Figure 1 demonstrates the effects of a balanced but convex LOB, where the price
impact functions for both ask and bid sides have equal overall slopes, indicating no LOB imbalance, but exhibit convexity—meaning that slopes farther from the midquote are steeper than those
closer to it. LOB convexity, defined as the ratio of slopes at higher versus lower levels, affects
the relative price impact of large versus small market orders. A convex (or concave) LOB occurs
when the slope farther from the midquote, i.e., at higher levels, is steeper (or less steep) than the
slope near the midquote, i.e., at lower levels. In a convex LOB, as seen in Panel (c), large market
orders exert a disproportionately stronger price impact due to reduced liquidity at levels away from
the midquote. Consequently, this shape leads to a return distribution with fatter tails and higher
kurtosis than a normal distribution, implying a direct relationship between LOB convexity and the
tails and kurtosis of the return distribution.
Building on our intuition from the simple framework, we develop a reduced-form model of
price formation in an order-driven market to analyze how the shape of the LOB influences the
return distribution. Under assumptions similar to those in Figure 1, returns can be expressed as
the sum of truncated normal variables. This allows us to derive closed-form expressions for the
first four central moments and to compute return quantiles via the Cornish–Fisher expansion or
simulation. Thus, the model not only confirms our initial intuition but also generates a richer set
of testable predictions.
The key implications of our model for the effect of LOB imbalance on the return distribution
are as follows: the return mean and skewness of returns are monotonically increasing functions
of LOB imbalance, regardless of MO imbalance or LOB convexity, underscoring the role of LOB
imbalance in shaping these moments. By contrast, the effects of LOB imbalance on variance and
kurtosis depend on the sign of MO imbalance: with positive MO imbalance, variance rises and
kurtosis falls with imbalance, while the opposite holds when MO imbalance is negative. Further2

more, all quantiles of the return distribution (with the exception of the median) are higher than the
corresponding quantiles of a normal distribution when there is positive LOB imbalance regardless
of the LOB convexity. This implies that, with zero MO imbalance, a higher LOB imbalance shifts
both the left (below the median) and right (above the median) parts of the return distribution to the
right, while leaving the median fixed at zero. This suggests that the effects of the LOB imbalance
on the return mean and skewness dominate its effects on return variance and kurtosis.
Turning to LOB convexity, we find that its impact on the return variance and kurtosis is straightforward when considering symmetric bid- and ask-side convexities. Specifically, return variance
decreases, while kurtosis increases with LOB convexity. The combined effect of these findings on
the return distribution is that all quantiles below (above) the median increase (decrease) as LOB
convexity rises, irrespective of LOB imbalance. In line with the framework discussed earlier, our
reduced-form price formation model further suggests that higher LOB convexity leads to fatter
tails and higher kurtosis compared to a normal distribution, after accounting for its effect on return
variance. Moreover, when considering bid- and ask-side convexities separately, our model implies
that ask-side convexity primarily affects the right side of the return distribution, while bid-side
convexity influences the left side.
We test the implications of our reduced-form price formation model using two primary data
sources: the Trade and Quote (TAQ) database for return and market order distributions, and the
Thomson Reuters Tick History (TRTH) database for LOB shape parameters. To mitigate intraday
noise in higher moments, we aggregate all variables at the daily frequency. Specifically, we compute the first four central moments of the return distribution from five-minute midquote returns
within each trading day, and the first four central moments of the market order distribution from
signed trade volumes. For LOB shape parameters, we compute them at the snapshot level and then
take their time-weighted averages to construct daily proxies.
We estimate separate daily fixed-effects panel regressions of the first four central moments of
the return distribution on lagged values of LOB shape parameters. In these regressions, we control
for the contemporaneous effects of the first four central moments of the market order distribution,
as well as for the three central moments of the return distribution other than the one being analyzed.
We aggregate the variables at a daily level and assume that the return distribution on day t is
determined by the market order distribution on day t executed against the LOB on the previous
trading day t −1. This approach helps to mitigate endogeneity concerns in interpreting the effect
of LOB shape parameters on the shape of the return distribution.
3

Our results provide strong empirical support for the main predictions of our model. Specifically, we observe that return skewness increases with LOB imbalance, independent of MO imbalance and LOB convexity, consistent with our theoretical expectations. Although the economic
magnitude of this effect is somewhat smaller than predicted by our model, LOB imbalance ranks
as the second most economically significant factor among the MO and LOB variables considered.
Similarly, we find that variance decreases and kurtosis increases with LOB convexity, aligning
with our theoretical predictions. Notably, LOB convexity emerges as the second most economically significant variable influencing return variance and kurtosis.
To establish a causal link between LOB shape and the return distribution, we examine the
exogenous shock to LOB shape caused by Regulation SHO (Reg SHO), a 2005 rule governing
short selling and thus liquidity. The regulatory implications on the market were tested for a random
subsample in a pilot program, making it ideal for our analysis. Our approach closely follows
Diether et al. (2009), who examined Reg SHO’s effect on market quality. Specifically, we assess
the causal impact of LOB parameters on return moments by using the exogenous changes to LOB
shape induced by Reg SHO.
Our results indicate that changes in LOB convexity following Reg SHO are the primary driver
of changes in return variance and kurtosis. Specifically, LOB convexity for pilot stocks decreases
significantly more than for control stocks after the uptick rule’s suspension. This relative decrease
in LOB convexity accounts for the observed increase in return variance and decrease in return
kurtosis. We further show that other factors, such as shifts in the market order distribution, cannot
explain these changes in the return distribution.
Our paper contributes to both the theoretical and empirical literature on LOBs in many dimensions. Theoretically, our reduced-form price formation model extends Kyle’s framework (Kyle
(1985)) to allow not only for distinct price impact functions (lambdas) on the buy and sell sides but
also different price impacts of large versus small market orders. Both Rosu (2009) and Goettler,
Parlour, and Rajan (2009) develop theoretical models of the LOB where the price impact functions can be nonlinear. However, these and other theoretical papers, such as Foucault, Kadan, and
Kandel (2005) and Chakrabarty, Li, Nguyen, and Van Ness (2007), are mostly interested in the theoretical relation between the LOB and future price movements, i.e., the first moment of returns. To
the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to consider how the shape of the LOB determines
the whole distribution of returns and not just its first moment.
Empirically, there is a growing body of literature analyzing the information content of LOB
4

for future price movements. Biais, Hillion, and Spatt (1995) are among the first to explore the
dynamics of limit order markets and observe that price adjustments tend to follow the direction
of prior limit order flows, implying that the LOB contains valuable information regarding future
price trends. Among others, Hasbrouck (1991), Hasbrouck (1992), Keim and Madhavan (1996),
and Knez and Ready (1996), Kaniel and Liu (2006), Wuyts (2008), Kalay and Wohl (2009), Cao,
Hansch, and Wang (2009), Beltran-Lopez, Giot, and Grammig (2009), Kozhan and Salmon (2012),
R¨osch and Kaserer (2014), Cenesizoglu, Dionne, and Zhou (2022), Cenesizoglu and Grass (2018)
examine LOB data from markets around the world, such as US, Spain, Israel, Australia, and Germany, and show that LOBs are generally nonlinear and contain information about future price
movements. Once again, most of these papers focus on the empirical relation between the LOB
and future price movements, i.e. the first moment of returns. The closest paper to ours is Brennan,
Chordia, Subrahmanyam, and Tong (2012) who conduct an empirical analysis of buy- and sellside liquidity pricing using monthly transaction data. Our high-frequency data provides a unique
opportunity to uncover new empirical relationships between LOB shape and return distribution,
contributing significantly to the empirical literature on the effect of LOB on returns.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents our reduced-form price formation model. Section 3 discusses the implications of our reduced-form price formation model for
the effects of the shapes of LOB and market order distribution on the return distribution. Section
4 presents the data and the variable definitions. Section 5 presents the results from our regression
analysis. Section 6 attempts to establish a causal effect of LOB shape on the return distribution
based differences-in-differences analysis using RegSHO as an exogenous shock to LOB shape.
Finally, Section 7 concludes.

Several remarks are in order before proceeding to the implications of this model for the relation
between the shape of the LOB and the return distribution. First, we make the implicit assumption
that no other order (limit or market) arrives when computing returns based on a given market order
and shape of the LOB. This is realistic at ultra-high frequencies but less so over longer periods of
time. Second, our approach is intentionally reduced-form in the sense that we do not consider the
underlying economic dynamics that give rise to a specific market order and/or shape of the LOB. In
reality, they are jointly determined by the investors trading in consideration of past trading activity,
newly arrived information, or changes in portfolio preferences, among others. In our model, we
abstract from these complications in favor of a simple analysis of the relation between the shape
of the LOB and the return distribution.
Before deriving the moments of the return distribution based on this reduced-form model of
price formation, we define several variables which summarize the shape of the LOB. We start with
the LOB imbalance, denoted as I and defined as the ratio of the overall ask and bid side slopes,
i.e., I = SA/SB. In order to have a symmetric measure centered around zero, we consider the log
transformation of LOB imbalance (i = log(I)) when analyzing its relation with the return distribution in Section 3. A negatively infinite LOB imbalance indicates that the ask side is infinitely
more liquid than the bid side and the price does not change following a market buy order of any
size. Similarly, a positively infinite LOB imbalance indicates that the bid side is infinitely more
liquid than the ask side and the price does not change following a market sell order of any size.
Between these two extremes, a positive LOB imbalance indicates that the slope of the ask side is
higher than that of the bid side and thus a relative buying pressure from limit orders, given that
there is relatively more liquidity on the bid side. A LOB imbalance of zero indicates a balanced
LOB, or equivalently, no buying or selling pressure from the limit orders.

Proposition 1 shows that the non-central moments of the return distribution can be expressed
analytically as functions of the market order distribution parameters (µ, σ) and the LOB shape

skewness and kurtosis are invariant to changes in Savg because the numerator and denominator of

Implications for the Return Distribution
In this section, we examine how our reduced-form price formation model links the market order
distribution and the structure of the LOB to the return distribution. We begin with the effects of the
market order distribution. We then analyze two primary LOB shape variables—LOB imbalance
10

and convexity—and show that they are the main determinants of return dynamics. Finally, we
assess the effects of secondary LOB shape variables, such as total available quantity on each side of
the book, their ratio, and the average LOB slope, which, as we show, have limited direct influence
on the return distribution.
3.1
Effects of Market Order Distribution on the Return Distribution
The following lemma characterizes the return distribution for the special case of a balanced and
linear LOB and provides the implications of our reduced-form price formation model for the unconditional effects of the market-order distribution on the return distribution.
Lemma 1. Assume that the LOB is balanced and linear, i.e., SA,low = SA,high = SB,low = SB,high =
S and, thus, I = 1 and CA = CB = 1. The return can be expressed as r = SQ and is normally
distributed with a mean of Sµ and a variance of (Sσ)2 and, thus, has zero skewness and (excess)
kurtosis.
In Lemma 1, our assumption that the market orders are normally distributed implies that returns
are normally distributed when the LOB is balanced and linear. One can easily generalize the result
in Lemma 1 under a more general assumption for the distribution of market orders. Specifically, if
market orders have a probability density function f, i.e. Q ∼f(x), returns will have a distribution
function given by 1/Sf(x/S). In other words, the return distribution reflects (up to a multiplicative
factor which is the reciprocal of the LOB slope) the market order distribution when the LOB is
balanced and linear. This has important implications for our empirical analysis as it shows the
importance of controlling for the distribution of market orders when analyzing the effect of LOB
shape parameters on the return distribution.
Not surprisingly, Lemma 1 yields several additional implications consistent with established
market microstructure models, such as Kyle (1985). First, expected returns are positive (negative)
when there is expected buying (selling) pressure from market orders, i.e., when the MO imbalance
is positive (µ > 0). This follows naturally from the fact that prices tend to rise when market buy
orders outnumber market sell orders. Second, the slope of the LOB amplifies the effect of MO
imbalance on expected returns: a steeper slope leads to larger positive (negative) expected returns
when MO imbalance is positive (negative). Intuitively, a steeper slope reflects lower liquidity,
11

which increases the price impact of a given market order. Third, return volatility increases when
the volatility of market orders rises, since a more dispersed MO distribution implies a greater
likelihood of large trades, which in turn generate larger price changes. Finally, return volatility
also rises with the LOB slope, again because lower liquidity magnifies the price impact of trades,
thereby amplifying price fluctuations.

When there is no MO imbalance, the return mean is positive (negative) when LOB imbalance is
positive (negative). In other words, in the absence of MO imbalance, the sign of expected returns
is determined entirely by the sign of LOB imbalance: more liquidity on the bid side signals buying
pressure, raising expected prices and returns. This effect of LOB imbalance on the return mean
strengthens as LOB convexity declines.
The relationship between LOB imbalance and return variance is U-shaped, reaching its minimum when the LOB is balanced (i = 0). This U-shaped pattern is more pronounced when LOB

LOB imbalance is one standard deviation below its mean, compared with about 0.00098 (corresponding to a daily volatility of 3.14%) at its minimum. This represents a 3.1% change in variance
for a one-standard-deviation shift in LOB imbalance.
Return skewness increases monotonically with LOB imbalance in the absence of MO imbalance, and its sign mirrors that of the LOB imbalance regardless of LOB convexity. In other words,
without MO imbalance, returns are positively skewed if and only if LOB imbalance is positive.
This result aligns with the intuitive framework outlined in the introduction: a positive LOB imbalance signals buying pressure from limit orders, producing a positively skewed return distribution
in the absence of market-order-driven pressure. Moreover, the positive impact of LOB imbalance
on return skewness is amplified when LOB convexity is high.
Our model predicts that any LOB imbalance—positive or negative—produces excess kurtosis,
with greater LOB imbalance leading to higher values. Starting from three (the return kurtosis when
LOB is balanced), kurtosis increases monotonically as LOB imbalance moves away from zero in
either direction. The U-shaped pattern is more pronounced when LOB convexity is high, whereas
the effect is virtually negligible when convexity is low, i.e., set to minus one standard deviation of
its empirical counterpart.
We now turn our attention to the effect of LOB imbalance conditional on MO imbalance presented in panel (b). The return mean and skewness are increasing functions of LOB imbalance
regardless of MO imbalance. In other words, the effects of LOB imbalance on both the return
mean and skewness do not depend on MO imbalance. However, MO imbalance influences the
magnitude of these effects: for instance, the impact of LOB imbalance on return skewness is

smaller in absolute value when MO imbalance is nonzero. In contrast, MO imbalance substantially alters the effect of LOB imbalance on the return variance and kurtosis. Specifically, when
there is MO imbalance, the return variance and kurtosis cease to follow the U-shaped patterns discussed above. When MO imbalance is low, the return variance decreases monotonically with LOB
imbalance, while the return kurtosis increases monotonically. The opposite pattern emerges when
MO imbalance is high.

Effects of LOB Convexity on the Return Distribution

3.3.1
Effects of LOB Convexity on the Moments of the Return Distribution
As in the LOB imbalance analysis, we set the mean of the market order distribution to zero to
eliminate MO imbalance. We then consider three values of (log) LOB imbalance–high (+0.7040),
zero, and low (−0.7040)–to assess whether the impact of LOB convexity varies with LOB imbalance. We begin with symmetric LOB convexity, setting the ask- and bid-side convexities equal

with (log) LOB convexity under these assumptions. LOB convexity values range from minus to
plus one standard deviation of its sample counterpart (0.4226).
[Figure 4 about here.]
We begin with the return mean. The effect of LOB convexity on the return mean depends
crucially on the LOB imbalance. First of all, as discussed above, in the absence of MO imbalance,
the sign of the return mean is determined entirely by the sign of the LOB imbalance, regardless
of the LOB convexity. Second, the return mean decreases with LOB convexity when the LOB
imbalance is positive and increases with LOB convexity when the LOB imbalance is negative.
When the LOB imbalance is zero, the return mean remains zero for all values of the LOB convexity.
Finally, a larger (in magnitude) LOB imbalance amplifies the effect of LOB convexity on the
return mean, with the amplification being particularly pronounced for negative values of the LOB
convexity.
We now turn to the return variance. For all values of the LOB imbalance, the return variance
is a monotonically decreasing function of the LOB convexity. Consistent with our discussion in
Section 3.2, the effect of LOB convexity on the return variance changes little across different levels
of the LOB imbalance. As the LOB becomes more convex, the price impact of small market orders
declines because these orders are executed against the lower levels of the book, which are more
liquid than the higher levels when the LOB is more convex.
We now turn to the return skewness. As with the return mean, the effect of LOB convexity on
the return skewness depends critically on the LOB imbalance. In the absence of MO imbalance,
the sign of the return skewness is determined entirely by the sign of the LOB imbalance, regardless
of the LOB convexity. However, unlike the return mean, the return skewness increases with LOB
convexity when the LOB imbalance is positive and decreases with LOB convexity when the LOB
imbalance is negative. When the LOB imbalance is zero, the return skewness remains zero for
all values of the LOB convexity. Finally, a larger (in magnitude) LOB imbalance amplifies the
effect of LOB convexity on the return skewness, with this amplification being more pronounced
for positive values of the LOB convexity.
Finally, the return kurtosis increases monotonically with LOB convexity, regardless of the LOB
imbalance. In the absence of MO imbalance and LOB imbalance, returns are leptokurtic when the
book is convex and platykurtic when the book is concave, consistent with the intuitive frame16

work outlined in the introduction. As the book becomes more convex—or equivalently, less concave—the price impact of large market orders rises because they are executed against higher levels
of the book, which are less liquid than the lower levels. This greater price impact of large market
orders produces fatter tails in the return distribution. The opposite effect occurs when the book is
concave.

3.4
Effects of Secondary LOB Shape Variables on the Return Distribution
In this section, we briefly discuss the effects of the secondary LOB shape variables on the return
moments and distribution. We start with the average slope of the LOB. Recall from the discussion
following Proposition 1 that the average slope of the LOB, Savg, is a scaling factor for the noncentral moments, which implies that if the average LOB slope is twice as high, the first four
non-central moments of the return distribution increase by factors of 2, 4, 8, and 16, respectively.
This in turn yields the following implications for its effect on the return distribution: (1) The return
mean is zero regardless of the average LOB slope when both MO and LOB imbalances are zero.
Otherwise, the return mean can be either an increasing or a decreasing function of the average
slope, depending on the signs and magnitudes of the MO and LOB imbalances. (2) The return
variance is an increasing function of the average LOB slope. (3) The return skewness and kurtosis
do not depend on the average LOB slope. (4) The overall effect of the average slope on the return
distribution depends critically on the signs and magnitudes of the MO and LOB imbalances.
We next consider the total quantity available in the bid and ask sides of the LOB. When these
quantities are changed symmetrically, the effects on the return moments and distribution are similar to those observed for the average slope: (1) The return mean is zero when both MO and LOB
imbalances are zero. Otherwise, the return mean can be either an increasing or a decreasing function of the total quantity, depending on the signs and magnitudes of the MO and LOB imbalances.
(2) The return variance is an increasing function of the total quantity if and only if the LOB convexity is positive, regardless of the signs and magnitudes of the MO and LOB imbalances. (3) The
return skewness and kurtosis do not depend on the total quantity. (4) The overall effect of the total
quantity on the return distribution depends closely on the signs and magnitudes of the MO and
LOB imbalances.
18

Finally, when the quantity available on one side of the LOB—either the bid or the ask—is
varied while the quantity on the opposite side is held constant, our model yields the following
predictions: (1) When the LOB convexity is zero, the total quantity available on either side has no
effect on the return moments or distribution, regardless of the MO or LOB imbalances. (2) When
the LOB convexity is non-zero, the return moments can increase or decrease with the total quantity
available on a given side, with the direction of the effect determined by other model parameters,
including the signs and magnitudes of the MO and LOB imbalances.

on the signs of the MO and LOB imbalances (see Figure 4). In the next section, we extend our
empirical specification to include interactions between LOB imbalance, MO imbalance, and LOB
convexity, and examine whether the conditional effects of LOB convexity are consistent with the
model’s predictions.
5.1.2
Return Variance
We now examine the effects of the primary LOB shape variables on return variance, beginning
with LOB imbalance. The results show that LOB imbalance has a significantly positive effect
on next-day return variance. A one–standard deviation increase in LOB imbalance raises the return variance by approximately 0.00026, an economically meaningful magnitude corresponding
to roughly 10% of the standard deviation of return variance in our sample. Our theoretical model
predicts that return variance should increase (decrease) with LOB imbalance when MO imbalance is positive (negative), as shown in Panel (b) of Figure 2. Therefore, it is not immediately
clear whether the unconditional effect we observe aligns with the theory. We address this question in the next section by analyzing the effect of LOB imbalance conditional on MO imbalance.
Nevertheless, the magnitude of the unconditional effect is broadly consistent with the model’s predictions. Specifically, Panel (b) of Figure 2 shows that, when MO imbalance is high, the return
variance in the model rises from about 0.00023 to 0.00035—an increase of 0.00012—following
a one–standard deviation increase in LOB imbalance, compared with the empirical increase of
0.00026.
Turning to LOB convexity, we find that a one–standard deviation increase reduces next-day
return variance by approximately 0.00042. This effect is economically significant, representing
about 16% of the standard deviation of return variance in our sample. Among the LOB and MO
variables considered, LOB convexity emerges as the second most economically important variable
26

for explaining return variance. Both the sign and magnitude of this effect provide strong empirical
support for our theoretical predictions. In particular, our model predicts that return variance should
decrease with LOB convexity regardless of the sign and magnitude of LOB imbalance (Panel (b)
of Figure 4) and MO imbalance (Panel (b) of Figure 4). The theoretical magnitude of the effect
depends on both imbalances; for example, with zero MO and LOB imbalances, the model predicts
a decrease in return variance from about 0.00023 to 0.00008 when LOB convexity increases from
zero to one standard deviation. This prediction is comparable in magnitude to the empirical effect
of 0.00042 observed in the data.
5.1.3
Return Skewness
Regarding return skewness, we find that a one–standard deviation increase in LOB imbalance raises
next-day return skewness by 0.0134. The positive sign of this effect is consistent with our model,
which predicts that return skewness increases with LOB imbalance regardless of MO imbalance or
LOB convexity. The economic magnitude of this effect is small relative to the standard deviation
of return skewness in our sample (1.2313), and its empirical size is also well below the model’s
prediction. For instance, with zero LOB convexity and MO imbalance (solid line in Panel (c) of
Figure 2), the model predicts an increase in skewness from 0 to roughly 0.75 when LOB imbalance
rises from zero to one standard deviation. This gap may stem from the high noise in daily skewness
estimates and the inclusion of control variables in the empirical specification that are absent from
the theoretical model. Despite its modest absolute size, LOB imbalance ranks as the second most
economically important variable among the MO and LOB variables examined—consistent with the
theoretical expectation that LOB imbalance is a key determinant of return skewness. By contrast,
LOB convexity has no statistically significant effect on return skewness. This is not unexpected:
the model predicts that the sign and magnitude of the LOB convexity effect on skewness depend
critically on MO and LOB imbalances. Hence, an insignificant unconditional effect is consistent
with our theoretical model.
5.1.4
Return Kurtosis
Our empirical results on return kurtosis show that LOB imbalance has a significantly negative effect. The model predicts that, regardless of LOB convexity, kurtosis increases (decreases) with
27

LOB imbalance when MO imbalance is negative (positive) and follows a U-shaped pattern when
MO imbalance is zero (see Figure 2). Given that MO imbalance is positive on average in our sample, the negative sign of the empirical effect is consistent with theory. Economically, a one–standard
deviation increase in LOB imbalance reduces kurtosis by 0.13162, a modest change relative to the
standard deviation of kurtosis in our sample (5.4989). Still, LOB imbalance ranks as the third most
economically important determinant of kurtosis, and the effect size is comparable to the theoretical
prediction: when MO imbalance is positive, the model shows kurtosis falling from about 3 to 2.7
after such an increase (dotted line Return Kurtosis graph in Panel (b) of Figure 2)).
LOB convexity, in contrast, has a significantly positive effect on kurtosis, consistent with the
model’s prediction that kurtosis increases with convexity regardless of LOB or MO imbalance.
While the effect size is small relative to the empirical standard deviation of kurtosis (5.4989)
and smaller than the theoretical prediction, it remains economically meaningful. In the model,
a one–standard deviation increase in convexity raises kurtosis from 3 to about 6 (see Figure 4).
Moreover, convexity is the second most economically important determinant of kurtosis, in line
with the theoretical view that it should be one of the primary drivers of higher-order return moments.
5.2
Unconditional Effects of Other Variables on Return Moments
In this section, we summarize the effects of the first four moments of the market order (MO)
distribution and the secondary LOB shape variables, namely the total cumulative quantity and
the slope of the LOB. The mean and standard deviation of the MO distribution, as well as the
secondary LOB shape variables, are directly implied by our theoretical framework, allowing for
a straightforward comparison between the empirical estimates and the model’s predictions. By
contrast, the skewness and kurtosis of the MO distribution are not explicitly incorporated in the
model. Nonetheless, as discussed in Section 3, our framework suggests that the third and fourth
moments of the MO distribution may exert significant effects on the corresponding moments of the
return distribution.
We begin with the first four moments of the market order (MO) distribution before turning to
the secondary LOB shape variables. An increase in the MO imbalance is positively associated
with the contemporaneous return mean. Specifically, a one standard deviation increase in MO
mean corresponds to an increase of approximately 20 basis points in the return mean, or about 6%
of the standard deviation of daily returns in our sample. The sign of this effect is both intuitive and
28

consistent with our theoretical predictions. However, its magnitude is smaller than that implied
by our model: Panel (b) of Figure 2 shows that, in the absence of LOB imbalance, a one standard
deviation increase in MO mean raises the return mean by slightly more than 1%. Nevertheless,
MO mean emerges as the most important determinant of the return mean.
Turning to the higher moments of MO imbalance, we find that the effect of MO standard deviation on return mean is neither statistically nor economically significant, in line with our model’s
prediction that MO standard deviation should not be a key driver of return mean. By contrast, MO
skewness and MO kurtosis exhibit significantly negative and significantly positive effects, respectively, on the return mean. It is difficult to assess whether these findings align with our theoretical
framework, as the latter assumes a normally distributed MO imbalance.
The MO imbalance mean has no statistically significant effect on the return variance, whereas
the MO imbalance standard deviation has a significantly positive effect. These results are consistent with Lemma 1, which implies that, in the absence of LOB imbalance, the return distribution
should mirror the MO imbalance distribution. That said, the economic magnitude and importance
of the MO standard deviation are smaller than expected; it ranks only sixth among the eight determinants considered. As previously noted, our model is largely silent on the effects of MO skewness
and kurtosis on return variance. Intuitively, one might expect MO skewness to be negatively related and MO kurtosis to be positively related to return variance. Our empirical findings support
this intuition: MO kurtosis has a significantly positive effect on return variance and is the third
most economically important determinant of this moment.
The MO imbalance mean and skewness both have significantly positive effects on return skewness, consistent with the expectations from Lemma 1. MO imbalance is by far the most important
determinant of return skewness, whereas MO skewness ranks only fifth in economic importance.
In contrast, neither MO variance nor MO kurtosis has a statistically significant effect on return
skewness, also in line with our expectations under Lemma 1, which does not predict a relationship
between these variables and return skewness.
The MO standard deviation has a significantly positive effect on return kurtosis, consistent with
Lemma 1 and the positive relation between variance and kurtosis of a distribution. Nevertheless, it
ranks only as the fourth most economically important determinant of return kurtosis. In contrast,
MO imbalance mean and skewness have no significant effects, as expected, since the mean and
skewness of a distribution are generally unrelated to its kurtosis. Surprisingly, MO imbalance
kurtosis has a significantly negative effect on return kurtosis, whereas Lemma 1 would suggest a
29

positive effect. This discrepancy may be due to the fact that Lemma 1 applies only in the absence
of LOB imbalance; in our setting, LOB shape variables are by far the most important determinants
of return kurtosis, and their dominant role may alter the impact of MO imbalance kurtosis.
We now turn to the effects of the secondary LOB shape variables: the average slope of the
LOB and the average total quantity available. The average LOB slope has significantly negative
effects on return mean and skewness, and significantly positive effects on return variance and kurtosis. Our model predicts that return variance should increase with the average LOB slope, while
return skewness and kurtosis should be unaffected. Thus, the positive effect on return variance is
consistent with the model, whereas the effects on return skewness and kurtosis are not. Regarding
the return mean, the model offers no clear prediction.
The average total quantity available in the LOB has significantly positive effects on return
mean and variance, and significantly negative effects on skewness and kurtosis. As with the average slope, the positive effect on return variance is consistent with the model—recall that the model
predicts an increasing relation when LOB convexity is positive, regardless of the signs and magnitudes of MO and LOB imbalances—while the effects on skewness and kurtosis are not. The model
does not provide a clear prediction for its effect on the return mean.

6
Establishing Causality
Since we use lagged LOB shape parameters in our analysis above, we argue that the preceding
results provide evidence of a causal relationship between LOB shape and return distribution. In
this section, we offer additional empirical support for this causal effect from a different perspective.
Our approach closely follows Diether et al. (2009), who examine how the suspension of the uptick
rule impacts market quality. In this section, we provide further empirical evidence on this causal
effect from a different angle. Our approach follows closely Diether et al. (2009), who analyze the
effect of suspension of the uptick rule on market quality. Specifically, we exploit the suspension
of the uptick rule for pilot stocks as an exogenous shock to the shape of the LOB and market order
distribution, and we show that the resulting changes in the return distribution are consistent with
our theoretical predictions. To this end, we first discuss the expected implications of the uptick
rule suspension for LOB and market order distributions, and present supporting evidence. We then
35

examine how the return distribution of pilot stocks, relative to control stocks, changes as a result
of the suspension. These changes, we argue, align with our theoretical expectations based on the
observed LOB and market order dynamics.
Short-selling restrictions were introduced in the U.S. in the aftermath of the 1929 market crash
to curb speculative pressure. The SEC introduced the uptick rule in 1938, which restricted short
sales on the NYSE unless the last price movement was upward. Regulation SHO, adopted in
September 2004, aimed to modernize short sale regulation in response to evolving market conditions and concerns over persistent delivery failures and potential abuses in naked short selling.
Rule 202T of Regulation SHO, known as the Pilot Program, serves as the focus of our analysis.
Its goal was to assess the impact of short sale price tests on market volatility, price efficiency, and
liquidity, as in Diether et al. (2009). Under Rule 202T, the SEC removed price test restrictions
for approximately 1,000 designated securities—termed pilot stocks—starting May 2, 2005. Every
third stock in the Russell 3000 index, sorted by volume, was selected as a pilot stock; the remaining
2,000 served as controls. The SEC eliminated short sale price tests for all stocks on July 6, 2007,
marking the conclusion of the Pilot Program.
Given that we only have LOB data for NYSE stocks, we focus on the effect of the Pilot Program
on the NYSE stocks. Diether et al. (2009) argue that the uptick rule of the NYSE significantly
affects how the specialist adjust non-compliant short sell orders so that they can be presented to
the market. Specifically, non-compliant short-sale orders, i.e. those that are not submitted after
an uptick, are effectively changed into nonmarketable limit orders and added to the LOB. As a
result, Diether et al. (2009) argue that when there are short-selling restrictions there would be a
disproportionate depth at the best ask and a disproportionate amount of trades executing above the
midquote, creating a positive market order imbalance. They also argue based on Jones (2003) that
passive short-sale order strategies adopted by short-sellers when faced with short-selling restrictions may also result in lower short-term volatility. Once the uptick rule is suspended for pilot
stocks, short-sale orders can no longer be classified as non-compliant and thus are not changed
into nonmarketable limit orders or added to the book. Thus, one would expect a decrease in the
depth at the best ask price, a decrease in the market order imbalance and an increase in short-term
volatility for pliot stocks relative to control stocks following the suspension of the uptick rule for
pilot stocks. They provide empirical evidence in support of these hypothesis using differences-indifferences analysis.
By construction, LOB imbalance and convexity depend on quoted depths at the best bid and
36

ask. Diether et al. (2009) document a significant decline in quoted depth following the rule suspension for pilot stocks. Consequently, we expect both the imbalance and convexity of the LOB to
change, absolutely and relatively, for pilot stocks. Similarly, the suspension of the uptick rule for
pilot stocks might also affect the higher moments of their market order distributions, in addition
to the changes in their first moments, i.e. the market order imbalance, reported by Diether et al.
(2009). While it is straightforward to hypothesize about changes in quoted depths at the best bid
and ask as well as the mean market order imbalance, it is less clear how higher moments of the
market order distribution and other LOB shape parameters would respond to the Pilot Program.
We therefore empirically investigate how these features evolve following the implementation of
the Pilot Program.

kurtosis increases more for pilot stocks—which should push return kurtosis up—LOB convexity
declines substantially more for pilot stocks, and since return kurtosis is increasing in convexity,
this convexity effect dominates, driving a net decline in return kurtosis for pilot stocks.
Figure 8 and Table 8 further illustrate the impact of the Pilot Program on the return distribution
by examining selected quantiles. Across the board, quantiles for pilot stocks shift to the right,
indicating an overall increase in return levels. For control stocks, the quantiles below the median, including the median and 75th percentile, increase significantly, while changes in the upper
quantiles (90th and 95th percentiles) are statistically insignificant. Comparing these shifts reveals
interesting asymmetries. The increase in lower quantiles is larger for control stocks, suggesting a
smaller upward shift in the left tail for pilot stocks. However, the higher quantiles increase more
for pilot stocks than for controls, indicating a stronger upward shift in the right tail. These combined effects suggest that, relative to control stocks, pilot stocks experienced both a broader return
distribution and a greater upward skew in their upper tails. These patterns reinforce the conclusion
that the increase in variance outweighs the decrease in kurtosis, leading to a simultaneous widening
and rightward shift in the return distribution of pilot stocks.

In summary, our results in this section confirm our theoretical predictions. First, we find that
LOB convexity is the primary determinant of return variance and is negatively related to it. Consistent with this prediction, return variance increases significantly for pilot stocks relative to control
stocks, largely due to a sharper decline in LOB convexity for pilot stocks. Second, LOB convexity
also emerges as the main determinant of return kurtosis, with a positive relationship. Accordingly,
we observe a decline in return kurtosis for pilot stocks while it rises for control stocks, a divergence
39

driven by the pronounced reduction in LOB convexity for pilot stocks, which more than offsets the
concurrent increase in their market order (MO) kurtosis. For the return mean and skewness, the
evidence is less definitive. While the return mean increases for both pilot and control stocks, the
difference is statistically insignificant, suggesting that the offsetting effects of MO and LOB imbalances—both key determinants of expected returns—neutralize each other across groups. Similarly,
return skewness increases for both groups without a significant differential effect, likely reflecting
two opposing forces: a relatively smaller decline in LOB imbalance for pilot stocks, which tends
to raise skewness, and a larger drop in MO skewness, which tends to lower it. Overall, these findings emphasize the central role of LOB shape—particularly convexity—in driving changes in the
higher moments of return distributions.
7
Conclusion
In this paper, we developed a reduced-form microstructure model of price formation to study the
joint role of market and limit orders in shaping the distribution of returns in an order-driven market. Our theoretical framework posits that the distribution of market orders and the shape of the
limit order book jointly determine the moments of the return distribution. By deriving closed-form
expressions for the first four central moments, we established clear predictions on how LOB imbalance and convexity affect the mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis and the quantiles of the
return distribution.
Our empirical analysis, based on high-frequency data for NYSE stocks, provides strong support
for these predictions. We find that LOB imbalance primarily drives the mean and skewness of
returns, independently of the market order distribution. By contrast, LOB convexity plays a central
role in determining return variance and kurtosis, underscoring the link between market liquidity,
transaction costs, and tail risk in asset returns.
Exploiting the Reg SHO pilot program as an exogenous shock to market liquidity, we further
establish a causal relationship between LOB shape and return moments. In particular, we show
that changes in LOB convexity were the main driver of shifts in return variance and kurtosis following the suspension of the uptick rule, reinforcing the importance of market structure in shaping
return dynamics. In addition, our analysis of return quantiles around the Reg SHO pilot program
reveals asymmetric shifts in the return distribution of pilot stocks relative to controls. Pilot stocks
experienced a stronger upward shift in the right tail but a weaker shift in the left tail, implying both
a widening and rightward shift of the distribution. These results reinforce our conclusion that the
40

increase in variance outweighed the decrease in kurtosis.
Taken together, our findings contribute to the literature on price formation and market microstructure by providing a unified model that links order book shape to the distribution of stock
returns. These results carry important implications for both market participants and regulators,
highlighting the role of LOB dynamics in determining risk, especially tail risk, and return in equity
markets.

The Effects of Asymmetric LOB convexity
We now examine the effect of asymmetric LOB convexity on the first four central moments of the return distribution.
Specifically, we set the (log) ask-side convexity to zero—so the ask side is linear—and vary the bid-side convexity
over the same range used above, and vice versa. Figures OA 1 and OA 2 show how the first four central moments
change as functions of the bid- and ask-side convexities, respectively.
First, consistent with the results for symmetric convexity, the return variance decreases and the return kurtosis
increases with both bid- and ask-side convexities, regardless of the LOB imbalance. The effects on the return mean
and skewness differ slightly from the symmetric case. Here, we focus on the bid-side convexity, noting that the results
for the ask-side convexity are similar but with opposite signs.
The return mean increases with bid-side convexity for all values of the LOB imbalance considered. Holding other
factors constant, increasing bid-side convexity reduces the slopes of the lower levels of the bid side and increases the
slopes of the higher levels. This shifts liquidity toward the lower levels of the bid side, increasing buying pressure and,
in turn, raising the return mean.
The effect on return skewness is more nuanced. Skewness increases with bid-side convexity when the LOB imbalance is zero or high but decreases when the LOB imbalance is low. The reason is that the rate at which buying pressure
from the lower levels of the bid side rises with bid-side convexity depends on the LOB imbalance: this increase in
buying pressure is much slower when the LOB imbalance is low than when it is zero or high. Consequently, under
low LOB imbalance, higher bid-side convexity results in lower skewness.

Finally, Figure OA 3 shows the effects of bid-side convexity (panels (a)–(c)) and ask-side convexity (panels (d)–(f))
on the quantiles of the return distribution. Panels (a) and (d) present the inverse CDF of simulated returns for low, zero,
and high values of bid- and ask-side convexities, respectively, under the same assumptions as above. As in Figure 5 of
the main text, the solid lines in panels (a) and (d) correspond to the quantiles of a normal distribution because returns
are normally distributed with zero mean when MO imbalance, LOB imbalance, and both bid- and ask-side convexities
are zero.
Panels (a) and (d) reveal that ask-side convexity affects only the right side of the return distribution, while bid-side
convexity affects only the left side. Moreover, the right (left) side of the return distribution shifts left (right) as askside (bid-side) convexity increases, mirroring the patterns observed for symmetric convexity in Figure 5 of the main
text. However, the effects of ask- and bid-side convexity on return quantiles—through their influence on the first four
central moments—are more nuanced.
Consider the ask-side convexity when LOB imbalance is zero. All else equal, an increase in ask-side convexity
shifts the return distribution to the left through its effects on the return mean and skewness (see Figure 2 of the main
text). At the same time, higher ask-side convexity produces a tighter distribution via its effect on return variance, but a
wider distribution via its effect on kurtosis. Panel (d) shows that, for quantiles above the median, the combined effects
of mean, variance, and skewness dominate the effect of kurtosis, resulting in a leftward shift of the right tail when
ask-side convexity is high. These opposing effects offset each other for quantiles below the median, leaving the left

Robustness Checks
In this section, we assess the robustness of our results reported in Tables 3 and 6 of the main text to alternative variable
definitions and empirical choices. For brevity, we summarize the robustness checks and the corresponding findings.
3.1
Using Different Levels for LOB Variables
In our main results, we measure LOB variables based on the first ten levels of the bid and ask sides. We adopt this
choice to incorporate all available information. However, if investors engage in aggressive order splitting, small market
orders may not reach the upper levels of the book and could instead be executed against only the lower levels. In such
cases, LOB measures constructed from the first ten levels might not be the most relevant, since relatively few market
orders would interact with all ten levels.
To address this concern, we recompute the LOB measures using only the first three or five levels of the bid and ask
sides. All LOB variables except convexity can be straightforwardly adjusted to these alternative depths. For convexity,
we modify the definition to reflect the reduced number of levels. Specifically, for the ask side, we define convexity at
