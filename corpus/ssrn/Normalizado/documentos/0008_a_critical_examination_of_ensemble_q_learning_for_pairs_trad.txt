Abstract 
We investigate whether spurious pairs created by multiple hypothesis testing can be minimized 
when firm characteristics are used to identify pairs. The results show that the portfolios of pairs 
that have higher similarities in firm characteristics outperform those that are fundamentally less 
similar by minimizing the non-convergence risk. The cost of trading spurious pairs is 
significant despite the empirical results that the profitability of pairs trading has continued to 
decline since 2003 and is not significant any more. Accounting information plays a crucial role 
in identifying pairs rather than market trading data, and the importance of firm fundamentals 
in pairs trading increases during market crises.

1. Introduction 
Pairs trading, a type of statistical arbitrage strategy, is designed to exploit temporal price 
deviation between two stocks that have a long-term relationship in their prices. Although the 
concept of pairs trading is simple and easy to understand, an empirical implementation of pairs 
trading is not. One important issue lies in identifying “pairs” that exhibit long-term relationship 
among many stocks, because incorrectly identifying pairs can increase non-convergence risk 
(Lin et al., 2006; Do and Faff, 2010, 2012; Caldeira and Moura, 2013; Papanicolaou and Yeo, 
2017; Stübinger et al., 2018). 
One reason that contributes to the non-convergence risk is the multiple hypothesis testing 
problem (Harvey et al., 2018), i.e., the number of wrongly identified pairs exponentially 
increases with the number of stocks, even though none of them have any long-term relationship. 
The robustness of the co-integration test (Engle and Granger, 1987; Johansen, 1991) or any 
other statistical tests becomes tarnished as the number of stocks to be tested increases. For 
example, the number of pairs incorrectly identified as having long-term relationship is

Although a higher level of significance can relieve the multiple hypothesis testing problem (i.e., 
three standard deviations, as suggested by Harvey et al. (2018)), applying too strict criteria can 
also increase type II error, that is, pairs with long-term relationship are rejected. 
To overcome this problem, we identify pairs that have similar fundamentals in addition to 
the conventional co-integration and minimum distance tests. For this purpose, we propose a 
measure called ‘firm characteristics similarity’ (FCS) using a large number of firm-level 
characteristics that are known to explain stock returns in the literature (Green et al., 2017). The 
firm characteristics that we use in this study are a subset of the firm characteristics summarized 
in Harvey et al. (2018), but they are sufficient for identifying firm fundamentals (Hwang and 
Rubesam, 2021), considering close correlations between firm characteristics (Green et al., 
2017). For the analysis of the large number of firm characteristics (independent variables) that 
are possibly correlated, we adapt the elastic net regression because it can minimize overfitting

and multicollinearity problems and also allows us to evaluate which firm characteristics matter

Our empirical results in the US equity market indicate that FCS is an effective tool to 
minimize the multiple hypothesis testing problem. The performance difference of pairs 
between high and low FCS is significant and the fundamental closeness measured by FCS can 
significantly reduce the non-convergence risk: the Sharpe ratios of portfolios with higher FCSs 
are greater than those with lower FCSs. The difference between the two portfolios suggests that 
forming pairs with higher similarities in their firm characteristics reduces the non-convergence 
risk by eliminating spurious pairs as compared with the pairs that are formed using only market 
data, such as price. 
Despite the outperformance of the pairs with fundamental similarities, these pairs do not 
show a significant alpha any more in the 2010s. As in Gatev et al. (2006), the profitability of 
pairs trading continues to decrease because of active arbitrage trading and the changes in 
trading environment and information (Green et al., 2016), and pairs trading does not seem to 
be profitable any more in the US market. The results indicate that temporal price deviations 
that can be exploited by arbitrage trading are less likely to arise in the future, and if any, these 
pairs may be spurious.  
Interestingly, the cost of trading spurious pairs is still significant in the 2010s despite the 
poor performance of pairs trading since 2003. As the difference between high and low FCS 
portfolios reflects the difference in the identification of spurious pairs, the performance 
difference between these two portfolios can be interpreted as the cost that arbitrageurs bear in 
trading spurious pairs if they do not consider fundamentals. The significance of the cost in the 
2010s suggests that arbitragers still trade wrongly identified pairs and suffer losses although 
most profit opportunities in pairs trading have disappeared. In fact, the cost reflects the multiple 
hypothesis testing problem that arbitrageurs face in practice and shows that exogenous 
variables such as firm characteristics that we use in this study would be useful to overcome the 
problem. 
Finally, we find that accounting information plays an important role in determining 
fundamental closeness. Firm characteristics such as capital expenditures and inventory (Chen 
and Zhang, 2010), current ratio (Ou and Penman,1989), financial statement score (Mohanram, 
2005), leverage (Bhandari, 1988), and industry sales concentration (Hou and Robinson, 2006)

appear more important than market trading information, i.e., illiquidity (Amihud, 2002), 
momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993), and size (Banz, 1981). In addition, the importance 
of fundamental closeness increases during crises, i.e., the global financial crisis, the dot-com 
bubble, and the Asian financial crisis. The relative importance of accounting information 
during crises indicates that pairs identified with accounting variables are less likely to be 
affected by sudden shocks in financial markets. On the other hand, pairs identified during crises 
by market data such as prices are more likely to be spurious because prices are exposed to 
behavioral biases such as sentiment or herding, the effects of which on prices become 
significant during these periods (Kumar, 2009; Hwang et al., 2021).  
While we can minimize the multiple hypothesis test problem using firm characteristics in 
pairs trading in the equity market, there are some limitations in our study, which we leave for 
future studies. For example, different results may be obtained for pairs trading in other asset 
classes or in other countries. It is also possible that refined methods for identifying pairs may 
be still profitable for arbitrageurs. We may have different conclusions when pairs are identified 
with machine learning that can incorporate big data and nonlinearity. For some assets like 
cryptocurrencies, it would be difficult to minimize non-convergence risk because their 
fundamentals are not available.     
Our study differs from others in several perspectives. A few studies have explored the use 
of fundamental data as criteria for forming pairs to minimize spurious pairs. However, they 
simply apply fragmentary classification using a few firm characteristics such as size or bookto-market ratio (Franco, 2014; Jakobsson, 2015). Recent studies, including Van der Have et al. 
(2017), Yoshikawa (2020), Sarmento and Horta (2020), and Han et al. (2020), have explored 
pair formation using machine learning techniques. However, these studies are prone to 
overfitting because they do not consider the fundamental similarity within pairs. On the other 
hand, we impose stricter restrictions for stocks that are used in the analysis to minimize these 
problems, the details of which will be explained later.  
This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we review previous studies on pairs 
trading. In Section 3, we describe the data collected for empirical analysis and the 
preprocessing method. In Section 4, we explain the basic pairs trading model and then present 
a novel approach that considers firm characteristics. In Section 5, we compare quintile 
portfolios based on FCS. Finally, we summarize the main findings, followed by a discussion 
on their implications.

2. Literature Review 
Pairs trading has been widely used since its introduction by Morgan Stanley in the mid1980s. Early studies (Gatev et al., 2006; Rad et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2017) demonstrate that 
pairs trading has a risk-adjust return which cannot be explained by Fama and French’s (1993) 
three factors or the four factors of Carhart (1997).  
Andrade et al. (2005) explain that temporal price divergence of pairs reflects noninformation shocks caused by noise traders, concluding that the profitability of pairs trading is 
a compensation for providing liquidity for the long-term relationship between pairs. This 
“Compensation for Providing Liquidity” hypothesis is supported by empirical evidence that 
pairs trading performs better in bear markets when liquidity is low (Andrade et al., 2005; 
Bowen and Hutchinson, 2014; Do and Faff, 2010; Jacobs and Weber, 2015; Jakobsson, 2015). 
On the other hand, Gatev et al. (2006) suggest that the source of those abnormal returns is a 
short-term violation of the “Law of One Price”. They document that although the risk-adjusted 
return of pairs trading still exists, the profitability of pairs trading tends to decrease over time 
by increased hedge fund activity, which is supported by other recent studies (Jakobsson, 2015, 
Rad et al, 2016; Clegg and Krauss,2018).   
Various approaches have been proposed for improving the profitability of pairs trading. 
Some studies try to improve the profitability by increasing the number of trades per pair in a 
constrained time by selecting pairs with high mean-reversion strength. For example, Do and 
Faff (2010) select pairs with high reversal frequency during the pair formation period, while 
Ramos-Requena et al. (2017) select pairs with the lowest Hurst exponent, which help to find 
the more anti-persistent pairs. Papanicolaou and Yeo (2017) use Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process 
for identifying pairs with high-speed mean reversion.  
Others try to reduce non-convergence risk. In pairs trading, non-convergence risk occurs 
when the price divergence of paired stocks does not converge. One reason for non-convergence 
risk is incorrectly identified pairs because of the multiple hypothesis testing problem. For 
example, when forming pairs, most studies adopt various statistical methods, such as distance 
measures (Gatev et al., 2006; Do and Faff, 2010; Jacobs and Weber, 2015), co-integration test 
(Vidyamurthy, 2004; Lin et al., 2006; Caldeira and Moura, 2013), or copula functions (Rad et 
al., 2016; Stübinger et al., 2018). Regardless of test methods, the number of incorrectly 
identified pairs increases exponentially with the number of stocks, even if none of them have 
any long-term relationship.

This is a serious problem in practice where an almost infinite number of pairs can be tested 
for a large number of high-quality financial data (e.g., Harvey et al., 2018). To solve this 
problem, some studies form pairs within industry groups. Dunis et al. (2010) divide stocks into 
10 Bloomberg sector classifications. Do and Faff (2010) use the 48 industry groups identified 
by the Fama and French (1997) industry classification. Others use firm characteristics besides 
market data to form pairs. For example, Franco (2014) form pair with common risk factors, 
such as size and value, and momentum as well as sector classifications. Jakobsson (2015) uses 
the lasso model with firm characteristics to predict returns and trading days for pairs trading. 
Test procedures have also been refined to reduce the non-convergence risk. Gupta and 
Chatterjee (2020) add dynamic cross-correlation method, which considers the phenomenon of 
‘lead-lag’ relationship between two time series, to the distance measures to identify pairs. 
Figuerola-Ferretti et al. (2017) use the super-cointegration method, which combines the 
Johansen (1991) and Engle–Granger co-integration tests (Engle and Granger, 1987), and set 
the confidence level to 1% to minimize the number of spurious pairs, as suggested by Harvey 
et al. (2018).  
Another reason for non-convergence risk in pairs trading is the fact that long-term 
relationships assumed from historical behavior are not always sustained into the future, which 
lead to a dramatic loss in pairs trading. Clegg and Krauss (2018) utilize partial cointegration, 
which distinguish between transient and permanent shocks to the long-term relationship, to 
select the optimal threshold at which trades are entered. Many other studies investigate 
structural changes in fundamentals to minimize the non-convergence risk. For example, 
Papadakis and Wysocki (2007) suggest that the profitability of pairs trading can be improved 
by limiting trading after accounting events. Engelberg et al. (2009) and Jacobs and Weber (2015) 
find that the profitability of trades triggered by common shocks to a pair is greater than that 
triggered by information-related shocks to only one stock in the pair. Mazo et al. (2016) show 
that structural breaks in fundamental ratios of a pair can be used to improve the profitability of 
pairs trading. Chang et al. (2020) apply the prediction test (Lütkepohl, 2005) to pairs trading, 
which detect structural changes in vector autoregression process to test whether or not the 
market-neutrality is still valid. 
With the recent popularity in machine learning,2 several studies have adapted various 
algorithms to improve the profitability of pairs trading. Some studies utilize unsupervised

In summary, the studies in pairs trading suggest that identifying pairs with similar 
fundamentals in the same industry is of the utmost importance. Testing similarities in terms of 
price co-movements could yield spurious pairs. Moreover, increasing the significance level is 
likely to reject true pairs. To overcome this multiple hypothesis testing problem, we use 
numerous firm characteristics to identify pairs in addition to the conventional methods. With 
these additional firm characteristics data, we can minimize the possibility of choosing spurious 
pairs and rejecting true pairs. For the analysis, we employ the elastic net regression for the large 
number of firm characteristics we use in this study.  
3. Pairs Trading and Firm Characteristics Similarity 
As any price deviation of a pair is more likely to disappear if two stocks in the pair have 
similar fundamentals, it is important to check if potential pairs chosen from statistical methods 
still feature similar fundamentals. In this section, we first describe pair selection criteria, 
trading rules, and performance measurements, and then explain how we calculate firm 
characteristics similarity (FCS) using firm characteristics.

3.2 Firm Characteristics Similarity 
The multiple hypothesis testing problem is serious in pairs trading because the number 
of pairs increases exponentially with the number of stocks even if there is no genuine pair. To 
minimize spurious pairs and not to miss out the true pairs, we use firm characteristics to filter 
out spurious pairs which are included in the pairs identified by the SSD and con-integration 
tests.  
We propose a measure for fundamental similarity (i.e., FCS) that shows the degree of 
closeness between stocks in terms of their fundamentals. FCS is constructed to satisfy the 
following criteria.  
- 
Firm characteristics of two stocks in a pair should be similar. 
- 
Firm characteristics should matter in pairs trading 
- 
Pairs with similar firm characteristics should increase the performance of pairs trading.  
The first criterion is clear: if firm characteristic of any two stocks in a pair are similar, they 
are fundamentally similar. The second one is required to find out which firm characteristics are 
important in pairs trading. For example, even if the two stocks in a pair have similar firm 
characteristics, some firm characteristics are not useful if they do not play any role in asset 
pricing, in particular, in pairs trading. Finally, the third one is required such that the 
performance of pairs trading increases when pairs have similar fundamentals because these 
pairs are less likely to be spurious.

4. Empirical Tests 
4.1. Data 
We use common shares (i.e., with a share code of 10 or 11) listed on the New York Stock 
Exchange (NYSE), American Stock Exchange (AMEX), and Nasdaq that have been widely 
investigated previously. In order to investigate the performance of pairs trading in practice, we 
impose a strict restriction for the stocks that are included in our analysis. Stocks with a trading 
volume less than the bottom 20% over the past 3 months, stocks with a price less than $1, and 
stocks whose size is less than 20% of the NYSE are excluded from trading to eliminate illiquid 
and non-short-selling stocks.  
We use a large number of firm characteristics that are known to predict stock returns in 
the literature. A total number of 81 firm characteristics of Green et al. (2017), which are listed 
in Table 1, are used. To reduce the impact of outliers in firm characteristics and make them 
comparable, we winsorize and then normalize each firm characteristic every month.  
For the identification of pairs, we use monthly data. Once pairs are identified, we trade 
them on a daily basis using daily returns from the Center for Research in Security Prices 
(CRSP). Our sample period is from January 1980 to December 2018. 
4.2. Performance of Pairs Trading 
The performance of P1 (pairs with similar fundamentals) in Table 2 is significant at the 
1% level for all sectors, but changes significantly for different sectors, i.e., from 1.08% per 
month in the financial sector to 0.49% per month in the retail sector. However, Figures 1 and 2 
show that most of these profits were possible in the 1980s and 1990s, and the profitability of 
pairs trading has declined sharply since 2003. These results are consistent with Gatev et al. 
(2006) and Rad et al. (2016).

Our main concern is whether the performance of pairs trading improves when pairs are 
identified considering fundamental similarity. The results in Table 2 indicate that portfolios 
formed on FCS show a significant difference in returns in all six industries: pairs with high 
FCS (P1) earn higher returns than those with low FCS (P5). For example, the returns difference 
between P1 and P5 in the finance sector is 0.64% per month and its annual Sharpe ratio is 1.24, 
which is statistically significant at the 5% level. The other sectors also show similar results. 
What drives the performance of pairs that have fundamental similarity? The results in 
Table 3 show that the performance of fundamentally similar pairs have a lower nonconvergence risk than that of fundamentally dissimilar pairs. For example, the average return 
of non-converged pairs of P1 (Close) in the transportation and public utilities sector is only 
−3.56%, while that of converged pairs is 6.72%. On the other hand, those of non-converged 
and converged pairs of P5 (Remote) are -6.52% and 8.08%, respectively. Compared with P5 
(Remote), the negative return of the non-converged pairs of P1 (Close) is much smaller than 
the positive return of its converged pairs, i.e., -3.56% vs -6.52%. Moreover, the standard 
deviations of the returns of pairs are significantly lower when the pairs are fundamentally 
similar. Other statistics in Table 3 do not show any significant difference between P1 and P5: 
i.e., there is little difference between the FCS of sorted portfolios in terms of convergence rate, 
average number of trades, average number of traded pairs, average trading period, and level of 
co-integration.6  
These results support our main claim that the non-convergence risk can be reduced by 
identifying the pairs that have similar fundamentals. The main difference in the performance 
of portfolios formed on FCS arises from the difference in the returns of non-converging pairs. 
Spurious pairs can be filtered out by FCS, reducing losses from non-convergence. The closer 
the firm characteristics are in a pair, the more likely the price difference is to be temporal and 
thus to converge. Therefore, we can identify more robust pairs by considering firm 
characteristics rather than only market data. This implies that using firm characteristics enables 
circumventing the multiple hypothesis testing problem by minimizing the number of spurious 
pairs.

4.3. The Cost of Spurious Pairs (COSP) 
Our main results show that pairs trading is exposed to the non-convergence risk from 
trading spurious pairs when they are identified with the conventional co-integration and SSD 
tests without considering fundamental similarity. But how large is the difference?  
We calculate the cost of trading spurious pairs from the difference between P1 and P5 
because our results in Table 2 report that P5 (pairs with dissimilar fundamentals) suffers heavier 
losses from trading spurious pairs than P1. Table 2 reports that all six industries show 
significant difference in the performance of the two portfolios, ranging from 0.18% per month 
in the Transportation to 0.64% per month in the financial sector.  
Interestingly, the COSP does not disappear in the 2010s. Figure 3 shows the cumulative 
return of COSP for each sector, indicating that the performance of COPS have still positive 
returns after 2003. Following Green et al (2016), we divide the sample period into pre-2003 
(the period ending Dec. 31, 2002) and post-2003 (the period starting Jan. 1, 2004). Table 4 
confirms that the COSP is significant in most sectors during both the pre-2003 and the post2003 periods. For example, the COSP in Mining sector has increased slightly from 0.31% to 
1.02% during the pre- and post-2003 periods, respectively. Note that the average result of pairs 
trading with fundamental similarity (P1) in Mining sector has declined from 1.30% per month 
during the pre-2003 to -0.17% during the post-2003.  
According to Green et al. (2016), the changes in the trading environment and the 
information during the period from July 2002 to June 2003 made it cheaper and technologically 
more feasible to rapidly implement arbitrage trading, which caused the increase in the 
efficiency of the stock market. For these reasons, the average returns of pairs trading also 
seemingly have disappeared since 2003, which indicates that active arbitrage trading prevents 
the temporal price deviation between stocks with similar fundamentals.  
However, the non-convergence risk from trading spurious pairs are still significant. For 
example, the COSP of the mining sector come from negative profit of pairs trading with 
dissimilar fundamentals (P5) during post-2003 periods, which is statistically significant. 
Arbitrageurs would suffer losses if they overlook fundamental similarity between stocks and 
suffer the multiple hypothesis testing problem.

4.5. Robustness of the Results 
For the robustness of our results, we compare our main results with those under other 
settings. First, when small and illiquid stocks are included, the performance of pairs trading 
increases. Table 5 show that the performance of pairs trading improves in all sectors. In 
particular, Figure 1 shows that pairs with high fundamental closeness appears profitable in the 
2010s (P1 including micro stocks). However, in reality, price anomaly in small and illiquid 
stocks are not easily exploitable due to their illiquidity and trading costs. Moreover, pairs 
trading during the post-2003 is not as profitable as it was during the pre-2003.  
Second, we compare the performance of the pairs we identify using the elastic net 
regression with that using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.7 Table 6 demonstrates that 
pairs trading calculated with the elastic net regression outperform those determined by OLS in 
all industries. This indicates that the elastic net selects firm characteristics more efficiently, the 
results of which are consistent with the previous studies in machine learning (Gu et al, 2020). 
Third, we investigate whether the returns of pairs trading are explained by Fama and 
French’s (2015) five factors, momentum factor, and liquidity factor. Table 7 presents the 
regression results of five pairs trading portfolios formed on FCS-based quintile portfolios when 
100 pairs are selected regardless of sectors. The results indicate that a significant risk-adjusted 
return exists even after controlling the seven factors. Further, our results confirm that 
momentum has a negative effect on all quintile portfolios, which is largely consistent with the 
previous literature, such as Gatev et al. (2006), Chen et al. (2017), and Rad et al. (2016). This 
indicates that some part of the profitability of pairs trading is derived from the reversal effect. 
More importantly, the results confirm that portfolios with similar firm characteristics earn 
higher risk-adjusted returns: i.e., the alpha of P1 is 0.48% per month and is significant. 
Finally, we have tested if our results are robust to the number of pairs in the portfolios. 
Instead of 100 pairs, we have tried 50 and also 500 pairs, but the results change little.8

In this study, we explore whether firm characteristics known to explain stock returns can 
be used to minimize spurious pairs caused by the multiple hypothesis testing problem in pairs 
trading. Our empirical results show that pairs that are fundamentally close to each other
