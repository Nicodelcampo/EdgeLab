Quantitative Trading and Stock Price Crash Risk: Evidence from China 
 
Abstract: We posit and demonstrate that, in China’s retail-dominated market, quantitative 
trading over-relies on non-fundamental signals, thereby crowding out fundamental information 
from stock prices and increasing crash risk. Using trading data from quantitative mutual funds 
and Chinese A-share firms during 2009-2023, we find that greater exposure to quantitative 
trading is associated with higher future crash risk. Mediation analysis further reveals that 
reduced information efficiency constitutes a key channel through which quantitative trading 
elevates crash risk. The effect is stronger for stocks with more retail investors, consistent with 
our proposed mechanism. Overall, we identify a novel potential risk of quantitative trading in 
underdeveloped emerging markets. 
Keywords: Quantitative trading; Stock price crash risk; Mutual funds; China’s capital market 
 
1. Introduction 
Advances in computer and information technologies have enhanced data- and algorithmdriven decision-making, improving forecasts and reducing human biases (Abis, 2022). 
Similarly, financial investment is increasingly digital and model-driven. Quantitative trading, 
which applies systematic, rule-based models to process market data, exemplifies this trend 
(Beggs and Hill-Kleespie, 2025). While such strategies can improve price discovery and 
liquidity, they also carry risks due to algorithmic similarity, opacity, and reliance on historical 
data, which may amplify market inefficiencies in emerging markets like China.  
We investigate how quantitative trading impacts stock price crash risk, defined as extreme 
negative drops in individual stock prices. Prior studies link stock price crashes to fundamental 
information1 that accumulates over time but is not promptly reflected in prices (Habib et al., 
2018). From a market microstructure perspective, price-embedded information depends on the 
information set investors use in trading (O’Hara, 1998). Quantitative trading employs 
technologies such as big data analytics and AI, improving fundamental information processing 
(Goldstein, 2023). Yet, the information ultimately incorporated into trading decisions depends 
on the signals that models identify as most prominent, typically extracted from historical data 
through back-testing, validation, and factor construction (Dyer et al., 2025; Beggs and HillKleespie, 2025). Thus, whether quantitative trading facilitates the incorporation of fundamental 
information into stock prices, potentially mitigating crash risk, depends on the relative

In China’s capital market, retail investors account for over 85% of trading and, due to their 
limited ability to process firm-specific information, focus on sentiment, trading frictions, and 
other non-fundamental signals, resulting in a relatively low incorporation of fundamental 
information into prices (Jones et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2023). This stands in contrast to U.S. retail 
investors, who possess firm-specific information (Boehmer et al., 2021).  
Accordingly, in a market with weak fundamental information, quantitative trading may 
rely more on non-fundamental patterns. Consistently, empirical studies driven by machine 
learning demonstrate that non-fundamental information is the most critical factor for 
quantitative investment in the Chinese capital market (Leippold et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019). 
Furthermore, the homogeneity of quantitative algorithms induces herding and myopic behavior 
(Beggs and Hill-Kleespie, 2025; Malikov and Pasquariello, 2021), which lead to market 
overreactions and may further reduce the role of fundamental information in prices. Indeed, Lu 
et al. (2025) provide empirical evidence that quantitative trading weakens the relationship 
between stock prices and fundamental information. Overall, given that a lower incorporation 
of fundamental information into stock prices elevates the risk of future crash (Hutton et al., 
2009; Jin et al., 2006), quantitative trading may further amplify stock price crash risk by 
impeding the assimilation of fundamental signals. 
Using mutual fund trading data, we construct a measure of stocks’ exposure to quantitative 
trading to examine our research question. Empirical analysis of Chinese A-shares from 2009 to 
2023 shows that quantitative trading increases future stock crash risk. Mediation analysis 
further reveals that reduced information efficiency constitutes a key channel through which 
quantitative trading elevates crash risk. Decomposing trades into buys and sells, the effect is 
stronger for sell trades, which may be related to the lack of a short-selling mechanism in China’s 
capital market. Supporting our hypothesis, the effect is more pronounced for stocks with an 
investor structure characterized by a higher proportion of retail investors. Our results remain 
robust to endogeneity checks, balanced sample tests or alternative measures (see Appendix F 
G and H for supplementary tests).  
Overall, we identify a novel potential risk of quantitative trading in underdeveloped 
emerging markets, providing new insights for both academia and regulators. Simultaneously, 
our findings engage with existing literature, complementing and distinguishing our work from 
previous research. First, similar to Xu et al. (2025) and Zou et al. (2025) we complement firminternal studies on crash risk (Li et al., 2025; Liu, 2023; Francis et al., 2016) by identifying a 
new external factor that affect stock price crash risk. Second, although Mestel et al. (2024) find 
no effect of algorithmic trading on mini flash crashes in a relatively developed market, we 
document the opposite in an underdeveloped emerging market, highlighting that the impact of

2. Data and variable definitions 
To empirically examine the relationship between quantitative trading and stock price crash 
risk, we compile a comprehensive dataset on the trading behavior of quantitative mutual funds 
in China’s A-share market over the period 2009-2023. Data on fund holdings and transactions 
are sourced from the China Securities Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database, 
which also provides firm-level financial and stock market information. 
2.1 Key independent variable: Exposure to quantitative trading 
Due to data availability, we focus on quantitative mutual funds as representative 
quantitative investors 2 . According to CSRC (China Securities Regulatory Commission) 
regulations, mutual funds must disclose their annual trading details for any stock whose 
cumulative buy or sell value exceeds 2% of net asset value or ranks among the fund’s top 20 
holdings. 
Following Lin et al. (2023), we first select actively managed mutual funds. Among these 
funds, those whose names contain “quantitative” or whose investment strategies include 
“quantitative-investment keywords” are classified as quantitative-investment funds3. Finally, 
we manually review the investment strategies of the funds, exclude irrelevant ones, and obtain 
a sample of 381 quantitative mutual funds.  
We focus on the trading behavior of quantitative mutual funds with respect to individual 
stocks, rather than merely their holdings. At the stock-year level, we aggregate the total trading 
volume of each stock by quantitative mutual funds and scale it by the stock’s total annual 
trading volume to obtain our main explanatory variable—Quantitative Trading Ratio (𝑄𝑇𝑅):

2.2 Dependent variable: Stock price crash risk 
Following Xu et al. (2025), Zou et al. (2025) and Chen et al. (2001), we employ negative 
conditional skewness (𝑁𝑐𝑠𝑘𝑒𝑤) and down-to-up volatility (𝐷𝑢𝑣𝑜𝑙) as two measures of stock 
price crash risk. Details on the construction of the indicator can be found in Appendix B.  
2.3 Control variables 
To mitigate confounding effects, following Li et al. (2025), Liu et al. (2023) and Xu et al. 
(2021), we control for a range of firm characteristics commonly associated with crash risk, 
including firm size (Size), age (Age), leverage (Lev), profitability (Roa), book-to-market ratio 
(BM), annual return (Ret), return volatility (Sigma), abnormal turnover (Dturn), revenue growth 
(Growth), and cash holdings (Cash). All variables are defined in Appendix C.

of 0.732 and 0.476, respectively. The average value of 𝑄𝑇𝑅 is 0.115%, with a maximum of 
5.285%, indicating significant concentration in quantitative trading across individual stocks.

underlying its potential adverse effect. According to our hypothesis, this potential adverse effect 
stems from China’s retail-dominated investor structure, which weakens the role of fundamental 
information. Therefore, the adverse effects of quantitative trading on individual stocks should 
intensify as the proportion of retail investors increases. We divide the sample into high and low 
groups based on a proxy for the retail investor ratio, then repeat the baseline regressions within 
each subsample and compare the estimated coefficients.

4. Conclusion 
Based on China’s unique investor structure, we posit and show that quantitative trading 
relies more on non-fundamental information, crowding out fundamental information in stock 
prices and increasing crash risk. Mediation analysis further indicates that reduced information 
efficiency serves as a key channel through which quantitative trading elevates crash risk. The 
effect is stronger for stocks whose investor structure comprises a higher proportion of retail 
investors, supporting our reasoning. Our findings highlight the potential risks of algorithm- and 
data-driven investing in underdeveloped emerging markets with structural imbalances.
