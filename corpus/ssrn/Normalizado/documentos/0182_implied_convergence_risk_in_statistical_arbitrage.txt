Abstract
Arbitrage in ﬁnancial markets is not without risk. Many statistical arbitrage
strategies depend on pricing error measures whose conditional volatility exposes capitalconstrained arbitrageurs to signiﬁcant convergence risk. We introduce a Modiﬁed
Generalized Smooth-Transition (MGST) function, whose parameters resemble those
of a GARCH-in-Mean process, to estimate pair-speciﬁc convergence risk. Unlike
traditional approaches, the MGST strategy adapts dynamically to varying market
conditions. In empirical tests against three benchmark arbitrage portfolios, the MGST
strategy trades more aggressively yet delivers higher cumulative returns and Sharpe
Ratios, even after accounting for transaction costs. Its superior performance is driven
by substantial cross-sectional variation in the endogenously determined no-arbitrage
band level and bandwidth. In contrast, benchmark strategies impose a zero band level
and use a ﬁxed, ad hoc bandwidth across all pairs and time periods—an approach
that fails to reﬂect the heterogeneity in convergence risk across trading pairs.
Keywords: No-arbitrage band; Limits to arbitrage; Pricing error.

Introduction
The no-arbitrage condition is a fundamental concept in ﬁnancial market research (Almeida
et al., 2023). The limits-to-arbitrage literature highlights widespread empirical deviations
from no-arbitrage pricing, shedding light on how constraints drive persistent cross-sectional
and time-series variation in pricing errors. In our paper, the pricing error ωi,t refers to the
misalignment in normalized prices between paired securities i at time t. Such pairings may
arise from parity-pricing conditions with clear economic foundations (e.g., cross-listed stocks
and the law of one price), or from cointegrated-pricing relations lacking an obvious or stable
economic link (e.g., pairs-trading and capital structure arbitrage).
Arbitrage constraints and trading frictions inﬂuence arbitrage via two channels. First,
they impose (explicit or implicit) costs that set upper and/or lower bounds for arbitrage.
Together, these determine the skewness and magnitude of the no-arbitrage band. Second,
constraints such as short-sale restrictions or funding illiquidity can limit an arbitrageur’s
ability to act on non-trivial pricing errors, particularly when convergence risk is present,
as this risk may di!er between upper and lower bounds. Our parsimonious outline of the
arbitrage mechanism applies to both parity-pricing and cointegrated-pricing (or statistical)
arbitrage. While many constraints a!ecting ωi,t are well studied, the arbitrage mechanism
itself remains unobservable to econometricians.
Chen et al. (2021) introduce a method to infer the implied arbitrage mechanism by modeling
the dynamics of ωi,t within its no-arbitrage band. When ωi,t moves outside this band,
it becomes non-trivially proﬁtable, attracting arbitrage ﬂows that push it back toward
equilibrium. They embed a generalized smooth-transition (GST) function in a Vector ErrorCorrection Model (VECM), allowing the error-correction process to transition between
arbitrage and no-arbitrage states. The GST also quantiﬁes arbitrage intensity as the
incremental correction during arbitrage states.
2

We address a key limitation in Chen et al. (2021): while their model includes a convergence
risk term, it is omitted from the empirical analysis due to estimation complexity. Their
model primarily applies to parity-pricing arbitrage, where ωi,t decays over time and εωi,t
follows a time-dependent volatility pattern, as in the case of futures arbitrage with a known
expiry. However, as Shleifer and Vishny (1997) explain, even parity-pricing arbitrage is
not riskless. Interim losses may trigger margin calls and force early liquidation. Since
arbitrageurs are often capital-constrained and use leverage, they are especially sensitive to
convergence risk, which should therefore be incorporated into statistical arbitrage strategies.
To incorporate convergence risk, we propose a modiﬁed GST (MGST) function. We allow
ωi,t to follow a conditional volatility process and specify no-arbitrage bandwidths using a
GARCH-in-Mean structure. This lets the band for cointegrated pricing errors be at least
as wide as that for parity-pricing errors. A wider band implies a larger expected proﬁt
per round-trip, compensating arbitrageurs for convergence risk. The MGST framework
enables pair-level calibration of trading parameters and captures cross-sectional variation in
convergence risk, which we empirically identify as a key driver of proﬁtability.
Compared to Chen et al. (2021), our MGST-VECM spans a broader strategy space and has
greater practical relevance. Parity-pricing opportunities are limited and heavily targeted
by high-frequency traders. In contrast, statistical arbitrage o!ers more potential proﬁt,
albeit with higher convergence risk. We also replace their GARCH process with a Doubleendogenous Threshold GARCH (DeTGARCH) speciﬁcation. Like EGARCH (Nelson, 1991)
and GJR-GARCH (Glosten et al., 1993), DeTGARCH allows asymmetric volatility, but
without assuming that negative pricing errors are inherently more volatile, since ωi,t is a
di!erence in normalized prices and directionality is arbitrary.
DeTGARCH o!ers three advantages over standard GARCH. First, separate thresholds for
positive and negative ωi,t allow for arbitrage asymmetry, resulting in a skewed no-arbitrage
3

band. Second, convergence risk may di!er by direction because certain constraints can
a!ect one security more than the other. Third, by estimating thresholds endogenously, we
obtain pair-speciﬁc no-arbitrage bands that support tailored trading decisions. The MGST
framework therefore captures variation in arbitrage bands across pairs i = 1, 2, . . . , N.
This conceptual advance requires a more complex estimation procedure. The MGST-VECM
embeds a DeTGARCH process within a GST function linked to the VECM error-correction
term. While a standard approach would sequentially estimate each component, simultaneity

Our empirical application involves two arbitrage strategies: one based on cross-listed A-H
stocks in mainland China and Hong Kong, and the other on statistically matched pairs
from the CSI300 index. Using the estimated MGST bands, we construct separate A-H and
pairwise arbitrage portfolios. We ﬁnd that the MGST strategy consistently outperforms
three benchmarks using ﬁxed no-arbitrage bands based on 90%, 95%, and 99% conﬁdence
intervals as in Gatev et al. (2006), with transaction costs included following Leippold et al.
(2022). The performance edge is especially strong for the pairwise sample, where MGST
imposes narrower bands and trades more frequently, yielding the highest net returns. Its

We attribute the MGST portfolio’s superior performance to its ability to capture crosssectional variation in no-arbitrage bands NaBi,M. Benchmark strategies ﬁx the band level
at zero and apply a constant conﬁdence interval ϑ across all pairs, making volatility εωi the
only source of cross-sectional variation. However, when we infer the implied ϑi from NaBi,M,
substantial residual variation remains, indicating that εωi alone does not fully explain
NaBi,M. Furthermore, although the average band level is close to zero, noticeable variation
across pairs suggests the presence of arbitrage asymmetry, which is ignored by benchmark
approaches. Ultimately, the MGST strategy’s ability to tailor arbitrage mechanisms to
individual pairs is what drives its superior returns.

The MGST-VECM
2.1
Related Literature
To provide readers a better appreciation of the merits of the MGST-VECM, we review the
existing literature on statistical arbitrage. The literature on statistical arbitrage consists
of two main strands. The ﬁrst investigates sources of proﬁtability and associated risks.
Gatev et al. (2006) examine pairs trading, which exploits relative mispricing between stocks,
and ﬁnd signiﬁcant excess returns net of transaction costs over Fama-French factors. They
attribute these proﬁts to compensation for a latent risk factor related to enforcing the Law
of One Price. Jacobs and Weber (2015) conduct a cross-country study across 34 stock
markets and document persistent abnormal returns. Chen et al. (2019) also report abnormal
proﬁts and identify two sources: short-term reversal in one or both stocks, and short-term
industry momentum, where a leader stock triggers entry and a follower stock prompts exit.
The second strand focuses on how calibration methods a!ect performance. Krauss (2017)
5

review over 100 papers and classify calibration techniques into ﬁve categories: distance,
cointegration, time-series, stochastic control, and others. The ﬁrst three relate to pair
selection, using non-parametric distance metrics, cointegration tests for stationarity, or
time-series models assuming mean reversion. Stochastic control methods optimize portfolio
weights within each pair, while the ﬁnal category includes less common techniques. The
study also considers how trading frictions impact proﬁtability. Rad et al. (2016) compare
distance, cointegration, and copula-based methods for identifying pairs. All yield positive
alphas, with cointegration performing best during volatile periods. Yan et al. (2022) explore
delayed cointegration under path-dependent models. More recent work adopts qualitative
similarity metrics, such as keyword-based stock matching in ﬁrm news to identify return
predictability (Box, 2018), stochastic control for cointegrated portfolios (Tourin and Yan,
2013), convergence trading under recurring arbitrage (Liu and Timmermann, 2013), and
models with transaction costs and stop-loss constraints (Lei and Xu, 2015).
Our MGST-VECM contributes to both strands of the statistical arbitrage literature. For
studies on the risks and returns of pairs trading, we emphasize the role of pair-speciﬁc
convergence risk and how cross-sectional variation in calibrated parameters drives our
portfolio’s outperformance over benchmarks. For the technical calibration strand, MGST
estimates enable us to calibrate both the band-level and bandwidth of no-arbitrage zones
at the pair level. While many studies explore calibration methods, they typically do so at
the strategy level. In contrast, our pricing error thresholds are endogenously determined,
allowing pairwise calibration of the MGST arbitrage strategy.

Model Estimation and Trading Application
3.1
A-H Trading and Pairs Trading
We evaluate the MGST-VECM through two arbitrage strategies with di!ering levels of
convergence risk: A-H arbitrage and statistical pairs trading.
First, the A-H sample consists of 20 ﬁrms cross-listed on the Shanghai/Shenzhen Stock
Exchange and Hong Kong Exchange, from 2021 to 2022. A-shares are traded in RMB and
H-shares in HKD. While the two share classes have identical dividend and voting rights,
pricing discrepancies persist due to market segmentation, trading frictions, and regulatory
constraints. Although not a strict parity condition, A-H pricing is grounded in economic
fundamentals and exhibits strong co-movement, suggesting limited convergence risk.
We obtain one-minute trading data from Wind Database and convert H-share prices
to RMB using the contemporaneous RMB/HKD exchange rate.
To account for level
di!erences between A and H prices, we normalize the price series before measuring mispricing.
Speciﬁcally, we estimate the regression At = ↼0 + ↼1Ht + ωi,t using in-sample data and
deﬁne the normalized pricing error ωi,t as the residual. We then normalize the price series
by setting At = 1 and applying the estimated coe"cients to compute the implied value of
Ht. This ensures the trading signals are based on relative mispricing, not raw price gaps.
Cointegration tests are conducted for each ﬁrm to verify the long-run equilibrium condition.
Firms that do not pass the test are excluded from out-of-sample trading. The ﬁnal A-H
sample covers a range of industries and market capitalizations to ensure representativeness.
Second, the pairs trading sample includes 20 unique stock pairs selected monthly from
15

CSI300 constituents based on a distance measure following Gatev et al. (2006). The measure
is calculated as the cumulative absolute di!erence in normalized prices over the prior year.
Pairs are re-ranked monthly using a rolling window from January to December 2021, and the
top-ranked non-overlapping pairs that pass the cointegration test are retained for trading
in the subsequent month. This monthly rebalancing avoids look-ahead bias and captures
evolving statistical relationships.
For each selected pair, we estimate the MGST-VECM using in-sample data, then apply the
estimated parameters to generate trading signals out-of-sample. The strategy opens a trade
when the normalized pricing error ωi,t breaches the estimated no-arbitrage band and closes
it when ωi,t reverts to zero. Trades are liquidated at month-end if convergence does not
occur, in line with a max holding period constraint. This setup allows for cross-sectional
variation in trading thresholds, as the MGST model endogenously determines pair-speciﬁc
no-arbitrage band levels and widths. We follow the same procedure for A-H trading, though
rebalancing is not required due to the stable nature of cross-listed pairs.
This empirical design enables a direct comparison between low-risk arbitrage (A-H) and
high-risk arbitrage (pairs), highlighting the MGST-VECM’s ability to adapt to convergence
risk. The model’s dynamic calibration of entry and exit thresholds accommodates both timevarying volatility and asymmetry in arbitrage mechanisms, which are especially important
in the pairs-trading context. Details on A-H and pairs trading, including data sources,
sample selection, normalization procedures and results, and cointegration test results, are
provided in the Supplementary Material.

3.3.2
Implementing the Arbitrage Strategies
We form the arbitrage portfolio as follows: each pair i is treated as a tradable asset with
RMB 100,000 in committed capital, totaling RMB 2 million for 2022. Each arbitrage
position is a self-ﬁnancing long-short trade. The committed capital serves as a reserve in the
22

trading account to cover margin calls and track portfolio returns. Proﬁt and loss are marked
to market, and the trading account balance is used to compute daily portfolio returns.
Figure 2a and Figure 2b plot cumulative daily returns for the MGST strategy and three
benchmark portfolios for the A-H and pairwise samples, respectively. The benchmarks
represent increasing no-arbitrage bandwidths: ϑ99 is nested within ϑ95, which is nested
within ϑ90. As expected, the benchmarks show strong co-movement, particularly between
ϑ90 and ϑ95, due to their closer critical values. The jump from a 90% conﬁdence interval to
95% increases the threshold from 1.65 to 1.96, while extending from 95% to 99% requires
a much larger jump to 2.57, as the probability density is thinner in the tails and higher
thresholds are needed to cover smaller probability masses.
In the A-H sample, Figure 2a shows that although the MGST strategy achieves the highest
cumulative return over the year, it underperforms ϑ90 and ϑ95 in the ﬁrst half.
This
underperformance is due to a sharp tech-sector sell-o! in Hong Kong in March 2022, which
also a!ected H-shares. The Hang Seng Index hit a six-year low that month, and the
Hang Seng Tech Index posted a 12% weekly loss. The downturn was triggered by the
Russia-Ukraine conﬂict and increasing U.S. regulatory scrutiny of Chinese ﬁrms listed in
the U.S.
Because MGST uses narrower, ﬁrm-speciﬁc no-arbitrage bands, it opens positions earlier
than the benchmarks. As the market continued to decline, MGST entered too soon and
incurred losses, while the benchmark strategies reacted more slowly. The ϑ99 strategy, with
the widest band, opened positions only in late March after most of the market decline
had already occurred. As a result, it too started with a loss. Around two weeks later, the
Hong Kong market began to recover, and all four strategies showed a reversal in cumulative
return. However, the MGST portfolio’s early entry depressed its overall return level and
increased volatility, especially in Q1. This helps explain why its Sharpe ratio is slightly
23

lower than that of ϑ90, despite delivering the highest average return for the year.
In the pairwise sample, Figure 2b displays more stable co-movement across strategies, as no
major crash occurred during the trading period. The MGST strategy’s performance edge
becomes more evident over time. Unlike the ﬁxed A-H portfolio, the pairwise sample is
updated monthly: ﬁrm pairs are re-identiﬁed, cointegration is re-tested, MGST parameters
are re-estimated, and all positions are closed at month-end. This explains the recurring ﬂat
segments in cumulative returns, that is, some months yield no trading signals. The e!ect is
more pronounced for the ϑ99 benchmark, which has the widest band and thus trades less
frequently.

4
Conclusion
We modify the GST-VECM in Chen et al. (2021) to incorporate convergence risk in statistical
arbitrage. Unlike parity pricing, the cointegrating relationship between statistically paired
securities is unstable over time and varies across pairs. This makes portfolio calibration,
such as setting no-arbitrage bandwidths and entry or exit signals at the strategy level,
suboptimal. The novelty of the MGST-VECM lies in its ability to embed convergence risk
into pair-speciﬁc no-arbitrage bands. To estimate convergence risk, we specify the transition
function to follow a GARCH-in-Mean structure, with the pricing error’s conditional volatility
generated from a DeTGARCH speciﬁcation. The thresholds correspond to the values of the
pricing error at which the transition function begins to increase. Speciﬁcally, the thresholds
ω+
i and ω→
i represent the implied upper and lower bounds of the no-arbitrage band, and
are endogenously estimated. As a result, they deﬁne pair-speciﬁc bands that vary across
securities.
We apply the MGST-VECM to two samples: cross-listed A-H Chinese ﬁrms and statistically
paired CSI300 constituents. Using the estimated MGST no-arbitrage bands, we construct
separate arbitrage portfolios for each sample. Our main ﬁnding is that the MGST-calibrated
portfolios outperform three benchmark portfolios based on ﬁxed no-arbitrage bandwidths
32

ϑ90, ϑ95, ϑ99, particularly for the pairwise sample. On average, the MGST strategy adopts
narrower bands and trades more frequently than the benchmarks. Despite this, it consistently
delivers higher cumulative returns, net of transaction costs, for both samples. The Sharpe
ratio is also higher than all benchmarks, except for the A-H portfolio under ϑ90.
The MGST strategy’s outperformance is driven by cross-sectional variation in the noarbitrage bandwidth NaBi,M. In the benchmark strategies, the band level is ﬁxed at zero
and the bandwidth is constant across pairs and over time. Consequently, variation in NaBi,G
arises solely from the unconditional in-sample volatility εωi. In contrast, NaBi,M shows
substantial variation in both the bandwidth and the band level across pairs. In summary, the
ability of the MGST strategy to incorporate implied arbitrage mechanisms into pair-speciﬁc
no-arbitrage bands explains its superior performance relative to the benchmarks.
Although we use pairs trading to illustrate the MGST-VECM’s capacity to account for
convergence risk, the model can be applied to a wider range of statistical arbitrage strategies
involving large cross-sections of paired securities, such as capital structure arbitrage. The
only requirement is that the prices are cointegrated and the normalized pricing error is
stationary. This highlights the practical relevance of the MGST-VECM across a broad
arbitrage strategy space.

The A-H arbitrage strategy serves as a contrast for pairs-trading, a well-established
statistical arbitrage strategy based on cointegrated pricing. As there is no clear economic link between pairwise stocks, pairs traders are exposed to substantially greater
convergence risk, compare to A-H pricing arbitragers. Analyzing both arbitrage strategies, allow us to draw out the ability of the MGST-VECM to address convergence risk in
statistical arbitrage. The estimated GST function in Chen et al. (2021) does not consider
convergence risk; It cannot be applied on pairs-trading, and to a lesser extend, even A-H
trading.
B.1
A-H trading
According to Li et al. (2014), overlapping trading hours and the close proximity between
China and Hong Kong, foster A-H arbitrage trading. Seasholes and Liu (2011) outline
various arbitrage-type strategies based on the A-H premium, which is tracked in realtime by the trading systems of major brokerage houses. Following Liu and Timmermann
(2013), we examine A-H mispricing on Chinese stocks cross-traded on either the Shanghai
Stock Exchange (SSE) or Shenzhen Stock Exchange (SZE) as A-shares in RMB, and on
the Hong Kong Exchange (HKX) as H-shares in HKD. Given both share types entitle
investors to the same dividend stream and voting rights, we ﬁnd strong co-movement in
A-H pricing over time, as do Liu and Timmermann (2013) and Lei and Xu (2015).
The three exchanges operate in the same time-zone, with overlapping trading hours.
Both the SSE and SZE trade 4 hours each day, from 09:30 to 11:30, and from 13:00 to
15:00. The HKX operates for 5.5 hours from 9:30 to 12:00, and from 13:00 to 16:00. Our
A-H sample is based on trading hours of the Mainland exchanges. We analyze minuteby-minute data from January 2021 to December 2022, downloaded from Wind Financial,
the largest ﬁnancial data vendor in China and whose data is widely used by published
5

academic papers3. To ensure the trading results are compatible, we use the real-time
RMB/HKD exchange rate to convert the H-share prices from HKD to RMB, denote as
Ht. Using (At, Ht), we compute return and normalized prices for the in-sample analysis.
If the purpose is to demonstrate the model, applying the estimation on a single A-H
ﬁrm would su"ce. But an important merit of the MGST function is that it allow for
cross-sectional variation in the implied no-arbitrage bandwidth, especially in the pairstrading sample. If the arbitrage mechanism varies across ﬁrms, this suggests that an
arbitrage strategy’s calibration e.g. Opening and closing signals, Stop-loss level, Max
holding period etc, should be calibrated at the pairwise ﬁrm level, rather than a one-sizeﬁts-all calibration at the strategy level.
Lei and Xu (2015) examine all A-H ﬁrms, which cover transportation, industrial,
utilities, and ﬁnancial sectors. In contrast, Liu and Timmermann (2013) illustrate their
theoretical results using seven pairs of A-H banking stocks, with sample periods ranging
from 1.5 to 4 years. Based on the above, construct our A-H sample to cover 20 ﬁrms over
two years from January 2021 to December 2022. By requiring 2 years of data, we avoid
A-H ﬁrms with short trading history; For these ﬁrms, the pricing mechanism is likely be
noisy, and there is insu"cient data for arbitragers to analyse and calibrate their trading
algorithms.
Following Wang et al. (2022) that select several stocks into a portfolio according to

into the A-H sample. In the appendix, we report the selected ﬁrms, market capitalization

B.2
Pairs trading
Pairs trading is well-established on Wall Street and is a proprietary strategy among hedge
funds and investment banks. The concept is straightforward; Identify pairwise stocks
whose historical prices move closely together over time, based on a statistical measure
of closeness e.g. correlation, cointegration, Euclidean distance. When the normalized
pricing error ωi,t exceeds a pre-speciﬁed threshold, short-sell the higher priced stock to
buy the lower priced stock. The strategy assumes that the chosen statistical relation
between identiﬁed pairwise stocks, is stable over time. Hence any observed ωi,t is deemed
transitory, and represents an arbitrage opportunity.
Unlike A-H pricing, which has a strong economic underlay, the fundamental link behind a statistical closeness between identiﬁed pairwise stocks, is unclear. It is likely for
paired stocks to come from the same or related industries, but this is not a necessary
requirement. Gatev et al. (2006) use a Euclidean distance measure to identify pairwise
stocks. While they also consider restricted ﬁrm pairing within four broad S&P sectors:
Utilities, Transportation, Financial, and Industrial, their baseline strategy applies unrestricted pairing. The statistical link between pairwise stocks is likely to be unstable,
which exposes pairs traders to greater convergence risk over time, as well as greater variation across pairwise ﬁrms. The MGST-VECM quantiﬁes the convergence risk premium
in pairwise assets, for the purpose of calibrating the arbitrage strategy, whether they are
A-H or pairwise ﬁrms. We evaluate if the MGST-VECM detects and incorporates greater
convergence risk in the pairwise sample, compare to the A-H sample.
We implement a pairs strategy that is similar to Gatev et al. (2006); To avoid illiq9

uidity and trading friction, we limit the identiﬁcation to pairwise combinations among
constituent stocks of the China Stock Index 300 (CSI300)4. We download one-minute
trading data for all CSI300 constituent stocks from Jan 2021 to Dec 2022 from WIND
Financial. Following Gatev et al. (2006), we calculate normalized price as the cumulative
total return with reinvested dividends, and measure closeness as the cumulative absolute
di!erence in normalized prices, for all possible pairwise combination of CSI300 stocks.
The Gatev et al. (2006) measure is similar to a Euclidean distance; A small value indicates a high degree of closeness between two normalized price series during the in-sample
period.
Using the Jan-Dec 2021 in-sample period, we sort all pairwise ﬁrm combination on
the distance measure, and shortlist the top ranked 20 pairs as the pairwise ﬁrm sample.
To note, the sample consists of non-overlapping pairs; If a stock is used in a higher ranked
pair, and if it reappears in a lower ranked pair, we will skip to the next pair. Doing so
ensures the 20 pairwise ﬁrms are unique, and the portfolio results are not driven by a
small number of ﬁrms. Furthermore, unlike A-H pricing, the cointegrating link between
pairwise ﬁrms is likely to be unstable during the trading period. To address this issue, we
construct a monthly rebalanced portfolio. Consider the ﬁrst trading round at the start of
January 2022. Using the 2021 in-sample, we identify 20 pairwise ﬁrms. For pairwise ﬁrms
that test the cointegration test, we estimate their MGST-VECM parameters to implement
pairs-trading for January 2022. At the end of January, we close all open positions, roll the
estimation window to the end of Jan 2022, and repeat the entire procedure i.e. identify
top 20 pairwise ﬁrms, test for cointegration, estimate MGST parameters for all passed
pairs, and conduct pairs-trading for February 2022.
