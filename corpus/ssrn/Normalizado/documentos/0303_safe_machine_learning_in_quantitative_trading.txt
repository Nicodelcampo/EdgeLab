Abstract
This paper explores the integration of Machine Learning (ML) techniques within the
domain of quantitative trading, with a particular emphasis on the development of
algorithmic trading strategies in financial markets. The research examines a diverse array
of models, encompassing both traditional methodologies such as Logistic Regression
and Support Vector Machines, as well as more advanced deep learning architectures,
including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
networks, and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU). Furthermore, this paper introduces the
novelty of the SAFE framework (Sustainability, Accuracy, Fairness, and Explainability)
as a means of assessing and enhancing the trustworthiness of deep AI models.
The
objective of this investigation is to assess the effectiveness of ML models in improving
trading strategies, while also evaluating the robustness of SAFE as a metric for ensuring
model transparency and reliability within the context of dynamic financial markets.
The models are evaluated using approximately 20 years of stock price data from
International Business Machines Corporation (IBM), covering the period from January
2000 to March 2021. This time frame was divided into two distinct segments: a training
phase, from January 2000 to February 2019, used to construct and calibrate the models,
and a validation phase, from February 2019 to March 2021, utilized to assess the
predictive performance of the generated trading signals .
This research addresses two primary Machine Learning tasks:
regression and
classification.
Logistic Regression and Support Vector Machines are introduced as
classification models, wherein model outputs serve as trading signals for IBM stock. In
contrast, deep learning models are employed for regression tasks, with outputs predicting
subsequent stock prices, which are then transformed into trading signals through
additional processing.
Moreover, the effectiveness of each model-based strategy is
rigorously evaluated through backtesting, which generates visual representations of

Introduction
This research primarily seeks to evaluate the integration of advanced machine learning
techniques, particularly neural networks, with core mathematical and statistical methods
to develop advanced trading strategies in financial markets.
Leveraging the rapid
advancements in computational power and the availability of large datasets, neural
networks are positioned as potent tools for uncovering complex patterns in market data
that traditional statistical approaches may fail to detect. The study aims to utilize these
capabilities to improve the accuracy of market predictions and trading decisions.
Specifically, the objectives include the development of a framework that combines neural
networks with statistical methodologies, such as time series analysis and regression
models, to forecast asset prices and identify profitable trading opportunities.
Furthermore, this research will investigate the adaptability of these models to varying
market conditions, such as different volatility regimes, and assess their capacity to

Literature review
In the subsequent section, we examine the foundational principles of Machine Learning
and its rigorous mathematical formulation in the context of financial applications. First,
we explore studies that transform time-series data into a supervised learning framework.
This step is crucial, as time-series data, in its raw form, does not conform to the structure
required for supervised learning, despite the prevalence of such data in finance. As a
result, without appropriate transformation, the application of supervised learning
algorithms to financial data may encounter significant challenges.
Second, we will
examine the relationship between the previously transformed supervised learning
framework and cross-validation, which stands as the most widely adopted method for
model selection.
Additionally, we will delineate the fundamental differences in the
application of cross-validation techniques to chronological versus non-chronological
data. The subsequent section will focus on the rigorous treatment of a stable Machine
Learning model, incorporating specific conditions related to the loss function.
The

the loss function can contribute to greater model stability in practical settings. The final
segment of the review is dedicated to the theoretical underpinnings of machine learning
models, encompassing both classical approaches and more advanced Deep Learning

Deep Learning
Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning leveraging a diverse array of neural
network architectures in conjunction with advanced optimization techniques. It has been
successfully applied across various domains,
from social sciences to finance,
demonstrating its broad utility and effectiveness. Deep learning has gained significant
prominence in the analysis of financial data due to its capacity to model complex,
nonlinear relationships within large datasets. Financial data, often characterized by a low
signal-to-noise ratio, presents challenges that traditional statistical methods may not
adequately address. Deep learning models, particularly those based on neural network
architectures such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory
(LSTM) networks, are particularly well-suited for time-series forecasting. Furthermore,
deep learning benefits from the integration of vast and diverse data sources, including
historical prices, trading volumes, and even alternative data.
Nevertheless, the
application of deep learning in finance necessitates careful attention to issues of model
interpretability and the risk of overfitting, given the potential consequences for trading
strategies and investment decisions, as highlighted by Fischer and Krauss (2018).
Deep learning has fundamentally transformed numerous fields, including Computer
Vision, Medicine, Recommendation Systems, and Quantitative Finance. Its applications
span a wide range of tasks, from human speech recognition and image detection to stock
price prediction. This section will focus on the general architecture of Recurrent Neural
Networks (RNNs) and their variants, all of which are primarily concerned with the

Empirical Results
A systematic 8-step procedure is employed in this research for the analysis of any traded
financial products. The procedure is outlined as follows:
• Step 1: Split a time-series data into training and testing parts;
• Step 2: Scale both training and testing parts by min-max or normal measurement;
• Step 3: Convert a time series data in both parts into a supervised learning form;
• Step 4: Cross-validation in training part to seek the optimal hyper-parameters;
• Step 5: Apply ℓ2 norm regularization to prevent Overfitting;
• Step 6: Train and validate the model in the training and testing part respectively;
• Step 7: Construct trading strategy from the results from the testing part;
13

• Step 8: Backtesting trading strategy.
Step 1 involves partitioning the time-series data into distinct training and testing subsets,
ensuring that the model is validated independently and mitigating the risk of look-ahead
bias. This division also facilitates the evaluation of the model’s performance within a
real-world testing environment. Step 2 outlines a method for scaling the original data to
ensure consistency and improve model efficiency. Steps 3 and 4 detail the process of
transforming the time-series data into a tabular format suitable for supervised learning,
as well as the optimization of hyperparameters. Cross-validation is performed rigorously
and effectively during these steps to enhance model robustness and accuracy.
As
highlighted by Shalev and Ben (2014), s in the design of machine learning models is
overfitting. Step 5 addresses this issue by introducing a regularization technique aimed at
mitigating overfitting. It is important to note that the regularization parameter, denoted
as γ, is highly sensitive and functions as a trade-off factor that balances the training loss
LS(θ) with the true loss LD(θ). In Step 6, the model is trained in a manner that is
agnostic to the specific task, allowing it to be applied to either regression or
classification. In Step 7, the procedure generates a full set of pseudo-trading signals,
which are considered simulated trading signals for the purpose of evaluation. Finally,
Step 8 assesses the performance of each strategy through intraday vectorized
backtesting. The results are visualized in equity curves, which depict the cumulative
profit or loss over time. Additionally, the drawdown indicator, an essential measure in
quantitative trading, is also examined.
The drawdown represents the maximum loss
experienced by the strategy during the testing period and serves as a key metric for
evaluating the model’s risk management effectiveness.
Model estimation is a fundamental step in the development of statistical learning
methodologies.
In traditional machine learning models, such as linear regression,
logistic
regression,
and
support
vector
machines,
well-established
estimation
techniques—such as Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Ordinary Least
Squares (OLS) are employed. These techniques operate by optimizing a predefined loss
function to identify the optimal model parameters. In contrast, deep learning models,
including neural networks, necessitate more sophisticated estimation processes due to
their highly non-linear structures and the large number of parameters involved. These
models typically utilize gradient-based optimization methods, such as Stochastic
Gradient Descent (SGD) and its variants (e.g., Adam), to iteratively adjust weights and
biases in order to minimize the loss function. Furthermore, regularization techniques l2
regularization and dropout, are incorporated to mitigate the risk of overfitting during the
estimation process, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data, as
discussed by Goodfellow et al (2016) and LeCun et al (2015).
In this context, we
distinguish between two primary types of model-based signal strategies: those based on
deep learning models and those grounded in classical machine learning techniques.
The deep learning models employed in this research are trained to produce
one-step-ahead forecasts, predicting the subsequent stock price based on historical price
data, and subsequently interpreting these forecasts into actionable trading signals. In
contrast, the classical models are designed to generate strategy-specific signals. In the
present research, ”short-selling” is excluded from the strategy, and the trading approach
14

is restricted to long-only positions.
While the backtesting procedure serves to
demonstrate the efficacy of the strategy, it is important to note that transaction costs,
which are crucial in real-world investment scenarios, are not incorporated into the
evaluation framework. To mitigate look-ahead bias, the prediction period is distinctly
separated from the training period, thereby ensuring the strategy’s practical applicability
and robustness when deployed in real-world settings.

SAFE Machine Learning
Machine Learning has revolutionized numerous fields, offering transformative solutions
across various applications, yet the safety and reliability of these technologies remain a
topic of considerable debate. From healthcare, computer vision, and autonomous car
driving, where algorithms can predict patient outcomes or recommend treatments, to
finance, where they assess credit risks and detect fraud, the potential benefits are vast. In
transportation, machine learning enhances autonomous vehicle systems, aiming to
improve safety and efficiency. However, the inherent opacity of the Machine Learning
model often referred to as the ”black box” problem, raises concerns about their safety
and decision-making processes. The lack of transparency makes it challenging to fully
understand how these models arrive at their conclusions, leading to potential risks such
as unintended biases or erroneous outputs.
As machine learning solutions become
increasingly integral to critical systems, ensuring their reliability and addressing these
safety concerns becomes imperative, requiring ongoing research and robust validation
processes to build trust and mitigate potential risks. That is the reason why we need a
“safe” AI solution.
Safe machine learning is an evolving framework aimed at mitigating the risks associated
with the deployment of AI systems, particularly in sensitive fields like finance. Machine
learning models, known for their predictive power, also present challenges such as lack
of transparency, fairness, and robustness, often referred to as the “black-box” nature of
AI. According to Giudici et al (2024),
the S.A.F.E. framework encompassing
Sustainability, Accuracy, Fairness, and Explainability provides essential metrics to
assess the trustworthiness of AI systems. This is especially crucial in finance, where
models can inadvertently introduce biases, creating unfair advantages or risks. The Rank
Graduation Box (RGB) metrics proposed by Babaei et al. (2025) measure these aspects
through model-agnostic tools, ensuring that AI systems in finance remain robust and fair
under various stress scenarios.
However, the inherent opacity of machine learning
systems raises significant concerns in the financial sector. A critical issue is the inability
to explain decisions made by complex models such as deep learning or random forests.
This lack of explainability can lead to substantial risks in high-stakes environments like
banking or insurance,
where decisions affect financial stability and individual
livelihoods. Regulatory bodies like the European Union have responded by introducing
guidelines such as the AI Act (2021), which mandates that AI systems be not only
accurate but also interpretable and fair (European Commission, 2021)
The financial sector is particularly vulnerable to these risks, as AI-driven decisions often
involve sensitive data and have far-reaching consequences. A failure to address fairness,
for instance, can lead to discriminatory practices, especially in areas like lending or
credit scoring. Giudici et al. (2023) underscore that AI models in finance must be robust
against extreme events, such as market crashes or cyberattacks, and ensure that biases do
not disproportionately affect disadvantaged groups. This is where the Rank Graduation
Fairness (RGF) metric comes into play, as it measures how well AI systems perform
across different demographic groups, thus mitigating unfair outcomes.
30

Moreover, Giudici and Raffinetti (2024) highlight that explainability is another critical
factor in ensuring the safety of machine learning models. For AI systems to be truly
trustworthy, they must offer clear reasoning for their decisions, which is especially
difficult for black-box models. The Rank Graduation Explainability (RGE) metric helps
by offering a quantitative measure of how understandable a model’s decisions are,
ensuring transparency without sacrificing accuracy.
Finally, the development of safe machine learning systems is vital for the responsible use
of AI in finance. With frameworks like S.A.F.E. and tools such as the Rank Graduation
Box, it is possible to measure and control the risks associated with AI, ensuring that
systems remain accurate, fair, and transparent.
The stakes are particularly high in
finance, where model failures can lead to significant financial and ethical consequences,
making it imperative that these systems are rigorously evaluated using comprehensive,
model-agnostic metrics. In this thesis, Python will be utilized assess the reliability of
models applied in trading systems.
The methodology for SAFE AI revolves around a framework for evaluating AI systems
across four key principles: Sustainability, Accuracy, Fairness, and Explainability. These
dimensions are unified under a statistical framework, grounded in the Lorenz curve and
its extensions. This common methodological basis ensures consistency across different
metrics of AI risk evaluation.
The core theoretical foundation uses the Lorenz curve, traditionally applied in
economics to assess income inequality. The authors, Babaei et al (2025) extend this
concept to AI evaluation by introducing two related tools: the Dual Lorenz Curve, which
orders the data inversely, providing an alternative view on data distribution, and the
Concordance Curve, a tool to compare the ranks of two distributions, used to measure
the degree of similarity between predicted and actual outcomes.
These statistical tools are integrated into the Rank Graduation (RG) measure, which is
central to the SAFE framework. The RG measure quantifies the distance between the
concordance curve and the Lorenz curves, yielding a value that reflects the alignment
between predictions and actual outcomes.
The Rank Graduation Box (RGB) incorporates four metrics, each corresponding to a key
principle in the SAFE framework: S for Sustainability, A for Accuracy, F for Fairness and
E for Explainability. The details of the definitions are illustrated as below:
• Sustainability:
Measured by the Rank Graduation Robustness (RGR), which
evaluates the AI model’s robustness by comparing predictions on normal versus
perturbed data (Babaei et al., (2025));
• Accuracy: The Rank Graduation Accuracy (RGA) generalizes the Area Under the
Curve (AUC) to continuous and ordinal predictions, capturing the closeness of
predictions to actual outcomes (Giudici and Raffinetti, (2023));
• Fairness: The Rank Graduation Fairness (RGF) metric assesses whether the AI
31

model treats different population groups equally by comparing models with and
without group-specific variables (Giudici et al., (2024));
• Explainability: The Rank Graduation Explainability (RGE) metric captures the
contribution of each input variable by comparing a full model to models excluding
specific predictors (Raffinetti, (2023)).
Additionally, to validate the metrics, Babaei et al (2025) propose statistical tests based
on U-statistics. These tests are used to determine whether the differences in metrics
between AI models (e.g., accuracy, fairness, robustness) are statistically significant. This
ensures that the performance and risk assessments made using the RGB framework are
reliable. To this end, Python code snippets are used to perform the statistical tests.
The results presented in this work were generated through Python code, which has been
included in the Appendix for reference.
The code follows the SAFE methodology
discussed in the previous section, ensuring consistency with the key principles outlined
earlier. It’s worth highlighting that all models in the thesis, ranging from sophisticated
deep learning algorithms to more traditional machine learning approaches,
are
constructed exclusively from the stock’s own time series data. No external economic
indicators or variables were incorporated into these models. This choice was deliberate,

The focus on time series data means that the models rely solely on past stock
information to predict future behavior, emphasizing the intrinsic value of the stock’s own
historical data. By excluding external economic factors, the analysis isolates the stock’s
inherent patterns and trends. As a result, the models are more self-contained, relying on
the stock’s own movements to drive predictions. This approach is particularly relevant in
finance, where stock-specific analysis can sometimes offer more precise insights than
models that include broader, less directly relevant economic data. Although the overall
methodology touches on multiple dimensions of AI model evaluation, including fairness,
this section of the thesis places particular emphasis on three main aspects: Accuracy,
Sustainability (or Robustness), and Explainability. Accuracy is critical in ensuring that
the models make reliable predictions based on the stock’s past performance.
Sustainability, or robustness, assesses the models’ resilience to changes or disturbances
in the input data, ensuring that predictions remain stable under varying conditions.
Lastly, Explainability is crucial for understanding how the models arrive at their
predictions, offering transparency in decision-making processes.
The empirical results of five models: LSTM, GRU, RNN, SVM, and Logit, in terms of

The

techniques, which play a critical role in preventing Overfitting. Regularization methods,
such as l2 regularization (weight decay), dropout, and early stopping, work by

models are better equipped to maintain stability and accuracy when faced with variations

Concluding remarks
This paper investigates the implications of quantitative trading strategies based on
machine learning algorithms applied to IBM’s stock price data spanning from January
2000 to March 2021. Specifically, the model is trained using data from January 2000 to
February 2019 and evaluated on the subsequent period. The algorithms analyzed include
Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). Each model adheres
rigorously to the comprehensive 8-step procedure outlined in the methodology section,
with model-based strategies independently validated during the testing phase.
The
effectiveness of each strategy is assessed across four key metrics: cumulative profit,
drawdown, hit rate, and false position rate. To facilitate comparison, the equity curves
for each strategy are presented.
Notably, the drawdown metric is integral to risk
management, as it reflects the maximum loss tolerated by each strategy in response to
fluctuations in the underlying asset’s price.
In summary, the results presented in this study indicate that deep learning models
outperform classical machine learning models in both profit generation and risk
management. Among the deep learning models, the GRU-based strategy demonstrated
the most exceptional performance, achieving an accumulated profit of 26.71%,
equivalent to a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 18.65%. The LSTM-based and
RNN-based strategies also delivered favorable outcomes, with the LSTM-based strategy
attaining an accumulated profit of 21.77%, corresponding to a CAGR of 10.50%, and the
RNN-based strategy generating an accumulated profit of 14.30%, which equates to a
CAGR of 6.00%. In contrast, the default buy-and-hold strategy realized only a modest
gain of 0.6%, or a CAGR of 0.4%. Furthermore, the classical machine learning models,
Logistic Regression (LR)-based and Support Vector Machine (SVM)-based strategies,
yielded accumulated profits of 8.80% (CAGR of 6.00%) and 6.15% (CAGR of 2.97%),
respectively. Although these strategies performed better than the buy-and-hold strategy,
their results were considerably more moderate compared to those of the deep learning
models. It is also noteworthy that SAFE methodologies play a pivotal role in elucidating
the decision-making processes of advanced models, which are typically opaque.
In
addition to evaluating model accuracy, SAFE provides users, traders, and stakeholders
with valuable insights into the ’hidden dynamics’ of deeply trained models, enhancing
interpretability and fostering greater transparency in their application.
In conclusion, the findings of this study provide a valuable empirical contribution to the
application of machine learning models in quantitative trading. The results indicate that,
despite areas for potential improvement, deep learning models offer a promising and
effective approach to both profit generation and risk management in quantitative
investment, outperforming traditional model-based strategies as well as the default
buy-and-hold strategy.
