Trading under pressure: Environmental compliance and trading 
19
behavior in the EU Emissions Trading Scheme
20
Abstract
21
Regulated firms under the EU Emissions Trading Scheme (EU ETS) face annually a 
22
strict deadline for buying any lacking emissions allowances and attain environmental 
23
compliance. By employing a complete dataset of firm-level transactions during Phase 
24
I and II of the scheme, our objective in this article is to investigate how this annual 
25
compliance pressure impacts their trading behavior. We find that firms in both EU 
26
ETS phases engaged more often in emissions allowances trading as the compliance 
27
deadline was approaching. Also, while in both phases they were generally following a 
28
“contrarian-type” of trading strategy, they were increasingly engaging in 
29
“momentum-type” trades towards the compliance deadline in Phase II. We explain 
30
these findings based on the specific circumstances surrounding each EU ETS phase 
31
under scrutiny, discuss how this trading behavior may potentially jeopardize the 
32
scheme in achieving its objective and provide relevant policy recommendations.
33
Keywords
34

1. Introduction
38
Reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and tackling climate change requires, 
39
among others, firms operating in emissions-intensive sectors, such as energy, aviation, 
40
and shipping to name a few, to invest into cleaner technology for the manufacturing 
41
of their products or the delivery of their services. Rather than adopting a command42
and-control approach to motivate these investments (Cropper and Oates, 1993), 
43
Europe opted to establish in 2005 the EU Emissions Trading Scheme (EU ETS), a 
44
cap-and-trade system for carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions allowances (Eu 
45
Commission, 2003). Firms falling under the provisions of the scheme are provided 
46
with an annual cap on their CO2 emissions; this comes in the form of emissions 
47
allowances, with each providing the right to emit one ton of CO2 into the atmosphere 
48
(Khaqqi and Sikorski, 2018). If a firm decides to invest into cleaner technology, and 
49
therefore emits less CO2 than its annual cap, the surplus allowances can be sold in the 
50
market, thus partly financing this investment. In contrast, if a firm wishes to emit 
51
more CO2 than its annual cap, it needs to purchase additional allowances from the 
52
market to attain environmental compliance and avoid the strict penalties set otherwise. 
53
In this manner, the necessary investments into cleaner technology are initiated by 
54
those firms facing comparably lower emissions abatement costs, providing the 
55
flexibility to those firms facing higher abatement costs to postpone such investments, 
56
with the expectation that the cost of the cleaner technology they will need to adopt 
57
will be reduced in the future. Through this market mechanism, environmental 
58
economists argue, the reduction of GHG emissions will be achieved at the least 
59
possible cost (Kling and Rubin, 1997).
60
61
The EU ETS is the oldest compulsory “carbon market” in the world (Xiong et al., 
62
2017), and prior to China following suit and adopting a cap-and-trade approach for 
63
reducing their CO2 emissions, the largest (World Bank, 2021). Given the size and 
64
importance of the scheme for European and indeed global environmental policy, the 
65
relevant research from an economics, policy, and finance perspective is vast and 
66
diverse (Schleich et al., 2006; Daskalakis et al., 2009; Li et al., 2019). In this article, 
67
we contribute to the latter strand of research by examining for the first time the impact 
68
of compliance pressure on the trading behavior of firms falling under the provisions 
69
of the scheme.
70
71
In contrast to “traditional” markets, in which speculators, for example, buy and sell 
72
securities in their attempt to maximize their returns, participants in the EU ETS 
73
engage in emissions allowances trading to attain environmental compliance. To 
74
achieve this, each firm has until the end of March each year to submit to the relevant 
75
authorities an externally verified report for the volume of their CO2 emissions over 
76
the previous calendar year, and until the end of April, to surrender the corresponding 
77
number of emissions allowances for cancellation. Failure to do so results in a penalty 
78
of €100 per ton of CO2 (Feijoo and Das, 2014), and the obligation to surrender the 
79
lacking allowances in the next compliance year (Daskalakis et al., 2011). This 
80
mechanism makes the EU ETS a “periodically-stressed” market, since firms lacking 
81
emissions allowances face annually a strict deadline to buy the allowances they 
82
require to become environmentally compliant, while firms with surplus allowances 
83
have to decide either to sell them in the market within the same deadline, or from 
84
2008 onwards, to bank them for compliance not only in the following year but in next 
85
EU ETS phase too.

The extant literature on traditional financial markets suggests that when under 
88
pressure from “stress-inducing” events, such as those related to specific weather or 
89
environmental conditions (Saunders, 1993; Kamstra, 2000; Hirshleifer and Shumway, 
90
2003; Zhang and Wei, 2010; Gelman and Kliger, 2021), the trading behavior of 
91
investors is significantly affected, particularly in terms of the frequency at which they 
92
trade and the trading strategy they follow. Motivated by these insights, we investigate 
93
the impact of compliance pressure on the trading behavior of EU ETS participants by 
94
focusing on these two dimensions, namely, trading frequency and trading strategy. 
95
Adopted from Boolell-Gunesh et al. (2009), we define the former to be the time 
96
interval between a transaction and the immediate previous one from the same firm. 
97
For the latter, motivated again by the literature on traditional financial markets 
98
(Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993; Badrinath and Wahal, 2002; An, 2016), we consider 
99
two trading strategies: a “momentum-type” one, if a firm buys emissions allowances 
100
at a higher price than its immediate previous purchase, or sells at a lower price than its 
101
immediate previous sale, and a “contrarian-type” strategy, if a firm buys emissions 
102
allowances at a lower price than its immediate previous purchase, or sells at a higher 
103
price than its immediate previous sale.
104
105
The analysis is undertaken for the period spanning 2005 to 2012, that is, during Phase 
106
I and most of Phase II of the EU ETS and is based on a novel firm-level trade dataset 
107
that covers all transactions in emissions allowances from non-financial institutions 
108
during the period under scrutiny. This is constructed from the official transaction logs 
109
that are publicly available from the EU Commission, and the data processing and 
110
descriptive analysis are elaborated in Sector of Data Description. Econometrically, 
111
we empirically examine the impact of compliance pressure on the trading frequency 
112
of EU ETS participants through an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model, and the 
113
impact on their trading strategy through a Generalized Linear Model (GLM). A 
114
detailed description of these empirical models is presented in Sector of Methodology.
115
116
Our results indicate that compliance pressure is a stress-inducing event for EU ETS 
117
participants that significantly affects their trading behavior. With the compliance 
118
deadline approaching, firms not only trade more often in emissions allowances, but 
119
they also seem to change their trading strategy from a contrarian- to a momentum120
type one, at least in Phase II of the scheme, that is, after the initial flaws in the 
121
operation of the EU ETS identified in Phase I had been ironed out. The former, while 
122
it is to be expected given the difficulty for any firm to accurately forecast the number 
123
of emissions allowances it will need to be environmentally compliant after a year, it 
124
can potentially lead to allowances’ prices deviating from their fundamentals. The 
125
implication of this would be that emissions allowances prices will no longer be a 
126
reliable signal for the needed investments in cleaner technology. The latter highlights 
127
a short-termism behavior of EU ETS participants as it suggests their preference in 
128
selling emissions allowances, rather than banking them for compliance in the future, 
129
even when there is a downward trend in their prices. Based on these findings, we 
130
argue that actions should be taken from a policy perspective to address the impact of 
131
the annual compliance pressure on the trading behavior of the EU ETS participants as 
132
it has the potential to compromise the scheme from achieving its objective. An 
133
example would be to promote emissions allowances trading throughout the year by 
134
allowing firms to surrender allowances prior to the end of the year, coupled with 
135
incentives to do this. This would improve the liquidity, depth, and efficiency of the

139
Our work renders three main contributions. First, we find that firms falling under the 
140
provisions of the EU ETS engaged more often in emissions allowances trading as the 
141
compliance deadline was approaching. This was particularly the case for those firms 
142
with high emissions allowances requirements, and less so for those that already held a 
143
large proportion of the allowances they needed to be environmentally compliant. This 
144
finding extends the literature investigating emissions allowances prices and the 
145
volatility of their returns (Feng et al., 2011; Aatola et al., 2013; Fan and Todorova, 
146
2017; Tan and Wang, 2017; Zhao et al., 2018; Lin and Jia, 2019; Lovcha et al., 2022) 
147
by identifying a further source behind the volatility clustering observed in the EU 
148
ETS. This refers to periods of low return volatility followed by periods of increased 
149
volatility. In the European carbon market, the latter occur at the end of the calendar 
150
year and also, in the months leading to the compliance deadline (European Securities 
151
and Markets Authority, 2022). While this has been attributed to the increased volume 
152
of emissions allowances traded during this period (Fan et al., 2016), research in 
153
traditional markets has shown that volatility clustering can also arise due to increased 
154
trading frequency (Xue and Gençay, 2012). Thus, our finding suggests that volatility 
155
clustering in the EU ETS is not only because more emissions allowances are traded 
156
from December to April each year, but also because firms during this period trade 
157
more frequently.
158
159
Second, we find that the emitters in the EU ETS generally followed a contrarian-type 
160
trading strategy, however, they were increasingly engaging in momentum-type of 
161
trades as the compliance deadline in Phase II was approaching. In the case of Phase I 
162
of the EU ETS, the fact that firms generally followed a contrarian-type of trading 
163
strategy throughout the period can be explained on the basis of the generous caps 
164
provided to firms, along with the fact that emissions allowances issued in Phase I 
165
could not be used for compliance in Phase II. This led to a situation in which the 
166
demand for emissions allowances in the latter part of Phase I was particularly weak 
167
and therefore, emissions allowances were trading during the end of that period at a 
168
price of a few eurocents (Daskalakis et al., 2011; Feng et al., 2012; Cong and Lo, 
169
2017). The switch in the trading strategy observed in the period leading to the 
170
compliance deadline in Phase II, however, leads to interesting new insights on the 
171
trading practices of participants in the EU ETS and in turn, extends the literature 
172
examining emissions allowances prices on the basis of the trading behavior of firms 
173
falling under the provisions of the scheme (Fan et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019; Guo et 
174
al., 2020).
175
176
Specifically, since buying transactions from firms lacking emissions allowances near 
177
the compliance deadline in Phase II were taking place when there was an upward 
178
trend in allowances’ prices, implies that firms, given the annual compliance pressure 
179
they are facing, are particularly concerned about the potential of incurring increased 
180
compliance costs if prices continue to rise. However, this may put an upward pressure 
181
on emissions allowances prices that is not reflective of an increase in their 
182
fundamentals, that is, marginal abatement costs. Correspondingly, since selling 
183
transactions from firms with surplus emissions allowances were mainly taking place 
184
when there was a downward trend in the allowances’ prices indicates that firms prefer 
185
to recoup part of the costs of their investments rather than banking the surplus

Third, while the revisions undertaken in the operation of the EU ETS after Phase I 
194

195
Markellos, 2008; Montagnoli and De Vries, 2010; Crossland et al., 2013; Daskalakis, 
196
2013), the annual compliance pressure firms face seems to impact their trading 
197
behavior in a manner that may still jeopardize the scheme in achieving its objective. 
198
In more detail, a necessary condition for a cap-and-trade system to facilitate the 
199
reduction of CO2 emissions in a cost-effective manner is for the price of the emissions 
200
allowances to accurately reflect marginal abatement costs (Rubin, 1996). The reason 
201
is that otherwise, the time at which a firm will decide to proceed into the adoption of 
202
cleaner technology will be suboptimal; if the emissions allowances price is lower than 
203
marginal abatement costs, a firm may opt to incorrectly postpone such investment, 
204
while if it is higher, it may be led to undertake a poor investment. In both cases, the 
205
extent at which the cap-and-trade system will be able to achieve its objective is 
206
adversely impacted as the adoption of the cleaner technology will not be initiated by 
207
those facing comparably lower abatement costs.

2. Literature review
216
2.1. Compliance and carbon trading
217
Annual compliance is perhaps one of the most important events in cap-and-trade 
218
carbon markets, and can be costly to the participants of such markets (Kellenberg, 
219
2009; Hartmann and Vachon, 2018; Wang et al., 2018). Increased compliance cost not 
220
only compromises the fulfillment of carbon markets’ objective, that is, to reduce GHG 
221
emissions at the least possible cost via this market mechanism (Kling and Rubin, 
222
1997; Khaqqi et al., 2018), but also affects a wide range of economic activities such 
223
as production (Alberola et al., 2009), stock investment (Clarkson et al., 2015), and 
224
even foreign direct investment (Nippa et al., 2021). In essence, compliance cost is 
225
related to the number of emissions allowances an emitting company will need to be 
226
environmentally compliant and the prices at which the required carbon allowances are 
227
traded. From a firm-level transaction perspective, the allowance prices are influenced 
228
by the allowances that were transferred from carbon market participants with a long 
229
allowance position to those with a short allowance position (Ellerman and Buchner, 
230
2008). More specifically, in Phase I of the EU ETS, the prices of carbon allowances 
231
were positively correlated to the volumes of compliance trading (Fan et al., 2016), 
232
which is defined as the carbon trading that aims at close the position gap between the 
233
number of allowances on hand and the number of allowances to be surrendered.

234
However, the influence of compliance trading became increasingly marginalized in 
235
Phase II of the EU ETS (Berta et al., 2017), possibly resulting from the growing of 
236
market activities (Berta et al., 2017), increase of maturity (Crossland et al., 2013; 
237
Daskalakis, 2013), and reduction of total compliance trading (Wang et al., 2019).
238
239
Aside from the prices of carbon allowances, trading behavior might also be under the 
240
influence of compliance. The present evidence suggests that in the EU ETS, verified 
241
emissions announcements could promote the volume of compliance trading, and such 
242
pattern is more noticeable in Phase I (Guo et al., 2018). Since non-compliance trading 
243
is closely related to compliance trading (Wang et al., 2019), compliance would impart 
244
its pressure on both types of carbon trading behavior. Theoretically, radical changes in 
245
asset prices are usually accompanied with sharp fluctuations in trading volumes 
246
(Baker and Wurgler, 2006). In this sense, compliance, which is regarded as the bane 
247
of causing periodically structural breaks in carbon prices (Alberola et al., 2009), 
248
would affect trading behavior of the participants of such cap-and-trade carbon 
249
markets. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, no article empirically examines the 
250
influence of compliance on the micro-level trading behavior of carbon market 
251
participants.

2.2. Trading frequency and strategy
253
Trading frequency is one fundamental dimension of trading behavior. It is commonly 
254
believed that trading frequency of investors has less flexibility to change, therefore 
255
this feature has been extensively employed as the proxies to investor characteristics 
256
such as sophistication (Shapira Z, Venezia, 2001; Dhar and Zhu, 2006; Boolell257
Gunesh et al., 2019), experience (Shu et al., 2002; Da Costa et al., 2013), and 
258
overconfidence (Barber and Odean, 2001; Chen et al., 2007). More recently, a piece 
259
of empirical evidence suggests that trading frequency can be affected by 
260
psychological effects such as disposition bias (Kahya and Ekinci, 2022), a 
261
phenomenon where investors tend to sell winning investments and hold on to losing 
262
investments (Shefrin and Statman, 1985). This opens the possibility of investigating 
263
the influential factors of trading frequency, and calls for a deeper understanding on 
264
this basic dimension of trading behavior.
265
266
Ever since the first documentation in stock markets (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993), the 
267
trace of momentum- and contrarian-type trading is reported in a wide variety of 
268
markets, including foreign exchange markets (Wan and Kao, 2009), real estate 
269
markets (De Haan and Kakes, 2011), cryptocurrency markets (Kosc et al., 2019), and 
270
carbon markets (Crossland et al., 2013), and both trading strategies are adopted and 
271
practiced by individuals (Baltzer et al., 2019), institutions (Badrinath and Wahal, 
272
2002; De Haan and Kakes, 2011; Baltzer et al., 2019), and even algorithmic traders 
273
(Arumugam and Prasanna, 2021). While a rich body of the extant trading strategy 
274
literature concentrates on identifying these strategies (De Haan and Kakes, 2011; 
275
Baltzer et al., 2019), estimating and explaining their profitability (Rouwenhorst, 1998; 
276
Jegadeesh and Titman; 2001), only limited studies tend to examine trading behavior 
277
of buy and sell separately for uncovering the differentiated trading strategies in 
278
different types of trading behavior. By decomposing institutional transactions into 
279
entries (new positions) and exits (clearing positions), Badrinath and Wahal (2002) 
280
show that institutions are momentum traders for entries but contrarian traders for 
281
exits. Based on an account-level trade dataset, Ng and Wu (2007) find that Chinese 
282
institutions are momentum traders and less wealthy Chinese individuals are contrarian

traders, while wealthiest Chinese individuals behave like institutions in buying but 
284
behave like less wealthy individuals in selling. Focusing on individual investors, 
285
Duxbury and Yao (2017) suggest that Chinese individuals are momentum traders 
286
when buying stocks, but become contrarian traders when selling stocks. Notably, all 
287
these works imply that a given investor would employ the same trading strategy to 
288

289

290
decisions are linked to the past returns of assets (Shefrin and Statman, 1985; Grinblatt 
291
and Keloharju, 2001; Barber et al., 2009), which can be rather inconsistent (Grinblatt 
292
and Moskowitz, 2004) and volatile (Barinov, 2018), thus might lead to the potential 
293
changes in trading strategy. This urges further investigation on the topic.
294
295
In summary, despite the significance of compliance is widely recognized in the 
296
current carbon market literature, there is no empirical evidence concerning how 
297
compliance, as one most notable stress-inducing event in cap-and-trade carbon 
298
markets, influence firm-level trading behavior. With respect to the two fundamental 
299
dimensions of trading behavior, that is, trading frequency and trading strategy, there 
300
are extremely limited studies that investigate the variation in trading frequency, and 
301
no existing work has been rendered to recognize the potential for market participants 
302
to change their trading strategies across buying and selling decisions.

3.2. Model for trading strategy
326
For examining the impact of compliance pressure on the trading strategy of firms 
327
falling under the provisions of the EU ETS, we use the following GLM model:
𝑇𝑟𝑆𝑡𝑟𝑎= 𝑇𝑖𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑝+ 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑝𝑅𝑒𝑞+ 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑝𝑅𝑒𝑞𝑑+ 𝛥𝑃𝑟𝑖𝑐𝑒+ 𝑆1𝑑+ 𝑆2𝑑
(2)
328
In Equation (2), 𝑇𝑟𝑆𝑡𝑟𝑎 takes the value of 1 if the firm follows a momentum-type 
329
trading strategy and 0 otherwise. We consider the former to be the case when the firm 
330
buys emissions allowances when 𝛥𝑃𝑟𝑖𝑐𝑒> 0, and sells allowances when 
331
𝛥𝑃𝑟𝑖𝑐𝑒< 0. Finally, 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑝𝑅𝑒𝑞𝑑 is a dummy that takes the value of 1 when, as a 
332
result of a transaction, 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑝𝑅𝑒𝑞 changes from a positive value (i.e., the firm prior 
333
to the transaction had surplus allowances) to a negative one (i.e., the firm after the 
334
transaction requires allowances for attaining environmental compliance), or vice 
335
versa, and takes a value of 0 otherwise. Finally, note that Equation (2) is used in the 
336
case of buying transactions, with 𝐶𝑜𝑚𝑝𝑅𝑒𝑞𝑑 being dropped in the case of selling 
337
transactions. The reason is that in our original dataset, as expected, there were no 
338
selling transactions from firms lacking emissions allowances for compliance.

4. Data description
340
4.1. Data aggregation
341
The data collection and processing procedure is the same one followed in our 
342
previous work (Guo et al., 2020), and non-emitting market participants are removed 
343
since their trading objectives are different from that of emitting firms and the market 
344
(Liu et al., 2017). Briefly, the complete Community Independent Transaction Log 
345
(CITL) for Phase I of the EU ETS and the EU Transaction Log (EUTL) for Phase II 
346
of the scheme were downloaded from the EU Commission’s website. From these 
347
unique firm-level transactions in emissions allowances, we focused only on those 
348
from firms with an Operator Holding Account (OHA). This was to ensure the dataset 
349
excluded transactions from speculators that are free from compliance pressure and 
350
included only transactions from firms regulated under the EU ETS. Overall, our 
351
sample consists of 724,790 firm-level transactions in all three types of allowances 
352
available for trading in the EU ETS, namely, European Union Allowances (EUAs), 
353
Emissions Reduction Units (ERUs), and Certified Emissions Reductions (CERs), over 
354
the period from February 2005 to June 2012. Naturally, the total number of 
355
surrendered allowances in each phase of the EU ETS under scrutiny equals the sum of 
356
the allocated, traded, and canceled allowances.
357
358
As previously mentioned, trading frequency is acquired as the time interval between a 
359
transaction and the immediate previous one conducted by the same firm, momentum360
type trading is defined as buying emissions allowances at a higher price than the 
361
firm’s immediate previous purchase or selling allowances at a lower price than the 
362
firm’s immediate previous sale, and contrarian-type trading is defined as buying 
363
emissions allowances at a lower price than the firm’s immediate previous purchase or 
364
selling at a higher price than the firm’s immediate previous sale.

6. Conclusion and policy implications
550
Focusing on the EU ETS, the oldest carbon market in the world, here we demonstrate 
551
that the pressure induced by compliance obligation could influence the trading 
552
behavior of the market’s participants. Two measures of the compliance pressure, i.e., 
553
the time intervals to the date of compliance and abundancy of allowances, all have an 
554
impact on the trading frequencies and trading strategies of the firms in the EU ETS. 
555
Specifically, carbon allowances transactions were intensified when the compliance 
556
date was closer, and the sufficiency of available allowances tend to reduce the trading 
557
frequency. Regarding trading strategy, the participating firms of the EU ETS can 
558
generally be regarded as contrarian traders in both phases, but in Phase II, they were 
559
increasingly making momentum trading towards the compliance deadline.
560
561
With the annual compliance pressure EU ETS participants face impacting their 
562
trading behavior in a way that contributes to increased volatility in the market, and 
563
potentially results in emissions allowances prices deviating from their fundamentals at 
564
the period leading to the compliance deadline, not only is the investment signal for 
565
firms not clear, but importantly, it can be misleading. From a policy perspective 
566
therefore, actions could be taken to alleviate the impact of the annual compliance 
567
pressure on the firms’ trading behavior. A way to achieve this is to allow and provide 
568
incentives for firms to surrender emissions allowances earlier than the April deadline. 
569
For instance, by providing a progressive reduction in the total number of emissions 
570
allowances they will need to surrender in a year, depending on the number of 
571
allowances they surrendered each month or quarter of the year, will motivate them to 
572
engage more actively in emissions allowances trading throughout the year, rather than 
573
primarily as the annual compliance deadline approaches. How to design this 
574
mechanism so that it has the desired effect on firms’ trading behavior, and at the same 
575
time not to imperil the achievement of the CO2 emissions reductions targets, is an 
576
issue we leave for further research.
