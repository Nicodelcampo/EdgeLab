The Intersectionality of High Frequency Trading
(HFT) and Market Fragmentation, and their Impact
on Liquidity: New Evidence
Shahadat Hossain¹
Abstracts: We address two important recent trends in global equity markets—high-frequency trading
(HFT) and trade fragmentation, in this paper and join the debate regarding their social benefits. We take
the European equity market as a laboratory as it has been confronting both the issues since its adoption
of Market in Financial Instruments and Directives (MiFID) in 2007. We employ a unique extraordinarily
large millisecond time-stamped trades and quotes dataset of the London Stock Exchange (LSE) listed securities which use the trading platform of the LSE and three dominant European multilateral trading facilities
(MTFs)—CHIX, Bats and Turquoise. We take a novel approach and evaluate the impact of HFT and fragmentation on market liquidity jointly. The results reveal a fresh evidence that the there exists an interlinkage
between HFT and trade fragmentation, and it determines, at least partially, the size of the individual effect
of HFT and fragmentation on liquidity. We find that higher HFT is beneficial for market liquidity, however
fragmentation is liquidity damaging. HFT has contributed positively over the past in reducing both the realized spreads and adverse selection costs, whereas market fragmentation appears detrimental to the both
during the same period. We show that HFT’s liquidity benefits are greater for large stocks, and small stocks
take more harms from the market fragmentation. The dynamic effect shows that the directions of association
between HFT and market fragmentation, and liquidity remain the same over time but the size of the impact
is time-varying. Our results manifest the simultaneity that exists between HFT, market fragmentation and
liquidity. We show that the results are robust.

1
Introduction
The financial market places have been undergoing pathbreaking changes due to the advent of sophisticated computing technology for the last two decades (Biais and Foucault, 2014; O’Hara, 2015).
The rise of machine intelligence have empowered human civilization to do things at ease, speed,
and low-cost, and at the same time, raised the concern of welfare damaging inequitable competition between man and machines. High-frequency trading (HFT) is one of the recent changes in
market microstructure that attracted most attention to the regulators, practitioners and academics.
A large share of trading volume in today’s equity market is traded by high-frequency traders (HFTs)
(Boehmer, Fong and Wu, 2021). There has been public debate since 2009 about its effect (Menkveld,
2016). The rise of machines has also given rise to the proliferation of machine-friendly trading
venues and their high speed connecting channels. The two remarkable financial market regulation
changes in the recent past, Reg NMS (in the US) and MiFID (in EU) broke the long dominated
concentration rule, and opened the avenue for dispersing trades across the venues. The effects of
the new exchange competition and the trade fragmentation on market quality have also raised concern(O’Hara and Ye, 2011; Degryse, De Jong and Kervel, 2015).
As academics, practitioners and regulators strive on these issues , the hidden mechanisms of
the high-frequency world is becoming visible more and more. A conjecture is on its way that HFT
and market fragmentation have ridden on each other in their advancement. Menkveld (2014, 2016)
argues “...the two most salient trends in securities markets since the turn of the century—order flow
fragmentation and HFT entry—are intimately related and both driven by technology and regulation. ... There is arguably a symbiotic relationship between new electronic venues and HFTs. These
new venues need HFTs to insert aggressively priced bid and ask quotes, and HFTs need the new
venues to satisfy their requirements in terms of automation, speed, and low fees.’’ We take this
novel aspect of market complexity and look into the deeper of the problem—how HFT and market
fragmentation interact and impact the market quality? In doing so, we find the European equity
market as a suitable laboratory since it has been experiencing both the influx of HFT and market
fragmentation and, to the best of our knowledge, no study to date address this issue.
MiFID repealed the concentration rule² in European equity market and paved the way for the

fragmentation developed on the millisecond time-stamped records. Our empirical approach target
to establish a causal relationship between HFT, market fragmentation and liquidity in several steps.

unknown heterogeneity across firms and between time, we use both day (time) and firm (stocks)

in HFT in large stock provides more liquidity, and a higher market fragmentation in small stocks

contrasting effects on large and small stocks. Unlike large stocks, a rise in HFT in a highly fragmented small stock harms the liquidity. On the other hand, the liquidity damaging nature of market

that the increased trade fragmentation in the same period took away at least some of the additional

2
Relevant Literature
Our study is relevant to two main strands of market microstructure literature—HFT and market
fragmentation. The HFT literature is relatively young and growing with respect to the research in
fragmentation. The main contribution of our study is to focus on a relatively new issue, i.e. the
interaction of HFT and order flow fragmentation and it covers three related aspects: (i) HFT, (ii)
order flow fragmentation, and (iii) interaction of HFT and order flow fragmentation and their impacts on market liquidity.
Before moving to the literature, we find it useful to clarify our stand on some relevant terminologies. Our studies deals with a subsection of Algorithmic Trading (AT) known as HFT and by
market fragmentation we deal only the visible or lit order flow fragmentation across exchanges.
Many prior studies attempt to distinguish between HFT and Algorithmic Trading (AT). Often, it
creates confusion when they are used interchangeably without mentioning the context, as both are
dominant in literature but have different roles to play in the market (Gomber, Arndt, Lutat and Uhle,
2011; The Netherlands Authority for the Financial Markets, 2010; Aldridge, 2013). To be on the
safe side, we take the definitions which are well founded in academic literature and use them within
the context interchangeably to refer a HFT firm or high-frequency traders (HFTs). Practically, HFT
and AT share features like low-latency in order routing and execution, use of algorithms, requires
less human intervention, maintains direct and high speed market access; but differ in the use of
algorithms, inventory position taking, proprietary/agency trading, frequency of quote update, market strategy and others. Gomber et al. (2011) provide an excellent coverage on the concepts of
HFT and AT. The distinct characteristics of HFT are high-speed quote updates reflected in huge
5

number of orders and rapid order cancellation, proprietary trading, generation of profit from market
making, keeping very short holding period with zero net inventory, use of low-latency technology
like colocation, sponsored market access and direct data feeds and other criterias. On the contrary,
AT represent the features like agent trading, trading strategies to minimize price impacts (from a
large order), keeping inventory for relatively a long holding period. Therefore, HFT is considered
a subset of AT.
2.1
Theoretical predictions
The HFT literature suggests the possible mechanisms using which HFT and fragmentation and also
their interaction affect the market liquidity. Biais and Foucault (2014) explain the economic channels through which HFT could impact market liquidity. HFTs can be imagined as modern traders
which perform the traditional market making role using computing technology. HFT firms used
to develop dynamic algorithms taking exchange feeds in real time, and carry out market making
function where they become ultra-fast. Modern technology enables HFTs to, (i) get fastest access
to the market; (ii) acquire and processes information in almost real time; and (iii) watch and routes
orders across markets. Thus, a general conjecture is that HFT should be able to provide liquidity at
a lower cost. On the flip of the coin is that slow traders are at an information disadvantage relative
to HFT, which might create adverse selection cost.
Foucault, Pagano and Roell (2013) and Cantillon and Yin (2011) explain the possible effects
of order flow fragmentation. As per as market fragmentation concerns, there are two opposite
views— one holds that fragmentation improves liquidity, and the other is that it harms liquidity.
The main arguments against fragmentation or in favour of exchange floor monopoly are scale economics and the existence of network externalities in trading. In a concentrated market, it is easier
to find a counter party, which minimizes search costs. Pagano (1989) shows “If trade is equally
costly across markets, this externality leads to the concentration of trade on one market. If not,
it can produce multiple conjectural equilibria, some where trade concentrates on one market and
others where large traders resort to a separate market or to search for a trading partner.” Again, the
price impact of transactions also tends to be smaller in markets that attract higher trading volume.

The dominant argument in favour of order flow fragmentation is that it places competitive pressure
on the transaction fees charged by exchanges. To attract traders, the modern exchange platforms
used to make huge investment for minimizing their latency time, which consequently intensifies
competition among modern market makers like HFTs.
Menkveld (2014) has explained the possible channels through which HFT and market fragmentation might impact the market. The economics is HFTs need new electronic venues for low fees
and faster trading, and new trading venues need HFTs for better quotes. It is argued that cost and
benefit of HFT and market fragmentation should be calculated jointly. We design our study on this
theoretical support, taking into account both HFT and fragmentation in the same empirical structure, and examine their joint impact on liquidity.
Models describing the effect of HFT on market liquidity are not enormous, limited to a few
aspects of HFT and fragmentation, and generate varying predictions, mostly depending on their
assumptions and focuses. Goettler, Parlour and Rajan (2009) predict when market makers like
HFT are more informed about fundamental value it can improve liquidity. In Biais, Foucault and
Moinas (2011), HFT generates adverse selection problem which harms market liquidity. Some
other models also predict detrimental liquidity effect of HFT through front-running (Li, 2014),
winner’s curse (Han, Khapko and Kyle, 2014) and a wasteful arms race for speed (Budish, Cramton
and Shim, 2015). In contrast, Jovanovic and Menkveld (2015) and Ait-Sahalia and Saglam (2017)
predict both positive and negative liquidity effects of HFT depending on the market environments
in which HFT works.
2.2
Empirical evidence
The empirical HFT literature has been growing on several strands. We limit our discussion on
those which are particularly relevant to our paper. Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld (2011) and
Hasbrouck and Saar (2013), two seminal papers in HFT, first propose the idea of proxy in detecting
HFT footprint using the limit order book updates. Hendershott et al. (2011) is the first to examine causality between AT and market quality, where they introduce an algorithmic trading proxy
based on electronic message rate (order book update). Using monthly observations for NYSE listed
7
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3993928

943 common stocks for the period 2001–2005, they report positive impact of AT on liquidity, for
large stocks in particular. Hasbrouck and Saar (2013) propose a different low-latency measure,
‘strategic-run’ based on a particular trade executing behaviour of HFT and use the same to assess
the impact of low-latency trading on market quality. Their findings suggest that low-latency activity improves liquidity. Boehmer et al. (2021) study the effect of AT on market quality using data
on 42 equity markets for the period 2001–2011. They use colocation as an instrument variable to
tackle the endogeneity and provide evidence that AT improves liquidity. Use of colocation in HFT
research is quite common but also controversial. Michael Aitken, Douglas Cumming (2021) show
that colocation does not qualify the criteria for being used as an instrument—it is not exogenously
determined and and highly correlated with market quality measures.
Exchange platforms in Europe and US updated their latency technology and related value added
services enormously over the past few decades so that they can attract more and more HFTs. This
feature of market competition has eventually empowered the HFTs market making capacity more
than ever. Many papers examine the impact of speed on market quality. Riordan and Storkenmaier
(2012) study the system upgrade of Deutsche Boerse with the 8.0 release of Xetra on April 23,
2007 which reduced system latency from 50ms to 10ms. Frino, Mollica and Webb (2014) examine
the impact of allowing traders to co-locate near exchange server on the liquidity of future contracts
traded on the Australian Securities Exchange. Murray, Pham and Singh (2016) investigate the role
of latency in market quality in the Australian Securities Exchange following the introduction of the
Integrated Trading Platform and ASXTrade which reduced latency from 70 ms to 30 ms. Frino,
Mollica, Monaco and Palumbo (2017) examine the impact of AT on market liquidity following the
implementation of proximity hosting service by Borsa Italiana. Hendershott and Moulton (2011)
study the impact of introducing hybrid market in New York Stock Exchange in 2006 which reduced
the execution time of market order from 10 seconds to less than one second. Brogaard, Hagströmer,
Nordén and Riordan (2015) exploit an optional colocation upgrade at NASDAQ OMX Stockholm
to assess how speed affects market liquidity. All of these papers on external HFT shocks other than
Hendershott and Moulton (2011) provide evidence that reduction in system latency or enhancing
the speed improves liquidity, and the converse is shown in Hendershott and Moulton (2011).
Several recent papers examine the impact of market fragmentation on market quality with contradictory results. O’Hara and Ye (2011) is one of the first study to documents the causality between

fragmentation and market quality. They use a dataset of 262 U.S. stocks over the six months in
2008. They find that more fragmented stocks are associated with lower transaction cost and fastest
execution speed. Unlike O’Hara and Ye (2011), some recent papers, mostly focused on order flow
fragmentation, analyse the cross market liquidity. Upson and Van Ness (2017) study the volume
fragmentation, cross market competition of AT, and their impact on liquidity using NYSE data only
for the first quarter of 2012. They find that volume fragmentation has a positive effect on the best
depth level across markets but venue competition and excess AT activities harm market liquidity.
Degryse et al. (2015) is the first study to address the issue of fragmentation in European equity markets by introducing cross market measures of depth and spreads similar to Foucault and Menkveld
(2008), however they does not consider any aspect of HFT. They study 51 Dutch stocks across
European venues for the initial post-MiFID period (November 2007–December 2009) and provide
evidence that lit fragmentation lowers liquidity at the traditional market. Gresse (2017) also assesses the impact of both lit and dark fragmentation in European markets in a cross market setting.
She uses data on the LSE and Euronext’s blue-chip stocks for the period October 2007–November
2009 and establishes control for AT in regressions. Her findings suggest that lit fragmentation improves liquidity across markets.
The literature examining the effect of both HFT and market fragmentation on market quality—
the research niche where we design our study, is comparatively new. Brogaard, Hendershott and
Riordan (2014b) study the interaction of HFT and fragmentation with the aim to explain the role
HFT on market quality through market integration. Based on Canadian market data, their preliminary results show that HFTs play a key role in tying market together, but results are inconclusive
regarding the impact on liquidity. Aitken, Cumming and Zhan (2014) study the joint impact of AT
and market fragmentation in U.S. equity market following the fragmentation regulation RegNMS.
They show that fragmentation of the lit market order flow improves market liquidity and reduces
manipulation, and the dark market order flow fragmentation damages the liquidity and increases
the manipulation. Our study mainly contributes in the area of intersection between HFT and lit
fragmentation.

Market, Data, Variables and Measures
3.1
Market Background
Two of the most recent striking changes in global equity market design (those have been proliferating trading venues) are the adoption of ‘Regulation National Market System (RegNMS)’ in the
US in 2005, and the enactment of MiFID in Europe in 2007, following the development in the
US. The adoption of MiFID in 2007 ( MiFID II in 2018) has been proliferating the trading venues
across European equity market. One of the vital change it brought into affect was abolishing the
monopoly power of traditional exchanges in trading securities and liberalizing them into many trading platforms like regulated markets (RMS), multilateral trading facilities (MTFs), and systematic
internaliser (SIs). These platforms have different market structure and reporting system that are
defined under MiFID directives⁴.
In broad terms, RMs and MTFs operate in a similar fashion, providing an electronic multilateral platform for users. These trading venues generally match orders on a non-discretionary basis
according to pre-defined rules that establish price and time priority for submitted orders. RMs and
MTFs are required to publish pre-trade quotes and report details of executed trades to the market (CFA Institute, 2011). Both RMs and MTFs are allowed to organize primary listing. However,
RMs facilitate the listing of regulated instrument and MTFs do the same for the unregulated one. In
practice, only RMs offer primary listing service. MTFs prefer not to do so and they may be viewed
as equivalent to electronic trading networks (ECNs) in the US (Gresse, 2017). A firm choose on
which RM to list, and once listed, MTFs may decide to organize trading for that firm as well. SIs
are investment firm that internalise order flow to deal on their own account on an organised, frequent and systematic basis. Trades executed through SIs are reported as the over-the-counter (OTC)
trades. Some of the large RMs in European equity market include the LSE Group (operator of the
London Stock Exchange and Borsa Italiana), NYSE Euronext (which operates exchanges in France,
Belgium, the Netherlands, Portugal, and the United Kingdom), and Deutsche Borse Group (operator of the Frankfurt Exchange and the Xetra trading system).
The LSE runs electronic order books on which buy and sell orders are continuously matched

Variables and Measures
3.3.1
High-Frequency Trading

exchange provided HFT flaaged dataset (direct method) or ii) to use a proxy (indirect method), for
measuring HFT. The choice is primarily determined by the nature of access to relevant datasets. The
supply of the datasets with HFT-flag are quite limited and subject to some criticisms due to their
possible sample selection bias and data coverage (Conrad, Wahal and Xiang, 2015). Conversely,
studies relying on a proxy for HFT identification are also diverse in their selection criteria. One of
the main reasons is that the features by which HFTs are identified are many, and every dataset is
not commonly enriched with all HFT tracing information. As our datasets do not have any direct

3.3.3
Liquidity
We compute several standard measures of liquidity available in the literature. The market microstructure literature classifies liquidity into several dimensions, like transaction costs, quantity,
time, and so on. We use the relative quoted spread (𝑠𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑑𝑖𝑡) and the effective-half spread (𝑒𝑠𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑑𝑖𝑡)
which measure the transaction costs dimension of liquidity. We decompose the effective half-spread
into the price impact (𝑝𝑖𝑚𝑝𝑎𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑡) and the realized half-spread (𝑟𝑠𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑑𝑖𝑡). The price impact measures the gross losses to liquidity demanders which is also known as the adverse selection cost. On
the other hand, realized spreads measures the revenue earned by liquidity providers.
To address the quantity dimension of liquidity, the quoted depth is measured at and beyond
the BBO. The recent evidence (AMF, 2017) suggests that HFTs’ liquidity supply surrounds several
depth levels of the limit order book. The quoted depth refers to the available buy and sell quantities at prices close to the best bid and offer (BBO) price, practically at mid price. The measures,

4.2.3
The sources of liquidity change
We reported earlier about the significant decrease in quoted and effective spreads during the sample period. As we know, the quoted and effective spreads both take the liquidity demanders view.
Beside, it can also be seen as a gain from the context of liquidity suppliers (realized spreads). However, the possible lasting pressure on prices due to buy and sell orders also add risk to the liquidity
suppliers inventory position(Foucault et al., 2013) which in turn decreases their revenues (price
impacts). To show the impact of effective spreads on liquidity suppliers, we decompose it into realized spreads and price impacts following Hendershott et al. (2011). A narrower (greater) effective
spreads implies either less (more) revenue per trade for liquidity providers, or smaller (larger) gross
loss due to informed liquidity demanders, or both (Hendershott et al., 2011).

and the price impact measured at 5-minute intervals over the past, whereas, market fragmentation
does the same for realized spreads but hindrances the adverse selection costs. Apparently, HFT has
improved the execution quality, and it can be seen in the descriptive statistics (Table 4 and 6) that
the realized spread has turned to almost negative in the post-MiFID era. The negative realized-half
spreads imply that liquidity providers are earning less revenue per trade than before. It also im24

5.1
A two-stage optimal IV-GMM regression (H2SLS) approach
Endogeneity issues are commonly admitted in the HFT and fragmentation literature (e.g. Hendershott et al. (2011); Hasbrouck and Saar (2013); Degryse et al. (2015); Gresse (2017)). In the
presence of endogeneity, establishing the causal link between HFT and market liquidity or fragmentation and market liquidity is challenging. It might be the case that HFT and market liquidity,
or market fragmentation and market liquidity, or both are simultaneously determined in equilibrium, so that there may be bi-directional causality. For example, as Hasbrouck and Saar (2013)
explain “an exogenous drop in spreads might establish a more attractive environment for, and lead
to increase in, low-latency activity.” This mechanism would induce correlation between HFT proxy
and the error terms, which makes OLS estimates inconsistent. It might also be the case that a highly
liquid stock is more fragmented than an illiquid one. There is also the possibility that fragmentation is driven by the HFT, or a highly fragmented market attracts more HFT, and eventually both
impact market liquidity. If this is the case then it poses the challenge on all previous specifications,
whether they are truly identified or not.
To tackle the possible biases arising from the endogeneity, one solution is to adopt an IV approach where it is necessary to find out at least one or more distinct instruments which are correlated
with the endogenous variable but not with the errors. In our case, we need two instruments, at least
one for each of the two endogenous variables 𝐻𝐹𝑇𝑖𝑡and 𝐿𝐹𝑟𝑎𝑔𝑖𝑡, which should be correlated with
𝐻𝐹𝑇𝑖𝑡and 𝐿𝐹𝑟𝑎𝑔𝑖𝑡but not with the error terms (𝜖𝑖𝑡), also maintain the same consistency in association with the variables over the period under study.
We choose a similar approach to Hasbrouck and Saar (2013), Degryse et al. (2015) and Gresse
(2017) where they develop instruments from the existing available measures of 𝐻𝐹𝑇𝑖𝑡and 𝐿𝐹𝑟𝑎𝑔𝑖𝑡.

5.2
A simultaneous equations model approach
We adopt now a second approach, the simultaneous equations model estimation, to tackle the possible endogeneity among the market liquidity, HFT and market fragmentation with the aim of overcoming some weakness reveled in the previous IV-GMM estimation. As explained in section 5.1,
there exist at least two possible mechanisms through which market liquidity and HFT could affect
each other: (i) the long trend of declining spread based transaction measures in the financial market
places might be attributable to the low market making cost of HFTs; and (ii) the rising competition among HFT firms through huge investment in high speed trading technology. There is also
evidence that both the level of liquidity or volatility affect the level of HFTs participation in market. On the other hand, if we consider HFT from the demand perspective, then the proliferation
of modern low-latency-based trading venues should be attributed to response by the supply side
(exchanges).
Market liquidity seems to play a role, being one of the determinants of order flow fragmentation
where we see that more liquid stocks are more fragmented. It is more likely that liquidity affects the
fragmentation decision than the other way round, though it is commonly accepted in the literature
that market fragmentation also impacts liquidity. Over the last few years, the most active channel
which affected the quoting and trading activities across markets is HFT. In this connection, the
recent responses of the supply side on the rising HFT demand have rapidly increased the number of
electronic exchanges with low-latency technology across the European equity market. It is apparent
that there are simultaneity among HFT, market fragmentation and liquidity.
To tackle the simultaneity among HFT, market fragmentation and market liquidity, we consider
simultaneous equations model which is relatively a new approach in market microstructure research.
Buti et al. (2011) and Aitken, Cumming and Zhan (2014) use similar arguments that market liquidity, fragmentation and HFT are jointly determined in equilibrium and they used simultaneous
equations model in their setting. Hasbrouck and Saar (2013) also use the same approach in a more
simple setting where an attempt has been made to determine the impact of low-latency on market
liquidity.

Conclusion
The liquidity , one of the important components of market quality, in global market places, particularly in US and European equity market, improved over the past , which many empirical papers
attribute to two important changes in recent market microstructures—HFT and trade fragmentation.
However, prior studies have considered these two astounding trends mostly unconnected, and consequently produced mixed evidence regarding their impact on liquidity. We believe, this is partly
due to insufficient empirical specifications and data limitation. So, the debate regarding the social
benefit of HFT is far from closed, and the evaluation of HFT effects is becoming more and more
complex due to its multifaceted exposures in financial marketplaces. We take the European equity
market as a laboratory since it has encountered the invasion of both HFT and market fragmentation
after its adoption of MiFID in 2007. We use a unique millisecond time stamped intraday dataset
for a considerable long period and study the joint impact of HFT and order flow fragmentation on
market liquidity. We not only incorporate HFT and market fragmentation in the same empirical
setup but also allow them to interact so that we can explore and provide evidence on their symbiotic relationship as suggested in (Menkveld, 2016).
our paper makes at least one original contribution that no papers documented before. We show
that there is an interlinkage between HFT and market fragmentation, and it determines, at least partially, the size of their individual effect on liquidity. We demonstrate that higher HFT is beneficial
for market liquidity. However, if HFT becomes more intense in highly fragmented stocks across
markets, the benefit is more. Our results suggest, market fragmentation only improves liquidity, ,
though it is mostly liquidity detrimental, when HFTs are more profound in the market. Our results
document that the recent usage of computing technology has increased the market making capability of HFTs on both sides of the order books (as liquidity demanders and providers), which reduces
both both realized spreads and adverse selection costs. As per as the quantity dimension of liquidity or market depth is concerned, HFT decreases trade sizes, and quoted depths, on the contrary,
fragmentation appears to have opposite role on quoted depth.
Apart from general impact of HFT and fragmentation on Liquidity, our results on cross-sectional
analyses show that HFT is associated with narrower spreads in both small and large stocks, even
so, the liquidity of small stocks becomes more scarce when trading are highly fragmented across
39
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3993928

exchanges. We also demonstrate that trade fragmentation becomes more liquidity harming when
HFTs are more intense on those stocks. Our study also uniquely produce a dynamic analysis where
we document that the directions of association between HFT and market fragmentation, and liquidity remain the same over time but the size of the impact is time-varying. HFT even appears to
provide liquidity in a high volatility period of financial crisis, 2008-2010 , an evidence which goes
against the popular belief. It appears that the increased trade fragmentation in the latter period of
the sample took away at least some of the additional liquidity deriving from the increased HFT in
the same period.
We have address the endogeneity but we would like to be cautious with respect to the crossmarket role of HFT (The Netherlands Authority for the Financial Markets, 2016) that could link the
order books, order flows and price behaviour across markets. O’Hara (2015) argues that traditionally employed empirical methods might no longer be appropriate to tackle this cross-market HFT
complexity. We already demonstrated the riding of HFT and order flow fragmentation on each other
through their interlinkage. There is also the possibility that fragmentation is driven by the HFT, or
a highly fragmented market attracts more HFT, and eventually both impact market quality. What
would be the more compelling in the present market order is to show how the cross-market connectivity determine market liquidity in equilibrium. An appropriate research design supplemented by
sufficient cross-market intraday data is worthy to address this issue.
In a nutshell, our findings confirm the stand of market microstructure literature with regard to
overall liquidity improving role of HFT, however it goes contrary to the liquidity improving view of
market fragmentation as documented in prior studies. We supplement both the HFT and fragmentation literature by establishing their interlinkage, identifying the sources of liquidity and expanding
the analysis across stocks and over time. The evidence provided in this paper has strong policy implications due to the rising popular concern on curving HFT. The regulators should go carefully and
consider the nonuniform cross-sectional and time variant characteristics of HFT. MiFID II has recently come out with strict HFT monitoring rules without implementing any direct measures which
seems to provide European equity market with a good device so that the regulation can trade-off
the concern of HFT for its benefit.
