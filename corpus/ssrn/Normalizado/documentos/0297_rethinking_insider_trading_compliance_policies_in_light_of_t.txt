Insider Trading and Sentiment Trading∗
Karl Ludwig Keiber†
September, 2015
Abstract
This paper studies strategic insider trading in continuous time when sentiment traders are present in the securities market. In equilibrium, we characterize both the insider’s trading strategy and the market maker’s pricing
rule. We ﬁnd that both the insider’s trading aggressiveness and the insider’s
unconditional expected proﬁts are decreasing in the informational content of
sentiment trading. The impact of the sentiment traders’ trading activity is
reported to be non-monotonic. We identify securities markets in which the
sentiment traders’ trading activity increases or decreases both the insider’s
trading aggressiveness and the insider’s expected proﬁts, respectively. The
securities market’s liquidity is found to be independent of the informativeness
of sentiment trading. But, the depth of the securities market increases in the
sentiment traders’ trading activity. Finally, sentiment trading does not aﬀect
the price discovery process adversely. We conclude that sentiment trading
does not impose any negative externality to the securities market at all. Caring about market quality, regulators should not be worried about sentiment
trading at all.
JEL classiﬁcation: G14, D82.
Keywords:
Insider trading, sentiment trading, liquidity, price discovery,
market microstructure, behavioral ﬁnance.

Introduction
Understanding the role of investor sentiment in ﬁnancial markets is crucial for ﬁnancial economists since the burgeoning of behavioral ﬁnance in the 1990s. The
relation between investor sentiment and asset returns had been focus of abundant
empirical studies, on the one hand. For instance, Neal and Wheatley (1998), Fisher
and Statman (2000), Brown and Cliﬀ(2004), Brown and Cliﬀ(2005), Baker and
Wurgler (2006), Baker and Wurgler (2007), Schmeling (2009), Baker, Wurgler and
Yuan (2012), and Huang, Jiang, Tu and Zhou (2015) study whether investor sentiment has explanatory power for the cross-section of stock returns or the aggregate
stock market. On the other hand, theoretical contributions aiming at analyzing the
eﬀects of investor sentiment in the stock market are De Long, Shleifer, Summers and
Waldmann (1990), Barberis, Shleifer and Vishny (1998), Baker and Stein (2004),
Dumas, Kurshev and Uppal (2009), and Mendel and Shleifer (2012) to name but
a few.
The present paper adds to the theoretical leg of these two strands of research on
investor sentiment in ﬁnancial markets. We do so by analyzing the relation between
insider trading and sentiment trading. To this end, we extend Kyle’s (1985) model
– a standard workhorse of market microstructure theory – by an additional group
of market participants, namely the sentiment traders. This extension allows for
deriving empirically testable hypotheses on joint insider trading and sentiment
trading.
In our model, sentiment traders as well as pure noise traders and a strategic
informed insider trade a risky asset through competitive market makers. Hence,
our approach relies on trader heterogeneity as usual in behavioral ﬁnance (e.g.
Hong and Stein (1999)).
More precisely, we capture sentiment trading by the
sentiment traders’ demand for the risky asset. This is consistent with Baker and
Wurgler (2007) who rationalize proxying investor sentiment by trading volume
besides other market-based proxies which they use in constructing an investor
sentiment index. Thus, in our model part of the order ﬂow cleared by the market

makers originates in investor sentiment and stems from sentiment traders. Our
approach is reminiscent of Mendel and Shleifer (2012) who incorporate investor
sentiment as an exogenous sentiment shock which aﬀects the sentiment traders’
demand for the risky asset immediately. In contrast to Mendel and Shleifer (2012)
who study investor sentiment in a static two-date rational expectations economy
similar to Grossman and Stiglitz (1980), we analyze sentiment trading in a dynamic
continuous-time economy with strategic insider trading in the spirit of Kyle (1985)
with gradual information release on the value of the risky asset as in Back and
Pedersen (1998) and Foucault, Hombert and Rosu (2015).
In detail, we suppose that the sentiment traders’ demand for the risky asset
consists of two parts. One part of the sentiment traders’ demand for the risky
asset is correlated with the gradually released information on the value of the risky
asset, whereas the second part of the sentiment traders’ demand is not. The latter
captures the notion that sentiment trading is not always based on fundamentals.
In particular, sentiment trading plays a hybrid role in our model. On the one hand,
it is to some extent informative for the value of the risky asset. On the other hand,
sentiment trading features a pure noise component. As regards the informational
content for the value of the risky asset, sentiment trading is right between insider
trading and noise trading.
For the sake of simplicity, we do not model a noisy public signal ﬂow on the value
of the risky asset which would be discarded by the insider anyway, interpreted by
the market makers rationally, but processed subject to some bias by the sentiment
traders.
We do so as any biased expectation of the risky asset’s value held by
the sentiment traders would result in a demand of the sentiment traders which is
correlated with the value of the risky asset to some extent, ultimately. Keeping
modeling parsimonious, we exclude this learning stage from the analysis. Anyway,
this exclusion does not prevent us from obtaining results on insider trading in the
presence of sentiment trading which is our focus.
For instance, Foucault, Hombert and Rosu (2015) incorporate a noisy public
3

signal ﬂow on the value of the risky asset which the market makers process either
upon clearing the order ﬂow (slow model) or after having cleared the order ﬂow
(fast model) in order to study the eﬀect of the insider’s speed advantage. Adding
such a signal ﬂow to our model implicitly imposes that market makers and sentiment traders have access to the same piece of public information which seems to be
quite unlikely given the abundant sources of information available to market participants on the one hand and the diﬀerent access to public information of market
professionals and laymen on the other hand.
In our model, the market makers solely learn about the value of the risky asset
from the aggregate order ﬂow. Compared to standard Kyle (1985)-type models, the
market makers’ learning form the aggregate order ﬂow is diﬀerent due to the hybrid
nature of the sentiment traders’ demand for the risky asset. As mentioned above,
the sentiment traders’ demand for the risky asset consists of both an informative
component and a pure noise component.
Therefore, the market makers do not
only learn about the value of the risky asset from the insider’s demand but to some
extent also from the sentiment traders’ demand.
The dual role of the sentiment traders’ demand for the risky asset – that is both
being informative and adding noise – gives rise to a series of issues immediately.
Will the additional noise component of sentiment trading provide the insider with
more camouﬂage vis-à-vis the market makers leading to more aggressive insider
trading? Or will the insider scale back his trading aggressiveness in order to compensate the information leaked to the market makers through sentiment trading?
Or will the informative component of sentiment trading dilute the insider’s informational advantage vis-à-vis the market makers leading to more aggressive insider
trading? Will the informative component of sentiment trading eﬀect faster price
discovery and lower liquidity of the market? Or will the informative component of
sentiment trading reduce the market makers’ adverse selection problem implying a
higher liquidity of the market? Finally, will the noise component of sentiment trading increase the liquidity of the market? We will tackle these issues thoroughly in

the course of the analysis and unveil that the interplay of strategic insider trading
and sentiment trading drives our results.
We ﬁnd that information leakage through sentiment trading aﬀects neither the
price discovery process nor market quality indicators as there are liquidity, trading
volume, or price volatility.
The rationale for these independencies is that the
strategic insider neutralizes the impact of the informational component of sentiment
trading completely. However, we ﬁnd that sentiment trading activity improves the
liquidity of the market and boosts trading volume. Hence, we substantiate Baker
and Stein’s (2004) ﬁnding that liquidity serves as indicator for the presence of
sentiment traders.
Furthermore, we show that the insider’s trading aggressiveness decreases the
more informative becomes sentiment trading. In parallel, the insider’s expected
proﬁts are aﬀected adversely by information leakage through sentiment trading.
However, the sentiment trader’s trading activity is shown to have a non-monotonic
impact on both the insider’s trading aggressiveness and the insider’s expected profits. In some market settings, more active sentiment trading makes the insider to
trade more aggressively and to reap higher expected proﬁts.
However, in markets with suﬃciently good mood of the sentiment traders combined with adequate
low sentiment trading activity, an increase of the sentiment traders’ trading activity comes along with a lower trading aggressiveness of the insider and reduced
expected proﬁts. Thus, our analysis corroborates Baker and Wurgler’s (2007) conjecture that insider trading may contain a systematic sentiment component. As
shown in our analysis, insider trading and sentiment trading are related to each
other and, hence, insider trading truly can be rationalized as proxy for investor
sentiment.

trading model of the securities market with sentiment traders in continuous time.
The equilibrium in strategic insider trading and price formation in the presence of
sentiment trading is presented in section 3. We analyze the impact of sentiment

4
Impact of sentiment trading
In this section, we study the impact of sentiment trading on both insider characteristics and market quality indicators.
Insider characteristics
Characteristics relevant to the insider are the trading aggressiveness, the unconditional expected proﬁts, and the autocorrelation of the insider’s market order. We
summarize the impact of sentiment trading on the insider’s trading aggressiveness
in proposition 1.
Proposition 1 The insider’s trading aggressiveness β (t) is always positive and
decreasing in the informational content ρ of the sentiment traders’ market order
for the innovation of the risky asset’s value. The insider’s trading aggressiveness
β (t) is decreasing (increasing) in the sentiment traders’ trading activity σ2
s if and
only if ρ > ρ∗(ρ < ρ∗). The threshold ρ∗is given in the proof.
Straightforward inspection of β (t) in theorem 1 delivers that the insider’s trading aggressiveness is decreasing in the informational content ρ of the sentiment
traders’ market order for the innovation in the risky asset’s value. At ﬁrst sight, it
is unclear whether β (t) can become negative for some positive values of ρ. If this
could be observed, then we would have an out-of-equilibrium market for the risky
12
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=2661994

asset as we imposed in (12) that β (t) is a positive valued mapping. However, the
ﬁrst part of proposition 1 reports that the insiders trading aggressiveness β (t) is
always positive. This means that the insider trades in any market with sentiment
trading. Hence, sentiment trading never becomes a perfect substitute for insider
trading even when the sentiment traders’ market order is perfectly informative for
the innovation in the value of the risky asset. The economic intuition is that even
perfectly informative sentiment trading does not crowd out insider trading. The
rationale for this observation is that the noise traders prevent the market maker
to perfectly learn from the sentiment traders’ market order. Note that dst + dut
is still a noisy signal for the innovation dvt even if ρ = 1. Because of imperfect
learning form the order ﬂow, the market maker always generates a pricing error
which is exploited by the insider.
That the insider’s trading aggressiveness β (t) is decreasing in ρ can be explained
intuitively, too. For instance, assume ρ < 0. This means that the sentiment traders
on average sell (buy) when the insider learns good (bad) news. Hence, the insider
can pick oﬀthe sentiment traders who trade on average against the news observed
by the insider. Now, if ρ increases – that is ρ becomes less negative – the insider
can pick oﬀthe sentiment traders to a lesser extent on average. Hence, the insider
reduces his trading aggressiveness when ρ increases.
Alternatively, let ρ > 0.
This means that the sentiment traders on average submit a buy (sell) order when
the insider observes good (bad) news. Thus, sentiment trading is on average in
accordance with the news learned by the insider. In this situation, the insider feels
competition from the sentiment traders. When ρ increases – that is ρ becomes more
positive – the sentiment traders’ market order becomes more informative for the
news observed by the insider, reveals more information to the market maker, and
reduces the insider’s competitive edge of his informational advantage. Therefore,
the insider reduces his trading aggressiveness in order to scale back the information
release in his market order.
In general, sentiment trading represents either a source of trading gains for the
13

insider or an alternative channel of information release to the market maker. When
this source of trading gains erodes or the information release strengthens – that is
with an increase of ρ – the insider’s optimal strategy is to trade less aggressively.
The second part of proposition 1 states the impact of the sentiment traders’
trading activity σ2
s on the insider’s trading aggressiveness β (t). As the proposition
reports, this impact is non-monotonic in general. There are markets for the risky
security in which an increase of the sentiment traders’ trading activity induces the
insider to trade less aggressively. In other markets for the risky asset the insider
responds by increasing the trading aggressiveness. This dichotomy as regards the
impact of the sentiment trader’s trading activity is depicted in ﬁgure 1. The solid
curved line in ﬁgure 1 represents the threshold ρ∗and separates all markets for
the risky asset – that is any combination of σ2
s and ρ – in regions I and II. For
markets in region I an increase of the sentiment traders’ trading activity implies
a reduction of the insider’s trading aggressiveness. In contrast, the insider trades
more aggressively in response to more active sentiment trading in markets located
in region II.
The economic intuition for this dichotomy as regards the impact of a more
active sentiment trading is as follows. First, let us focus on markets for the risky
asset beneath the dotted horizontal line in ﬁgure 1. In these markets, sentiment
traders on average submit an aggregate sell (buy) order when the insider observes
good (bad) news. Roughly, the sentiment traders on average trade on the wrong
side of the market. Hence, a higher sentiment trading activity enables the insider
to pick oﬀthe sentiment traders more aggressively.
Second, in markets for the risky asset above the dotted vertical line the insider’s
situation is diﬀerent. In those markets, sentiment traders compete with the insider
by purchasing (selling) the risky asset on average when the insider gets to know
good (bad) news. However, two opposing eﬀects are at work simultaneously when
sentiment traders become more active in those markets. On the one hand, a higher
sentiment trading activity leaks more information to the market maker what implies

that the insider scales back his trading aggressiveness. On the other hand, the
more active sentiment traders are the greater becomes the noise component in the
sentiment traders’ market order.
Provided with more camouﬂage by sentiment
noise, the insider trades more aggressively. Now, for markets with suﬃciently low
sentiment trader activity – that is markets in region I to the left of the solid concave
line – the former eﬀect dominates.
Hence, the insider trades less aggressively
when the sentiment traders’ trading activity increases. Finally, for market with
suﬃciently active sentiment traders – that is markets in region II to the right
of the solid concave line – the latter eﬀect is predominant.
Thus, more active
sentiment trading makes the insider trading with increased aggressiveness.
The previous discussion emphasizes the dual role of sentiment trading in the
market for the risky asset. Sentiment trading is an additional source of information
valuable to the market maker but also an additional source of noise to be exploited
by the insider.

faces suﬃciently high competition from the sentiment traders as regards instantaneous information revelation to the market maker, more active sentiment trading
cuts back the insider’s informational rent.

Conclusion
In this paper we studied both dynamic equilibrium insider trading in and the informational eﬃciency of the market for a risky asset in the presence of sentiment
trading. On grounds of our analysis, we conclude that sentiment trading is not
detrimental to the securities markets as a whole.
The relevant market quality
characteristics had been shown to be either unaﬀected by or improve in the informational content of sentiment trading or the sentiment traders’ trading activity.
In particular, the liquidity of the securities market and trading volume increase
through more active sentiment trading whereas the informational eﬃciency of the
securities market studied along the price discovery process remains unaﬀected.
We conclude that strategic insider trading must be recognized as means eliminating any defective eﬀects of sentiment trading. For instance, when sentiment
traders on average trade on the wrong side of the market, become more active and
thereby hamper the informational eﬃciency of the securities market – although
the liquidity of the securities market increases – it is the insider who improves the
price discovery process by trading away the deﬁciencies of sentiment trading in
price discovery. Thus, regulators should not be concerned about insider trading
excessively but should appreciate insider trading from the perspective of informational eﬃciency.
Baker and Wurgler (2006) report that investor sentiment is relevant to securities whose valuations are highly subjective and diﬃcult to arbitrage. These are
small stocks, young stocks, high volatility stocks, unproﬁtable stocks, non-dividendpaying stocks, extreme growth stocks, and distressed stocks. If investor sentiment
aﬀects theses securities then one naturally expects sentiment trading activity in
23
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=2661994

those stocks.
We refer to these stocks as sentiment-prone stocks compared to
stocks outside this category.
Our integrated analysis of strategic insider trading and sentiment trading brought
forth a series of empirically testable implications in the cross-section of risky assets. Among sentiment-prone stocks, stocks with suﬃciently low (high) sentiment
trading are expected to exhibit both lower (higher) insider trading aggressiveness
and lower (higher) proﬁtability of insider trading (hypothesis 1).
Additionally,
the more sentiment trading is aligned on average with the fundamental value the
lower should be both insider trading aggressiveness and insider trading proﬁtability (hypothesis 2). Another hypothesis can be formulated from the ﬁnding that
an increase of the sentiment traders’ trading activity comes along with a higher
liquidity of the securities market. Hence, one could test whether stocks with high
sentiment trading activity feature lower bid-ask spreads or a smaller price impact
compared to stocks with low sentiment trading activity (hypothesis 3).
On theoretical grounds one could study a variant of the proposed model by assuming that the informational content of the sentiment traders’ aggregate demand
for the risky asset improves throughout the trading period. This would capture
a group learning eﬀects from implicit coordination of the sentiment traders. We
leave this issue for future research.
