Substitute Trading
and
the Eﬀectiveness of Insider Trading Regulations
Abstract
US securities laws prohibit insiders from using “inside” information to trade their
own stocks. But it is not illegal for insiders to trade related stocks, such as stocks of
their ﬁrm’s competitors, suppliers, and customers. Borrowing from the law literature
(Ayres and Bankman, 2001), we call insiders’ trading in related stocks “substitute
trading”. This paper studies the eﬀectiveness of insider trading laws in the presence of substitute trading. We extend Kyle’s (1985) model to a (static) model with
two correlated ﬁrms, each with its own insider. We consider two diﬀerent trading
environments, an opaque one where the market maker observes only the order ﬂow
of the asset he makes the market in, and a transparent one where he also observes
the order ﬂow of the other (correlated) stock. We ﬁnd that in transparent markets,
insider trading regulations do limit insiders’ ability to proﬁt from inside information.
However, in opaque markets, the regulations may actually increase insiders’ proﬁts.
JEL Classiﬁcation: G14, G18.
Keywords: Substitute Trading, Stocks of Related Firms, Insider Trading Regulations.

Introduction
There have been extensive discussions in ﬁnance, economics, and law about whether insider
trading should be regulated, that is, whether insiders (primarily executives, board members,
oﬃcers, and controlling shareholders) should be prohibited from using inside information
to trade the stocks of their own ﬁrms1. Almost exclusively, these discussions are made
under the assumption that other than trading their own stocks (e.g., Kyle, 1985; Glosten,
1989 ) and/or manipulating information (e.g., Benabou and Laroque, 1992), insiders cannot
beneﬁt from their private information. However, insiders can proﬁt by trading the stocks of
related ﬁrms and it is legal for them to conduct such trade. This paper contributes to the
literature in two ways. To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst paper to present a formal study
of the eﬀectiveness of insider trading regulations when insiders are able to trade the stocks
of related ﬁrms. It also adds to the market microstructure literature a detailed study of
the strategic trading behavior of insiders when they can trade their own stocks as well as
related stocks.
Section 10(b) of the 1934 Securities Exchange Act and the “misappropriation” doctrine
clearly prohibit insiders from using private information to trade the stocks of their own
ﬁrms. They do not, however, prohibit trading the stocks of related ﬁrms, such as competitors, suppliers and customers.2 This raises the possibility that insiders of one ﬁrm who
possess non-public information relevant to both their own ﬁrm as well as related ﬁrms might
be able to trade on this information proﬁtably in the related ﬁrms’ stocks. Borrowing from
the law literature (Ayres and Bankman, 2001), we call this “substitute trading”. How effective are insider trading regulations in the presence of substitute trading3? That is, when

To address these questions, we compare the cases with and without insider trading
regulations in the presence of substitute trading. Existing papers on insider trading assume
one risky asset in the market. Here, we extend Kyle’s (1985) static model to one with
two correlated risky stocks, where the correlation could be positive or negative. There
is one insider for each ﬁrm who can legally trade the other stock. An insider has precise
information about his own ﬁrm and can therefore make inferences, though imperfect, about
the other ﬁrm. Liquidity trading in the two assets is exogenous, and there is one competitive
market maker for each asset. The strategic trading behavior of insiders is studied under
two diﬀerent trading environments, one opaque and the other transparent. In the ﬁrst, the
market maker observes only the order ﬂow of the asset he makes the market in, whereas in
the second, the market maker also observes the order ﬂow of the other (correlated) stock.
There is one trading round and the trading mechanism resembles that in Kyle (1985).
The distinction between the trading environments is motivated by some facts about
stock exchanges. In the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), each stock is handled by a
single specialist. A specialist may represent one heavily traded stock and one less active
stock, or several stocks across a range of industries. The trading of two correlated stocks
could be conducted by two specialists, or by the same specialist. Moreover, related stocks
are sometimes traded in diﬀerent exchanges. For example, Dell is traded on the NASDAQ
while Hewlett-Packard (Compaq) is traded on the Big Board. The marker makers/dealers
of these two stocks cannot see each other’s order ﬂows instantaneously, reﬂecting the opaque
trading environment in this paper.4 On the other hand, with the development of modern

The main results are as follows. In an opaque trading environment, insider trading
regulations do not always reduce the aggregate proﬁts that can be made by the two insiders.
If insider trading is not regulated, insiders trade both stocks in the same direction as their
information (buy on positive information and sell otherwise). This could mean that the
insider will buy his own stock and sell the other if the two stocks are negatively correlated
and he possesses positive information about his own stock. If insider trading is regulated,
however, insiders can only do substitute trading. The regulations do limit the aggregate
proﬁts that can be made by the two insiders if the two ﬁrms are not closely correlated.
However, if the two ﬁrms are closely correlated, the regulations could in fact increase
the aggregate proﬁts the two insiders make. This is because when two ﬁrms are closely
correlated, the two insiders will compete against each other for almost the same piece
of information if there are no regulations.
With regulations, each insider becomes an
information monopolist in the other ﬁrm’s stock and makes more proﬁt.5
In contrast, in a transparent environment, insider trading regulations always limit the
insiders’ ability to proﬁt from their information. If insider trading is not regulated, insiders
will deﬁnitely trade their own stocks in the same direction as their information. However,
market makers possess information about order ﬂows not available to the investors instantaneously, including arbitrageurs. For example, on the NYSE, before the introduction of OpenBook service on January
24, 2002, only the specialist had access to an immediate electronic display of the limit order book beyond
the best quotes. After January 24, 2002, even though OpenBook shows the aggregate limit-order volume
available in the NYSE Display Book system at each price point, it is updated every 10 seconds and does
not include the specialist’s proprietary trading interest or ﬂoor broker interest.

However, the insider of the larger camouﬂage stock ﬁnds it optimal to trade the substitute

hence create more room for the insider to proﬁt from trading his own stock. If, on the
other hand, insider trading is regulated, only substitute trading happens. Comparing the
equilibria with and without regulations, we ﬁnd that insider trading regulations are eﬀective
in the transparent environment. The aggregate proﬁts of the insiders are always reduced
by the regulations.
In this paper we predict that substitute trading will be observed in both types of
markets. Tookes (2006) provides empirical evidence of intra-industry substitute trading,
which she calls “competitor trading”, in the NYSE and AMEX. We also predict that
substitute trading is not always in the same direction as the insiders’ information. If two
correlated stocks are represented by diﬀerent specialists in the NYSE, or traded in diﬀerent
exchanges, we will observe substitute trading in the direction of the information. However,
if the two correlated stocks are represented by the same specialist in the NYSE, or traded
in transparent exchanges, we will observe some informed traders trading substitutes against
their information. To our knowledge, no empirical study addresses these predictions.
This paper makes a signiﬁcant contribution to the literature in two ways. Firstly, to our
knowledge, this is the ﬁrst paper that formally studies the eﬀectiveness of insider trading
regulations by taking substitute trading into account. There is a large literature on the pros
and cons of insider trading regulations. Proponents of insider trading regulations suggest
that insider trading may reduce a ﬁrm’s value (e.g., Manove, 1989; Bebchuk and Jolls,
1999 ). They argue that insider trading also decreases price informativeness (e.g., Ausubel,
1990; Fishman and Hagerty, 1992) and market liquidity (e.g., Glosten, 1989; Leland, 1992;
5

and many others). Opponents show that insider trading may reduce the conﬂict of interest
between managers and shareholders (e.g., Manne, 1966; Hu and Noe, 2001). They also
argue that it facilitates the process of incorporating information into prices so that prices
are closer to fundamental values (e.g., Carlton and Fischel, 1983; Bernhardt, Holliﬁeld
and Hughson, 1995 ). Further, Bhattacharya and Nicodano (2001) suggest that insider
trading may beneﬁt outsiders with stochastic liquidity needs.6
However, none of these
studies consider substitute trading and its impact on the eﬀectiveness of insider trading
regulations. This is the purpose of our paper. We show that the regulations may actually
be harmful in opaque markets when the correlation between related stocks is high, but they
are eﬀective in transparent markets.
Secondly, this paper studies strategic trading by insiders when they can also trade in
substitute stocks, in opaque as well as transparent markets. Caball´e and Khrisnan (1994)
also use a Kyle-type model to study imperfect competition among risk-neutral informed
traders in a multi-security transparent market where fundamentals are correlated. However, this paper diﬀers in several ways.
First, they do not address the eﬀectiveness of
insider trading regulations. Second, they do not study informed traders’ strategic trading in opaque markets. Finally, their analysis of strategic trading in transparent markets
cannot be applied in our environment because they implicitly assume that each trader receives at least one diﬀerent signal (not from the correlation) on every asset. Technically,
this assumption ensures that the variance-covariance matrix of an informed trader’s best
guess of the fundamentals is nonsingular.7 In this paper, insiders naturally know private
information about their own ﬁrms but make inferences about correlated ﬁrms; that is, the
number of signals each insider has is less than the number of securities in the market.

As previously mentioned, most of the traditional literature on insider trading allows
only one risky asset. A few papers (e.g. Huddart, Hughes and Brunnermeier, 1999) employ
multi-asset models to examine voluntary disclosure policies by insiders, but the ﬁrms are
independent in these models. Easley, O’Hara and Srinivas (1998) model informed traders’
decisions whether to trade options or the underlying stocks. In their model the assets are
correlated, but the informed traders are competitive and cannot trade options and stocks
simultaneously.

trading in their own stocks as well as in substitute stocks, when the market is opaque.
Comparisons of insiders’ proﬁts, price informativeness, and market liquidity before and
after introducing insider trading regulations are provided. Section 3 studies the transparent
market. Section 4 concludes and discusses future research. All proofs are provided in the

The Transparent Market
The models in this section are similar to those in Section 2 except that the market is
transparent in the sense that the market makers can see the total order ﬂows for both
assets, or equivalently, one market maker makes the market for both assets.13 Since the two
ﬁrms are correlated, the market maker can infer more information about the fundamental
values of the two assets from the total demands for both assets.

From Proposition 5, we ﬁrst observe that β11 and β22 are always positive which suggest
that the insiders always trade their own stocks according to their private information.
But, very interestingly, the signs of β12 and β21 depend on the relative size of σσu – the
camouﬂage the insider can use to hide his trading. When the two ﬁrms have the same
level of camouﬂage, β12 = β21 = 0. That is, the insiders choose not to trade the substitute
stocks. This is surprising since substitute trading can a priori also be proﬁtable. More
strikingly, when σ1σu1 > σ2σu2, β12 < 0 and β21 > 0; when σ1σu1 < σ2σu2, β12 > 0 and
β21 < 0. That is, the insider with the larger camouﬂage (σσu) trades the substitute stock
against his private information, while the insider with the smaller σσu trades the substitute
stock in the direction of his information.
The seemingly counter-intuitive trading of the substitute comes from the strategic competition between the players. The beneﬁt of competing in the substitute stock is to sneak
into the other market and get more proﬁts since the two ﬁrms are correlated and substitute
trading is one form of informed trading. However, substitute trading in the direction of
private information may reveal so much about the fundamental values of the assets that
the prices of their own stocks will be very accurate and thus the insiders make less proﬁts
from trading their own stocks. Therefore, it may not be worth competing in the substitute
stock, or the insiders may be even better oﬀby trading the substitute against their private
information to “confuse” the market maker.
More speciﬁcally, if the two ﬁrms have the same level of camouﬂage, the insider ﬁnds it
optimal not to trade the other stock since it has the same camouﬂage as his own stock but
he has better information about his own stock. Trading the other stock without reducing
20

his trade in his own stock will only reveal too much of his private information to the market
maker. So he is not interested in trading the substitute.
If one ﬁrm has larger camouﬂage, for example, ﬁrm 1 has larger camouﬂage than ﬁrm 2,
it is obvious that Insider 2 would not trade stock 1 against his information since informed
trading is better “camouﬂaged” in the market of stock 1. Therefore Insider 2’s trade in
stock 1 has a small impact on the price of his own stock. For Insider 1, trading stock 2
according to his private information will cause the price of his own stock to be much closer
to the true value, since the market maker can better infer the true value of stock 1 by
looking at the total demand of the less camouﬂaged stock 2. Therefore it is optimal for
Insider 1 to trade against his information in stock 2 in order to “mislead” the market maker
and thus protect the proﬁt from trading his own stock.
From Proposition 5, we also see that the total order ﬂows of both assets are used in
both pricing rules. Just as in the regulated case, the self price pressure is always positive
and the cross-asset price pressure depends on the correlation of the assets. However, one
interesting feature here is that cross-asset price pressures are the same for the order ﬂows of
both assets, λ12 = λ21. Caball´e and Khrisnan (1994) also ﬁnd the matrix of cross-asset price
pressures to be symmetric for unregulated informed trading in their setting. This probably
is because both insiders are more aggressive in the asset with the larger camouﬂage and
less aggressive in the one with smaller camouﬂage. Thus the net information the market
maker infers from the order ﬂow of one asset for the other asset is the same across the
assets. With regulated insider trading, the cross-asset price pressures are diﬀerent for the
insiders. The reason for the diﬀerent pattern of cross-asset price pressures in unregulated
and regulated markets may lie in the insiders’ diﬀerent treatment of order ﬂows’ feedback
eﬀect on the prices of their own assets. When insider trading is unregulated, the insiders
have to balance the feedback eﬀect with the self price pressure. When insider trading is
regulated, the insiders do not care about the feedback eﬀect and thus the cross-asset price
pressures are diﬀerent.

Conclusion and Discussion
US securities laws prohibit insiders from trading their own stocks based on their private
information. However, insiders can make substantial proﬁts through a legal use of their
information – substitute trading. Through studying the eﬀectiveness of insider trading
regulations taking substitute trading into account, we have established that insider trading
regulations are eﬀective in most cases.
We use a static model with two correlated ﬁrms in the spirit of Kyle (1985). There
is one insider for each ﬁrm. Insiders’ strategic trading behavior in their own stocks and
substitutes are studied in both opaque and transparent markets. We ﬁnd that in opaque
markets, an insider chooses to trade both his own stock and the substitute in the same
direction as his private information. In transparent markets, the insider with smaller camouﬂage always trades his own stock and the substitute stock in the same direction as his
information; but the insider with larger camouﬂage trades the substitute stock against his
private information. If the two ﬁrms have the same level of camouﬂage, the insiders choose
not to trade substitute stocks.
The eﬀectiveness of insider trading regulations are examined by applying three criteria:
26

the insiders’ aggregate expected proﬁts, price informativeness, and market liquidity. The
most important criterion to focus on is proﬁt. In opaque markets, if the ﬁrms are not closely
correlated, the regulations reduce insiders’ aggregate proﬁts, but if the ﬁrms are closely
correlated, the regulations may increase insiders’ aggregate proﬁts; while in transparent
markets the regulations always reduce insiders’ aggregate proﬁts. A further criterion is
price informativeness. Market prices are always less informative with regulations in opaque
markets; while in transparent markets the regulations do not aﬀect price informativeness.
Finally, market liquidity is considered.
The regulations cause the markets to be more
liquid in most cases in both types of markets. However, market liquidity may decrease
with regulations when the two ﬁrms are highly correlated.
Our analysis suggests that
transparent markets may be a better environment from the view of regulating insider
trading.
There are a few issues not discussed in this paper. Firstly, we assume insider trading
regulations can be perfectly enforced without costs. Therefore, the optimal enforcement of
regulations studied in DeMarzo, Fishman and Hagerty (1998) is not considered in this paper. Secondly, in the static model studied here, the insiders can only use their information
to “passively” trade in the markets. But with substitute trading being legal, insiders could
deliberately disclose information about their own ﬁrms and buy/sell shares of substitute
stocks. Prices of substitute stocks will be strongly aﬀected by the deliberate disclosure so
that it may be possible for the insiders to make substantial proﬁts from legal substitute
trading. We would need a dynamic model to study this behavior. Thirdly, we assume
that the insiders’ managerial compensation is not aﬀected by the proﬁts from insider and
substitute trading. Shareholders and managers may deal with substitute trading by contracting on the proﬁts from such trading in the stock markets in order to reduce agency
costs. Moreover, the noise traders in this paper do not have any discretion to choose which
stock to trade. If the noise traders could choose an asset with a lower trading cost, there
would be no noise traders for the other asset. But as in all the other Kyle-type models,
noise traders are needed for the markets of the two assets not to break down. One could
add a fourth type of trader who comes to the market for liquidity reasons but with the
discretion to choose which asset to trade. This may change the insiders’ trading strategies.
27

These questions can be addressed in more complex models and are left for future research.
