High Frequency Trading, Market Volatility and Trading Counterparty
Performance
P. Joakim Westerholm 1
Abstract
The ﬁndings in this paper conﬁrm that there is an economic and statistic negative association
between High Frequency Trading [HFT] activity and price volatility. There is however an indication
of an increased volatility in the ultra-high frequency intervals around HFT. This paper also conﬁrms
that large market dominating high frequency traders make consistently proﬁtable trades intra-day,
but their performance is also highly impacted by the overnight positions they hold. Identifying the
counterparties of high frequency trades is important to fully understand the impact of HFT. The
counterparties of HFT are in this paper found to endure signiﬁcant and consistent short term costs.
Most counterparties are within the category of HFT, other common counterparties are buy side
institutional investors, and ﬁnancial ﬁrms, typically not households. The bulk or the large short
term returns earned by the largest HFT ﬁrms are generated from trading with other smaller HFTs
and slower intermediaries.
I suggest that the relatively moderate costs suﬀered by institutional
counterparties to HFT may be justiﬁed as a compensation for liquidity provision and fast execution.
keywords: High Frequency Trading, Realized Volatility, Trader Welfare

Introduction
The aim of this paper is to investigate if there is any evidence of a common perception that there
is an association between High Frequency Trading [HFT] activity and market volatility that needs
to be regulated. This paper also aims to determine the impact of HFT on the performance of the
counterparties they trade with, as these counterparties is the immediate source of the allegedly
consistent proﬁts generated by HFT.
The emergence of HFT as a fundamental driver of trading activity on ﬁnancial markets is at
the center of the current regulatory discussion. Since HFT driven marketable orders are expected
to improve liquidity through the magnitude of trading volume that algorithmic strategies infuse
(Hendershott, 2011), low frequency traders and regulatory bodies may be willing to accept HFT
participation in modern ﬁnancial markets, provided that it can be shown that HFT is not negatively
aﬀecting the several dimensions of market quality. Industry participants however, particularly large
buy side investors have since the credit crisis of 2007 and 2008 been concerned with a change in
the way todays fragmented stock markets behave. Today investors, particularly when placing large
trades, have to be very careful with the way orders are routed to the market, or the orders may end
up executing at less favorable price than expected, or face an implementation shortfall when faster
computerized trading engines preempt the traders moves (see e.g. Lewis (2014)). Large institutions
are well resourced and should be able to counteract any market development that is adverse to their
activities, and consequently the major concern by regulators should be the impact of HFT on market
quality for households directly and through their retirement savings and insurance companies who
invest funds on their behalf. The bulk of the research ﬁnd that HFT is overall good for market quality
(see Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld, 2011, Hagstromer, Norden, 2013, Brogaard, Hendershott

Malinova and Park (2015) and Malinova, Park and Riordan, 2016). Some studies ﬁnd results that

Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld, (2011) speciﬁcally suggest that the ﬁnancial crisis of 2007/2008

extreme volatility. Brogaard, Hendershott and Riordan (2014), ﬁnd that HFTs overall trade in the

during a period of relative market turbulence (2008-2009). Du and Zhu (2014) propose a theoretical

contribute to or take away from market welfare. They conclude If all traders have the same speed,
the socially optimal trading frequency tends to be low for scheduled arrivals of information but high
for stochastic arrivals of information. If traders have heterogeneous trading speeds, fast traders
prefer the highest feasible trading frequency, whereas slow traders tend to prefer a strictly lower
frequency p. 1. Their results indicate that the welfare consequences of HFT may vary between high
and low information periods.
This paper contributes to the literature by addressing the impact of HFT on volatility during the
ﬁnancial crisis of 2007/2008 when liqudity provision was particularly important for the liquidity and
volatility of the capital markets. Second, having access to highly detailed market wide data on high
frequency trading this paper is able to verify or contradict previous HFT supportive results from
certain market segments. Third this paper aims to investigate the welfare eﬀects of HFT on the
counterparties they trade with and to contrast high and low information periods. Finally this paper

Institutional Setting
The institutional setting at the NASDAQ OMX Helsinki stock exchange [OMXH] is similar to
other European (and Asian) exchanges where trading is conducted electronically in a central limit
order book with no designated liquidity suppliers in any major stock issues and since 2006 trading
broker identity is pre-trade anonymous, trading opens with an auction at 10 am and closes after
a long post trading period that ends at 6.30 pm with a randomized closing auction. The market
has allows oﬀ-market crossings that are reported immediately for smaller trades compared to the
average daily turnover, while larger blocks can be reported with delay. Trade halting principles,
order size scales and Average Daily Turnover are deﬁned in accordance with European directives in
MiFID (2004/39/EC). The market applies dynamic volatility guards applied using liquidity bands.
The minimum tick-size is 0.01 for the sample large cap stocks and investigated period October 1,
2007 to September 30, 2009, price dependent ticks with three decimals for small price stocks were
introduced in January 2010.
The Helsinki market has become a signiﬁcant part of the global portfolio despite its relatively
small size. OMXH is home to well known companies in the technology sector, and their presence may
also have alerted international investors to the other large companies at the exchange, typically in
the industries engineering, forestry and resources. During the period of study, foreign investors held
on average 61% of the market capitalization of the exchange, which was equivalent to approximately

Methodology
4.1
Empirical Methodology
4.1.1
Deﬁnition of HFT activity
HFTs conduct trading operations through a hyper-active computer algorithmic based strategy,
whereby traders buy and sell stocks based on extremely short holding periods with the aim of
capturing micro proﬁts. Along with the algorithmic nature of HFT strategies, other characteristics
that deﬁne their behavior is a tendency to hold low net positions by the end of trading days, and
their role as net liquidity providers in equity markets. A prime limitation in analyzing the HFT is
the lack of a universal deﬁnition to dichotomize HFT market participants from non-HFT partici8
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=2826621

pants. To diﬀerentiate and classify trading accounts the Investor classiﬁcation model developed by
Kirilenko et al. (2011) is employed. Investors are deﬁned by the actual transactions they execute
and how they operate daily on the exchange. This method is based on an inventory versus turnover
analysis as opposed to a traditional trade-based prescription to deﬁne Investors. By applying this
model to the data it is possible to analyze how diﬀerent categories of investors operate across the
time period. The shortcomings of the model are addressed through a comparison with previous HFT
proxies in the literature.
4.1.2
Investor Classiﬁcation Model
Kirilenko et al. (2011) successfully apply an inventory versus turnover Investor Classiﬁcation model
to deﬁne and describe traders on the US S&P E-Mini Future Contract market in the period surrounding the 2010 Flash Crash. This method deﬁnes Investors as HFT or non-HFT based on their
trading behavior, particularly their daily net holding position in the instrument, the level of activity
on the market in terms of value traded, and the quantity of transactions executed in which the
Investor is party to. The central supposition of the model is that ﬁnancial exchange markets facilitate a platform for traders with diﬀerent holding horizons and trading strategies to interact. For
example, large institutional investors seeking to attain a signiﬁcant stake in a company will generally
accumulate a large buy position over a long period of time. In contrast, other traders will seek to
maneuver their trading strategy throughout the day to keep their net position to a minimal value
whilst trading a high volume of stock. Other investors may utilize both strategies across diﬀerent
periods. To apply the Investor Classiﬁcation model and determine what constitutes HFT activity I
process the original transaction-level data to identify attributes of particular trading accounts. The
initial dataset of over 51 million transactions includes information on all individual trades based
on the following data ﬁelds
company international security code (ISIN), date, time (to nearest

ID), buyer or seller initiator indicator, price and volume. These ﬁelds enable us to manipulate the
data to calculate each accounts tick-by-tick net holding and total trading positions throughout the
day, and determine the transactions that the account initiated. Standard transaction datasets such
as the one used in this paper lack a discernible high frequency trader classiﬁcation system, which
justiﬁes the application of Kirilenko et al. (2011) framework to classify accounts into the following
trader categories Intermediaries, High Frequency Traders, Fundamental Buyers, Fundamental Sellers, Small Traders and Opportunistic Traders. These investors are classiﬁed based on the following
characteristics
1) Intermediaries (Int)
are very short horizon investors who buy and sell a large volume of
securities, but stay around a relatively low target level of inventory. So, their end of day net position
is no more than 5% of the value of daily trading transactions in which they are involved. These
investors hold a very small position when the markets close whilst participating in a large volume
of intra-day trading. Intuitively, it could be argued that these traders have signiﬁcantly short-term
investment horizons, and generally net out a large majority of their positions by the end of the
trading day. 2) High-Frequency Traders (HFT) can be identiﬁed as a subset of Intermediaries and
represent the top 7% of trading accounts when ranked by the number of daily transactions in which
they are involved. I assess the impact of classifying using diﬀerent percentages of to accounts by
daily transactions, but the 7% threshold isolates a distinct group of trading accounts. Results are
not signiﬁcantly aﬀected by changes in this percentage. Essentially, these accounts are the most
active or High-Frequency Intermediaries in the market. This cut-oﬀlevel has been calculated to
designate HFT accounts that are signiﬁcantly diﬀerent in the magnitude of trading activity prevalent
in Intermediaries. Once an account is designated as HFT it is removed from the Intermediary set.

4.1.3
Robustness of the investor classiﬁcation method
While Kirilenko et als (2011) framework represents a potentially powerful method of classifying
trading accounts, there are limitations in applying the model to the Finnish dataset used in this
paper. This study applies the model (originally designed for one trading day and one instrument)
across all a sample of large cap equity securities and individually for each of the 502 trading days
analyzed.
Hence, accounts are classiﬁed uniquely each trading day with accounts able to shift
between categories inter-day depending on how they behave on any given day and the analysis is
conducted using observations from those days only when a trader is actively trading according to a
HFT strategy.
HFT activity over the sample period from 2008 October to 2009 September is prevalent in 33% of
average monthly trades by number of trades and by value. This measure oscillates between minimum
and maximum levels of 20-45% across the dataset. These results are in-line with expectations and
the pervading academic literature. Jarnecic and Snape (2014) ﬁnd that between 40-64% of trades
executed on the London Stock Exchange (LSE) in 2009 were conducted by HFT ﬁrms. Furthermore,
a report conducted by the CESR (2010) presented an estimate that HFT have a market share of
between 25-35% of the activity on the LSE in the ﬁrst quarter of 2010. Even if these levels are
signiﬁcantly diﬀerent those seen in the US, where HFTs currently participate in up to 92% of trades
(Ito, 2012), the fragmented nature of the European market in terms of clearing, settlement and
post-trade services may account for these diﬀerence.
The results attained in this study appear
reasonable in comparison to previous research and indicate that the Kirilenko et als (2011) investor
classiﬁcation model can be applied dynamically across time periods to equities markets that list a
large universe of securities.

4.4.3
What is the dynamic association between short term volatility and HFT?
As the counterparties to HFT are not expected to be able to react to the actions of HFT within one
second our main results are generated in a longer time interval, where a robust model estimation
framework can be created. A generally applied interval for measuring realized volatility is 5 minute
interveals (Andersen et.al, 2001). I continue to investigate the association between volatility and
HFT participation at 5 minute intervals incluing up to ﬁve lags, which covers another commonly
used time time interval 30 minutes. I perform robustness checks to verify the results for stocks that
do not trade during all these intervals.
Table 5 shows that for the two 5 minutes intervals leading a trade the realized volatility is
sigfnicantly lower with higher HFT participation, indicating that HFT presense does contribute to
liduidity and lower short term volatility. The sign then reverses and ﬁve periods (30 minutes) prior
to a trade is followed by higher realized volatility.
The ﬁrst leading period t-1 higher realized volatility is associated to lower HFT participation,
indicating that in the short run HFTs avoid increases in volatility. This corrobarates the results in
the short term second by second analysis in Table 4 above.

4.4.4
Counterparty Welfare
Before Virtu Financial, a large high frequency trading and market making ﬁrm, listed on Nasdaq in
April 16, 2015 they reported for the ﬁrst time oﬃcial records of trading proﬁts generated by HFT.
The ﬁrm showed the enormous potential of automated fast market making and reported virtually
no losses and instead showed continuous signiﬁcant proﬁts for each day of trading. This astounded
other market participants and demonstrated the value of having technological advantage in ﬁnancial
markets. These kind of results also raise the question, who are the counterparties that HFT ﬁrms
trade with and are they the source of these consistent proﬁts? In such analysis it is important to bear
in mind that the incentives for the trades of the counterparties to HFT may be entirely diﬀerent, that
and they may also beneﬁt from the liquidity provided by HFT in creating long term positions that
may produce the intended outcomes of their investment strategy in the long run. The counterparties
that take the opposite side of HFT will be many and not easy to categorize. It is of great interest to
do so, as these counterparties are the perceived sources of the allegedly consistent proﬁts generated
by HFT. I ﬁrst investigate the proﬁts of HFT ﬁrms and ﬁnd persistent, economically and statistically
signiﬁcant HFT proﬁts. This conﬁrms the results of Brogaard, Hagstromer, Norden and Riordan,
2015, who ﬁnd that the proﬁt HFT generate are signiﬁcant, however dependent of their ability and
willingness to invest in high speed of access to the market. More importantly I also ﬁnd that the
counterparties of these trades suﬀer consistent short term losses, which indicates that while they
may have diﬀerent more long term incentives to trade than their HFT counterparties, there must

be more optimal ways of entering and exiting the market for these counterparties to HFT.
Table 7 reports aggregate statistics for how the counterparty investor categories are distributed
across diﬀerent investor categories. Attention here I use the full sample period January 1, 2007 to
December 31, 2009, to include a larger proportion of a market cycle.3 This analysis shows that
the most common counterparty to HFT are other high frequency trading ﬁrms, however the second

Robustness Check
Volatility has many determinants. The rich literature on volatlity does not however provide high
frequency determinants for volatility, and as mosst of these determinants are macro economy related
they are measured on a monthly or lower frequency. In a robustness check well known determinants of
volatility are applied in a tradiitonal volatility model to estimate the relation of HFT participation
to long-run volatility, measured as the monthly standard deviation in returns. The variables are
described in Appendix table A1 and the results are presented in Appendix table A2.
Given that only 24 monthly observations are available the model utilizes the GMM method in
order to better meet the assumptions posed by OLS regression models. In order to account for
statistical limitations, I follow the volatility literature and estimate conditional volatility measures
at the monthly frequency by ﬁtting an EWMA model to market volatility and a GARCH (1,1) model
to the Market-level, Cyclical and Macroeconomic variables and this way obtain monthly observations
of annualized volatility for each variable. As the purpose is to control for the most important drivers
of volatility the combination of control variables that explains volatility with the highest signiﬁcance
and r square are selected. Equation 7 is estimated with the dependent variable conditional market

Conclusions
This paper has shown that while HFT participation is associated with lower volatility in the intermediate and long run, there is an indication that in the ultra short interval where algortithms
operate, they do contribute to volatility. During the trading day the counterparties that suﬀer must
from the negative impact of HFT are other slower HFTs and intermediaries. Fundamental buy-side
investors appear impacted only the day after the trade and possibly beyond. Households appear
little impacted as their holding periods and investment horizons are generally longer and any minor
increase in short term transaction costs have little impact on the present value of their future cash
ﬂows from their investments.
