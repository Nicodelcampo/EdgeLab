Mechanical properties of hybrid glass fibre-reinforced polymer and timber 
(HFT) laminates and a new failure theory
Chuang Miaoa, Dilum Fernandob, *, Darshana Rathnayaka Mudiyanselageb, Shishun Zhangc, 
Marcelo Diasb, Michael Heitzmanna
aSchool of Civil Engineering, The University of Queensland, St Lucia, QLD 4072, Australia

ABSTRACT
Hybrid glass fibre reinforced polymer (GFRP)-timber (HFT) laminated thin-walled structural 
members are a new low carbon composite technology which has shown great potential to be used in 
load bearing structural members. Existing studies have shown that HFT laminates exhibit complex 
behaviour under different loading, and existing numerical modelling approaches using well-known 
failure theories to model the behaviour of laminae (or laminate) cannot capture the true behaviour of 
HFT laminates. While micro or meso scale approaches may provide a pathway to capturing the 
complex behaviour of HFT laminates, such modelling approaches become computationally 
demanding when modelling large scale structures. This paper presents an experimental investigation 
on the behaviour of HFT laminates under different loading conditions and a new failure theory to 
accurately capture the constitutive behaviour of the HFT laminates. Data generated through 
experimental investigations as well as through a numerical approach were compared with most 
commonly used existing failure theories demonstrating the shortcomings of them to capture the 
behaviour of HFT laminates. Based on the generated data, a new failure theory consisting of a new 
failure initiation criterion and a fracture energy based failure evolution law is proposed. The new 
failure theory was implemented in finite element (FE) software ABAQUS using a vectorized user 
material subroutine (VUMAT). FE results agreed well with the experimental results. 
Keywords: HFT laminates; Mechanical properties; Finite element modelling; Failure theory
1. Introduction 
As a result of the climate emergency, construction engineers are constantly faced with the challenge 
to find low carbon technologies for replacing existing high carbon technologies. Hybrid glass fibrereinforced polymer (GFRP) and timber (HFT) laminated thin-walled structures (Fig. 1) is a novel 
technology recently developed as a sustainable alternative to thin-walled steel and aluminium 
structures [1,2].

The HFT laminate consists of timber veneers and GFRP laminae, where grains in timber layers are 
kept parallel to member axis and fibres of the GFRP layers are kept predominantly perpendicular to 
timber grain direction (Fig. 1) [1]. Most of the HFT laminates use very low amount of GFRP (<12% 
by volume), and manufacturing of HFT laminates structures is low energy intensive. Therefore, HFT 
laminated structures are expected to results in minimal CO2 emissions, especially compared to 
metallic alternatives [1]. Several studies have been conducted on demonstrating the potential of thinwalled structural members made of HFT laminates as a sustainable alternative to thin-walled steel 
and aluminium structures [1–6]. These studies have clearly demonstrated the excellent performance 
of HFT structures as load carrying elements, and their higher weight specific load capacity than thinwalled steel structures [1,5,6]. However, research on HFT structures is still at an early stage and much 
more efforts are required to better understand the behaviour of HFT structural members under 
different loading and boundary conditions. 
While experimental methods are essential in investigating the behaviour of HFT structural members 
and developing and validating the design theories, they are expensive and time consuming thus cannot 
be used as the sole method in investigating the behaviour of HFT structural members. Development 
of a suitable numerical modelling approach to capture the behaviour of HFT laminates and HFT 
structural members is necessary to enable the use of HFT structural members. Existing studies have 
shown that behaviour of HFT laminates under different loading conditions is complex [7, 8]. 
Membrane elastic modulus of HFT laminates was found to be different to flexural elastic modulus, 
which indicates transverse shear deformations between lamella during bending [7]. When subjected 
to shear, behaviour of the laminate was found to be affected significantly by the fibre rotations [8]. 
Such behaviours have made numerical modelling of the HFT structures difficult. 
Few efforts have been made on numerical modelling of HFT structures under different loading 
conditions [8, 9]. Ability of the existing numerical modelling approaches to capture the initial 
stiffness of the HFT structures was found to be dependent on the manufacturing pressure [7]. When 
sufficient pressure was applied during the manufacturing process, it was found that difference

the behaviour of HFT laminates beyond initial elastic region due to complex behaviours of HFT 
laminates such as fibre rotations, progressive failure of lamella, and bonded interface failures [8].  
Existing approaches where fibres and matrix are modelled as a single homogenized medium does not 
account for mechanisms such as fibre rotations. While modelling fibres and matrix as separate 
elements, theoretically has the ability to consider fibre rotation effects, such detailed modelling 
requires significant computational efforts and not suitable for modelling the behaviour of structural 
scale elements. Therefore, a suitable constitutive model capable of capturing the behaviour of HFT 
laminates is necessary to enable accurate FE modelling of the HFT structural members. 
Against this background, this paper presents a study aimed at developing a constitutive model for 
HFT laminates considering the whole laminate as a single layer. As modelling individual layers and 
complex mechanisms governing the interaction between different lamella becomes too 
computationally demanding for modelling large scale HFT structures, this study attempts to capture 
the behaviour of the HFT laminates as a single layer, by smearing all the behaviours occurring within 
the laminate in to a single laminate behaviour. Failure theory in this model was developed using data 
created through both experimental and numerical approaches. This failure theory was then 
implemented using a vectorised user material subroutine (VUMAT) in FE software 
ABAQUS\Explicit [10], and the model was applied in the numerical modelling of HFT laminates 
under tension-tension biaxial loading.  
2. Material coupon tests
To determine the mechanical properties in the principal material directions, material coupon tests 
were carried out, including tensile, compressive and shear tests. Coupon tests were carried out on 
timber laminates, GFRP laminates, and HFT laminates. Coupons in both timber grain direction and 
perpendicular to grain direction were prepared and tested for timber and HFT coupons, while coupons 
in fibre direction and perpendicular to fibre direction were prepared and tested for GFRP specimens. 
Additionally, off-axis HFT coupons with fibres at 20o, 40o and 70o to the longitudinal axis were also 
prepared and tested under tension to investigate the behaviour of HFT laminates under biaxial stress 
states.
2.1 Materials
Hoop pine (Araucaria cunninghamii) veneer sheets with planar dimensions of 1200mm × 2400mm 
and 1.5mm thickness were used to prepare the timber and HFT specimens in this study. Unidirectional 
E-glass fibre (GF) woven fabric with 1025 grams per square meter (gsm) density was used for GFRP 
and HFT specimens. Based on the recommendation of Ref. [11], one-component polyurethane 
(Loctite Purbond HB S109) adhesive was used in the fabrication of all laminated specimens.

2.2 Specimen preparation
As timber properties could vary from one veneer sheet to another, efforts were made to ensure the 
timber veneers are distributed approximately evenly amongst the sample sets of different test 
specimen types. In total four 1.2 m × 2.4 m timber veneer sheets were used in specimen 
manufacturing. Each timber veneer sheet was cut into 320 mm × 320 mm squares, from which both 
pure timber and HFT plates (also in the size of 320 mm × 320 mm) were fabricated. Each timber 
plate and HFT plate consist of two layers of timber veneers, with grains of both veneers kept in the 
same direction. For HFT specimens, GF fabric layer of 320 mm × 320 mm squares were added to 
either side of the timber plate. Efforts were made to ensure adhesive is equally spread in bonding 
timber veneers together, while equal spreading as well as proper wetting of GFs were ensured in the 
fabrication of HFT specimens. Fibres of the GFRP layers were kept perpendicular to the timber grain 
direction. Layer configuration of HFT plates is shown in Fig. 5. Once all the layers were assembled, 
plates (both HFT and timber) were placed in a press machine under 1 MPa at room temperature for 4

2.3 Test setup and instrumentation
All the tests were carried out using a servo controlled 250 kN capacity, Instron testing machine. For 
tensile tests of GFRP specimens and HFT specimens with transverse fibres, 1 mm thick aluminium 
tabs were attached to the ends of the specimens, and the specimens were gripped to the jaws of the 
Instron testing machine (Fig. 6a). For compressive tests, specimens were first placed in the Wyoming 
compressive test fixture [14] and then the fixture was placed between the top and bottom loading 
plates of the Instron machine (Fig. 6b). For shear tests, specimen was first placed in the Wyoming Vnotched rail shear test fixture [15], and the two halves of the test fixture were connected directly to 
the Instron loading machine (Fig. 5c). For all the tensile tests, a loading rate of 2 mm/min was used, 
for compressive tests a loading rate of 1.3 mm/min was used, and for shear tests a loading rate of 0.6 
mm/min was used. Strains of timber and HFT tensile test specimens were measured using a digital 
image correlation (DIC) system. DIC system malfunctioned during GFRP tensile coupon tests, thus 
strain gauges were used to measure the strains of GFRP tensile test specimens. For compressive 
coupons, the use of DIC system was not possible due to the limited space available in the test fixture, 
and strain gauges were attached to either side of the specimen. DIC system was used for measuring 
strains in the gauge section between the tips of the two notches for shear specimens. Instrumented 
test specimens for tensile, compressive and shear tests are shown in Fig. 6.

2.5 Discussion on experimental results
Comparing the tensile test results of the timber, GFRP, and HFT coupons it can be observed that: (a) 
Tensile elastic modulus of HFT lies between the tensile elastic modulus and timber elastic modulus 
(in their respective directions within HFT). Similar could be said for tensile strength. Similar 
observations could be made for compressive and shear test results. Addition of GFRP improved the 
properties of timber in transverse direction, while addition of timber improved the properties of GFRP 
in transverse direction. Unlike pure timber and pure GFRP coupons, HFT specimens showed similar 
stiffness in two principal directions. 
During the testing, none of the HFT specimens showed delamination of the GFRP layer from the 
timber layer, which indicates that it is reasonable to assume all laminae of the HFT specimens remain 
fully bonded until the complete failure. Considering delamination did not occur, strength of HFT at 
the first laminae failure was calculated (assuming strain continuity) to be: tensile strength parallel to 
grain 112 MPa; tensile strength perpendicular to grain 66 MPa; compressive strength parallel to grain 
46 MPa; compressive strength perpendicular to grain 31 MPa; shear strength parallel to grain 5.4 
MPa; and shear strength perpendicular to grain 13 MPa. For tensile and compressive strengths, 
calculated values over predicted the strength in parallel to grain direction but underestimated the 
values in perpendicular to grain direction. Shear strength parallel to grain was under predicted by the 
calculations, while shear strength perpendicular to grain was reasonably well predicted. Overall, 
except for shear strength perpendicular to grain, all other strength values were incorrectly predicted 
by the calculations based on strain continuity until first ply failure.

HFT specimens under shear loading showed somewhat different behaviour to pure GFRP and timber

progressive failure. It was also shown that accurate modelling of the behaviour of HFT specimens 
under shear loading require modelling of the fibre rotation. 
HFT specimens with off-axis fibres showed significant nonlinearity in load-displacement curves.

different behaviour with a sudden load drop as the ultimate load is reached. As off-axis fibre 
specimens are subjected to bi-axial loading with respect to the principal directions, in plane shear 
loading can be expected. Therefore, at certain fibre angles, shear dominant failures could be expected. 
However, from the experimental results alone, it is difficult to determine the exact failure process of 
the off-axis fibre specimens. 
3. Numerical modelling of HFT laminates under biaxial loading
Several specimen shapes have been used in existing studies to investigate the behaviour of composite 
laminates under biaxial loading [16–18]. One key difficulty reported in many of those studies is the 
high number of samples failing in pre-dominantly uniaxially loaded regions. Some of those specimen 
shapes were trialled in the current study to determine the behaviour of HFT laminates under bi-axial 
loading. However, failure of all the specimens occurred either within the gripping region or in the 
regions with predominantly uniaxial loading. Therefore, after many failed attempts, experimental 
approach to investigate the behaviour of HFT laminates under bi-axial loading was ruled out. Instead, 
numerical modelling was used to determine the failure initiation of HFT laminates under bi-axial 
loading. While existing studies have shown that numerical modelling of HFT sections using layered 
approaches cannot capture the full range behaviour of the HFT sections, behaviour until failure in 
first ply was reasonably well captured [9]. Therefore, numerical modelling approach was deemed 
satisfactory to study the failure initiation of the HFT laminates. This section presents the details of 
the numerical modelling approach, verification of the numerical model against uni-axial data, and 
numerical results under bi-axial loading with the aim of capturing the failure initiation of the HFT 
laminates. 
3.1 Numerical modelling approach
HFT laminates were modelled using composite layup approach available in commercially available 
FE modelling software ABAQUS [10]. Thickness of timber was taken as 3 mm, based on the 
measured values of HFT laminates, and the nominal thickness of each GFRP layer was considered as 
0.75 mm. Reduced integration shell elements S4R were used to model the HFT laminates. 
Both GFRP and timber were considered as orthotropic materials. Under both tension and compression, 
the uniaxial behaviour in each principal material direction was assumed to be characterized by a bilinear (i.e. a linear ascending branch and a linear descending branch) traction-separation law. The 
gradient of the ascending branch of the traction-separation curve was taken as the respective elastic 
modulus, and the gradient of the descending branch of the curve was determined based on the fracture 
energy in each principal material direction based on material coupon tests. The critical fracture energy 
values used in the models are [8]: (1) For GFRP- parallel to fibre in tension=50 N/mm,  perpendicular

4.2.3 Damage evolution when failure initiates due to shear
The damage parameter for shear used in Eq. (8) is related to the damage parameters in 1- and 2- 
directions. In the model presented in section 3.1, since shear and normal stress coupling is considered 
in failure initiation, even if failure initiation occurs due to shear alone, damage parameters in 1- and 
2- directions can still capture the damage due to shear stresses. However, in the proposed model in 
section 4, as no coupling between shear and normal stresses are considered, modification to the 
damage parameters is necessary to account for damage due to shear. Study by Miao et al. [12] showed 
that when subjected to shear loading, rotation in fibres results in tensile stresses in fibres. Therefore, 
final failure occurs when the fibres fail. Rotation of the fibres also means, while tensile stresses in 
fibre direction increases, fibre direction will not be parallel to specified 1- or 2- directions. During 
the increase in deformations, shear stress was found to remain a constant [8]. That means, the increase 
in tensile stresses in fibre direction will result in such a manner that force vector parallel to shear 
direction will remain a constant. An exact consideration of the fibre rotation effects can only be done 
through modelling of the fibre rotation as in Miao et al. [8]. However, such modelling is complex, 
thus does not comply with the objective of simplified modelling of the HFT laminates. Therefore, the

Conclusions
This paper presents an investigation into understanding the behaviour of HFT laminates and a new 
failure theory to model the constitutive behaviour of HFT laminates. Experimental studies were 
carried out to determine the behaviour under uniaxial tensile, compressive and shear loading with 
respect to both principal material directions of HFT laminates. As behaviour of HFT laminates under 
bi-axial load was not possible to capture through experiments, a numerical approach based on 
previous research of HFT laminates was adopted to determine the different stress components at 
failure initiation of HFT laminates. Based on the obtained experimental and numerical data, a new 
failure theory was proposed. This failure theory was then implemented in FE software ABAQUS 
using a vectorized user material subroutine (VUMAT). Results were then compared with 
experimental results of HFT coupons. From the experimental and theoretical investigations presented 
in this paper, following conclusions can be achieved:

Uniaxial tests showed that HFT specimens showed approximately linear behaviour until 
ultimate load. When loaded in tension, specimens failed suddenly once ultimate load is 
reached. When loaded in compression, some specimens showed gradual reduction of load 
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5244199
Preprint not peer reviewed

30
once ultimate load is reached. Elastic properties under normal stresses were found to be more 
balanced between the two principal material directions than in pure GFRP and timber.

When loaded in shear, HFT specimens showed significant nonlinear behaviour with a load 
plateau after the ultimate load is reached. A parallel study by the authors’ research group [8] 
showed through numerical modelling and experimental observations that fibre rotation of 
glass fibre is the key reason for such nonlinear behaviour under shear loading. 

Simple calculation of the failure initiation load based on strain continuity was not able to 
capture the failure loads of HFT laminates.

Numerical modelling based on layer wise modelling approach was used to generate data for 
failure initiation of HFT laminates under bi-axial loading. These data showed that both Hashin 
failure criteria and Quadratic stress criteria cannot capture the failure initiation accurately in 
all the stress quadrants of the normal stress diagram.  

A new failure theory was proposed based on the data generated using experimental and 
numerical approaches. Failure theory was implemented in FE software ABAQUS using a 
VUMAT. FE predictions using the new model provided excellent agreements with the test 
results of HFT laminates. Proposed failure theory can be used to model the behaviour of HFT 
structures, where full laminate can be modelled as a single layer, thus significantly reduce the 
computational cost.
