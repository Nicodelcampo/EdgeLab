Algorithmic Trading:

Abstract 
The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning into algorithmic trading has fundamentally 
transformed financial markets. This paper examines how AI-driven trading systems spanning supervised 
learning, reinforcement learning, and natural language processing  have enhanced execution efficiency, 
price discovery, and liquidity provision. Simultaneously, these systems introduce systemic risks 
including flash crashes, herding behavior, and a novel overfitting-driven correlated failure mechanism. 
Drawing on established theoretical frameworks and empirical literature, this study evaluates the dual 
character of AI in trading: as a source of competitive advantage and as a potential amplifier of market 
instability. Policy implications for regulatory frameworks, including a proposed model genealogy audit, 
are discussed. 
 
1. Introduction 
Algorithmic trading now accounts for between 60 and 75 percent of equity trading volume in the United 
States (Hendershott, Jones & Menkveld, 2011). The rise of machine learning has accelerated this shift, 
enabling market participants to process vast data streams from order books and tick data to 
macroeconomic signals and news sentiment at speeds impossible for human traders. As AI techniques 
grow more sophisticated, their influence on market structure, price formation, and systemic stability 
deepens. 
Traditional algorithms operated as fixed rule sets. Modern AI systems, by contrast, learn from millions 
of historical trades, adapt to evolving market conditions, and execute thousands of decisions per second. 
The same techniques powering recommendation engines and image classification now operate inside 
live financial markets with real capital and real systemic consequences. 
Yet a unified assessment of AI’s impact across efficiency, systemic risk, and regulation remains absent 
from the literature. This paper addresses that gap by synthesizing findings across financial economics, 
market microstructure theory, and computational finance. 

Three research questions guide this study: 
RQ1:  How do AI and ML techniques improve market efficiency, liquidity, and trading performance 
relative to traditional rule-based algorithmic strategies? 
RQ2:  What systemic risks and market instabilities are introduced or amplified by the widespread 
adoption of AI-driven trading systems? 
RQ3:  How should existing regulatory frameworks evolve to address the unique challenges posed by AI 
in financial markets, balancing innovation with stability? 
 
2. Methodology 
This paper adopts a systematic literature review methodology. A structured search across SSRN, Google 
Scholar, and JSTOR used keywords including ‘algorithmic trading,’ ‘machine learning in finance,’ 
‘high-frequency trading,’ and ‘flash crash.’ Selected studies span seminal empirical work (Hendershott 
et al., 2011; Kirilenko et al., 2017), market microstructure theory (Kyle, 1985; Glosten & Milgrom, 
1985), and recent computational finance literature on reinforcement learning and transformer-based 
models (Spooner et al., 2018; Lopez de Prado, 2018). 
Findings are organized using a two-dimensional analytical framework:  
(a) an efficiency–risk tradeoff dimension mapping AI techniques against documented benefits and 
harms; and  
(b) a temporal dimension tracing evolution from rule-based systems to deep reinforcement learning 
agents.  
This enables comparison across supervised learning, reinforcement learning, and NLP paradigms across 
equity, derivatives, and cryptocurrency markets. Primary data collection is outside this study’s scope; 
limitations include publication bias and the rapidly evolving nature of AI techniques. 
 
3. Background: From Rule-Based Systems to AI-Driven Trading 
The evolution of algorithmic trading mirrors the broader evolution of computing. Early systems in the 
1970s and 1980s automated simple order routing. The 1990s introduced quantitative strategies 
exploiting statistical relationships in price data. The 2000s saw the rise of high-frequency trading (HFT), 
characterized by ultra-low-latency execution and market-making strategies (Biais, Foucault & Moinas, 
2015). 
The current era, beginning around 2015, is defined by deep learning and reinforcement learning. The 
critical distinction from prior generations is adaptability: where earlier algorithms execute 
pre-programmed rules, modern AI systems identify novel patterns and update their behavior 
continuously. In terms of market microstructure, this shift has measurable consequences for bid-ask 
spread dynamics (Fama, 1970; Cont, 2001), information efficiency, and volatility regimes (Shiller, 1981; 
Zhang, 2010). 

In AI/ML terms, financial markets represent one of the most demanding deployment environments: 
non-stationary data distributions, adversarial dynamics (other participants adapt to observed strategies), 
high-dimensional feature spaces, and real-time decision requirements under uncertainty (Lopez de 
Prado, 2018). 
 
4. Opportunities: Efficiency and Performance Gains 
 
4.1 Market Microstructure Improvements 
AI-driven market makers have measurably narrowed bid-ask spreads and deepened order books. 
Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld (2011) demonstrated empirically that algorithmic trading improves 
liquidity and price discovery. Greater market depth allows larger transactions without adverse price 
impact. Easley et al. (2011) further showed that order flow toxicity, an information-theoretic measure of 
adverse selection risk, was significantly elevated during the 2010 Flash Crash, underscoring how AI 
both enables and stresses liquidity conditions simultaneously. 
4.2 Supervised Learning for Return Forecasting 
Supervised learning models including random forests, gradient boosting (XGBoost), and support vector 
machines demonstrate superior short-horizon return forecasting compared to linear benchmarks. These 
models capture nonlinear feature interactions that OLS regression misses. Even modest improvements in 
directional accuracy, compounded across thousands of daily trades, produce substantial performance 
gains (Lopez de Prado, 2018). Ensemble methods are especially valued for robustness to financial data 
noise. 
4.3 Reinforcement Learning for Optimal Execution 
Reinforcement learning represents a natural fit for trading framed as sequential decision-making under 
uncertainty. Spooner et al. (2018) showed that deep Q-network agents learn adaptive execution strategies 
that minimize market impact costs, outperforming deterministic algorithms such as VWAP in dynamic 
conditions. The agent’s policy learned through millions of simulated interactions generalizes across 
market regimes in ways that hand-crafted rules cannot. 
4.4 Natural Language Processing and Alternative Data 
Transformer-based language models such as FinBERT enable real-time sentiment extraction from earnings 
calls, financial news, analyst reports, and social media. Baron et al. (2019) documented that HFT strategies 
incorporating information signals systematically outperform slower participants. The broader alternative 
data ecosystem satellite imagery, credit card transactions, shipping data further extends the information 
advantage of AI-enabled participants.

5. Risks: Systemic Fragility and Market Instability 
5.1 Flash Crashes and Liquidity Withdrawal 
The Flash Crash of May 6, 2010 during which the Dow Jones Industrial Average lost nearly 1,000 points 
in 36 minutes before partially recovering remains the most widely studied example of AI-amplified 
market instability. Kirilenko et al. (2017) demonstrated through trade-level analysis that HFT firms 
amplified the crash by withdrawing liquidity precisely when stabilization was most needed. The 
feedback loop compressed what might have been a gradual price adjustment into a near-instantaneous 
collapse. 

From an ML perspective, this is an emergent failure: no individual system was incorrectly designed, yet 
their collective behavior produced a system-level outcome far exceeding any single failure. Zhang 
(2010) further documented that HFT activity is positively correlated with short-term stock price 
volatility, suggesting the relationship between AI trading and instability is structural, not episodic.

5.2 Structural Herding 
Beyond discrete crash events, structural herding operates continuously. When multiple AI systems train 
on overlapping datasets and similar feature sets, they develop implicitly correlated strategies. Unlike 
strategic herding where human traders consciously follow leaders, structural herding is algorithmic and 
invisible to standard monitoring. The microstructure framework of Kyle (1985) and Glosten and 
Milgrom (1985) models markets as information aggregation mechanisms; structural herding distorts this 
aggregation, converting idiosyncratic shocks into systemic events. 
 
5.3 Overfitting as a Novel Systemic Risk (Original Contribution) 
This paper introduces a distinct argument: model overfitting represents an underrecognized source of 
systemic financial risk. In machine learning, overfitting occurs when a model learns noise in training 
data rather than underlying signal producing strong in-sample performance but poor generalization. In 
most ML applications, such failures are localized. In financial markets, the failure mode is systemic. 
The mechanism is as follows. Multiple AI trading firms source data from the same vendors (Bloomberg, 
Refinitiv, public exchanges), training models on substantially overlapping historical datasets. These 
models encode shared regime assumptions embedded beliefs about interest rate dynamics, volatility 
relationships, and macro-asset correlations. These assumptions hold during stability because they reflect 
historical regularities. 
During structural regime shifts the COVID-19 liquidity contraction of March 2020, or the Federal 
Reserve rate hike cycle beginning 2022 these assumptions are violated simultaneously across firms. The 
distributional shift from training to live data is abrupt. The result is correlated model failure: multiple 
systems generating incorrect predictions and executing destabilizing trades concurrently. 

This mechanism requires no coordination. It arises purely from structural similarity of training datasets 
and architectures across competing firms. Crucially, it is invisible to standard Value-at-Risk models, 
which assume historical volatility patterns persist precisely the assumption violated during a regime 
shift. This hypothesis is empirically testable: trade-level data around identified regime shifts could 
determine whether AI-heavy stocks exhibit greater correlated drawdowns than traditionally managed 
portfolios.

5.4 Empirical Illustration: Proof-of-Concept Experiment 
To illustrate the overfitting-driven correlated failure mechanism empirically, a proof-of-concept 
experiment was conducted using publicly available equity price data for five large-cap S&P 500 stocks 
(AAPL, MSFT, JPM, XOM, JNJ) sourced from Yahoo Finance. Two ML classifiers, a Random Forest 
and a Gradient Boosting model, were trained independently on identical feature sets derived from the 
same 2015–2019 historical data. Both models were then deployed on out-of-sample 2020 data, spanning 
the February - March COVID-19 crash. 
The experiment directly simulates the structural conditions identified in Section 5.3: two firms using the 
same data vendor, constructing similar features, and training on the same historical regime. Eight 
technical features were computed per asset (momentum at multiple horizons, rolling volatility, RSI, and 
price deviation from 20-day moving average) and averaged across assets to produce a single daily 
feature vector. 
Results are presented in Figure 4. Several findings are directly consistent with the correlated failure 
hypothesis. First, both models exhibit near-identical error trajectories throughout the test period: the 
inter-model error correlation is 0.99 during stable pre-crash conditions and 0.94 during the crash. This 
sustained high correlation demonstrates that independently trained models sharing a training data source 

produce structurally synonymous prediction behavior. Second, both models suffer sharp simultaneous 
accuracy deterioration during the crash window (February 24 - March 23, 2020). Random Forest 
directional accuracy falls from 0.61 on the full test set to 0.38 during the crash period below random 
chance while Gradient Boosting declines from 0.58 to 0.52. Third, mean absolute error rises by 
approximately 10 percent for both models during the crash relative to pre-crash baseline conditions, 
consistent with the distributional shift from training data to crisis-period live data. 
These results are illustrative rather than definitive: a five-stock sample and directional classification task 
do not capture the full complexity of live trading systems. However, the experiment demonstrates that 
the core mechanism is reproducible with standard tools and publicly available data. Future work using 
trade-level data from exchanges or regulatory repositories spanning a broader asset universe and more 
granular time series would constitute a rigorous test of the hypothesis at market scale.

6. Regulatory Implications 
 
6.1 Inadequacy of Existing Frameworks 
The two principal frameworks governing algorithmic trading MiFID II in the EU and SEC Rule 15c3-5 
in the United States were designed for rule-based systems. MiFID II introduced requirements for firms 
to test algorithms and implement kill switches. SEC Rule 15c3-5 mandates pre-trade risk controls. Both 
are necessary but insufficient for the ML era. 
Neither framework requires interpretable model logic, stress-testing under distribution shift, or 
disclosure of training data structure. Both treat each firm’s risk as primarily idiosyncratic, when the real 
danger in AI trading is emergent from cross-firm structural interactions precisely the failure mode 
identified in Section 5.3. 
 
6.2 Proposed Regulatory Enhancements 
Effective regulation must incorporate three additional dimensions: 
First, explainability requirements for model logic. Firms operating systemically significant algorithms 
should demonstrate auditability using established tools such as SHAP values and LIME, adapted from 
the ML fairness literature to financial oversight contexts. 
Second, stress-testing obligations analogous to those imposed on systemically important banks under the 
Basel framework. AI trading models should demonstrate acceptable performance under simulated 
regime shift scenarios specifically, training-test distribution mismatches calibrated to historical crises. 
Third, model genealogy audits: regulatory assessments of training data overlap and architectural 
similarity across systemically significant firms. As bank stress tests map common balance sheet 
vulnerabilities, model genealogy audits would map shared embedded assumptions across the AI trading 
ecosystem  providing early warning of correlated failure potential. 
 
6.3 Regulatory Sandboxes as Adaptive Oversight 
The FCA and MAS sandboxes offer a constructive adaptive oversight model. By allowing firms to test 
AI applications in a controlled environment, these sandboxes generate evidence about risk 
characteristics without stifling innovation, a significant improvement over reactive regulation. Extending 
sandbox methodologies to multi-firm simulation environments would enable observation of collective 
AI trading system behavior, generating empirical evidence directly relevant to the systemic risk 
questions this paper raises.

7. Discussion 
The findings reveal a fundamental tension in AI adoption in financial markets. The efficiency gains 
documented in Section 4 are real and empirically grounded: tighter spreads, better price discovery, and 
improved execution quality benefit all participants, including retail investors through reduced 
transaction costs. 
Yet these gains are achieved through the same mechanisms of speed, scale, and historical 
pattern-learning that generate novel systemic vulnerabilities. Each individual AI system may be rational 
and well-designed, while their collective behavior produces outcomes none individually anticipated. 
The emergent nature of AI risk has direct implications for regulation. Traditional frameworks focus on 
individual firm solvency and risk management. The overfitting-driven failure mode operates at the 
ecosystem level, arising from structural similarity across competing firms not from any single actor’s 
behavior. Regulating it demands a systemic perspective current frameworks lack. 
For AI/ML practitioners, this is a distribution shift problem elevated to market scale. A model’s 
real-world performance is bounded by the similarity between its training and deployment distributions. 
When AI systems across a market share training distributions diverging from the current regime, 
instability follows as a near-mathematical consequence. Financial regulators must internalize this 
intuition. 
8. Conclusion 
This paper has examined the dual character of AI in algorithmic trading: the efficiency gains it delivers, 
the systemic risks it introduces, and the regulatory evolution required. The efficiency case is strong and 
empirically grounded. The risk case particularly the overfitting-driven correlated failure mechanism is 
novel and underappreciated. 
The principal original contribution is the identification of model genealogy as a source of systemic risk, 
supported by a proof-of-concept experiment on real S&P 500 equity data. Two independently trained 
classifiers sharing a training data source maintained error correlations of 0.94 - 0.99 throughout the test 
period and suffered simultaneous accuracy collapse during the COVID-19 crash Random Forest 

directional accuracy falling from 0.61 to 0.38 (below random chance) while both models’ mean absolute 
error rose approximately 10 percent. This empirical illustration is consistent with the correlated failure 
hypothesis and demonstrates reproducibility using standard tools and public data. 
At full scale, model genealogy audits, distribution-shift stress-testing, and explainability requirements 
constitute a coherent regulatory agenda for the failure modes of ML-driven trading. Extending this 
experiment to trade-level data across a broader asset universe and multiple regime-shift events would 
constitute rigorous validation of the regulatory proposals advanced here. 
As AI systems become more capable and widely deployed, the boundary between computational 
intelligence and systemic risk becomes increasingly consequential. Understanding that boundary 
requires bringing the conceptual toolkit of machine learning distribution shift, overfitting, model 
genealogy into the core vocabulary of financial regulation.
