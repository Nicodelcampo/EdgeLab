Algorithmic Trading of Portfolios
Sanghyun Park∗
Kapil Phadnis†
January 24, 2017
Abstract
We develop a portfolio trading strategy that aims to achieve optimal performance against
arrival prices by minimizing market impact and risk. We ﬁrst lay the groundwork by
formulating a single-security trading strategy and then generalize the framework to the
portfolio trading context. Correlations between securities are incorporated via a multi-factor
risk model, and the resulting optimization problem is solved numerically by quadratic
programming. Comparing the efﬁcient frontier and the early-end VWAP (volume-weighted
average price) strategies leads to an intuitive method to determine the risk aversion
parameter. We also present an efﬁcient way to identify securities for which block trading
opportunities can be pursued without compromising the risk structure of the portfolio.
1
Introduction
Portfolio trading is much more than simply trading multiple securities at the same time.
Securities in a portfolio are correlated with each other, and this correlation must be taken into
account in ﬁnding an optimal trading schedule for each security.
A trading algorithm attempts to optimize performance measured against a benchmark. The
behavior of an algorithm varies widely depending on which benchmark is being used. Here we
are concerned with the arrival price benchmark. Arrival-price algorithms operate in ways to ﬁnd
the balance between market impact and market risk, or equivalently, between slow and fast
trading paces.

This is already evident in trading of a single security. With slow trading, market impact may be
reduced but the risk increases that the market may move away. With fast trading, market risk is
small but the algorithm itself may move the market considerably, hence causing big market
impact. An arrival price algorithm minimizes a combination of market impact and market risk,
typically with a parameter controlling the balance between the two. This parameter is called risk
aversion level or urgency level.
When it comes to trading a portfolio, the impact-risk trade-off becomes more involved. Because
of the correlation among the securities in the portfolio, the risk terms of the securities may cancel
one another out, leading to much lower risk for the entire portfolio. This is essentially the same
idea as diversiﬁcation or hedging. A portfolio arrival-price algorithm takes advantage of this
important idea in order to minimize the market impact and risk.
Part I
Single-security trading
Before we get to trading of portfolios, we will formulate an arrival-price algorithm for a single
security.
2
Model
In order to quantify market impact and risk, we need to model how the security prices move and
how our trading impact the movement.
We deﬁne the equilibrium price of the security to be the market price without our participation.
Using the volume time u that measures the expected market volume from now (u = 0) to the end
(u = U), we assume that the equilibrium price s(u) evolves according to
s(u) = S + σSW(u)
(1)
where W(u) is a standard Brownian motion and S = s(0). σ is the volatility per share.

We specify a trading schedule via the remaining quantity x(u), which is to vary monotonically
from X at u = 0 to zero at u = U. The order quantity X is positive for buys and negative for sells.
We consider only the strategies that target to complete, i.e. x(U) = 0.
With a given trading schedule, we assume that the execution price will deviate from the
equilibrium price as
ˆs(u) = s(u) + γSv(u) ,
(2)
where v(u) = −dx(u)/du is the trading speed in volume time and γ is the market impact
parameter. In this model, the market impact is essentially proportional to the trading rate and the
impact is only temporary, not affecting the equilibrium price. Many market impact models of
various degrees of complexity are out there, but here we choose a simple linear model for the
sake of efﬁcient optimization. In view of the extremely noisy nature of market impact, it is
unclear whether more complex models will offer enough beneﬁt to offset the more demanding
optimization that they require.

7
Block Trading
What if there is an opportunity to get a block of shares without market impact? Clearly, such
block trading does not follow the market impact model of Eq 2. Assuming it has no impact on
the subsequent trading, getting a block ﬁll is always beneﬁcial because it decreases the risk by
reducing the remaining quantity. Therefore, we should combine the scheduled trading with
opportunistic block trading. The schedule can be re-optimized periodically or whenever we get a
big block ﬁll. If we get no blocks, we still ﬁnish according to the schedule. If we get lucky and get
big blocks, we will ﬁnish sooner with lower risk.

Optimization
Unfortunately, the closed-form solution for the single-security trading cannot be generalized to
the portfolio context. Recall that the closed-form solution was derived without the monotonicity
constraint. That was not an issue because the solution comes out to be monotonic anyway. For
portfolio trading, without that constraint, the optimal solution may not be monotonic. For
example, initially selling a buy order may actually reduce the objective function if it quickly
makes the portfolio risk neutral. Non-monotonic trading schedules are not allowed in most
practical applications, and we must impose the constraint explicitly. With the monotonicity
constraint, closed-form solutions are no longer available and we have to perform numerical
optimization.

Block Trading
We saw that getting block ﬁlls, in addition to the scheduled trading, is always beneﬁcial for
single-security trading. For portfolio trading, however, block ﬁlls are not always favorable. Block
ﬁlls reduce remaining quantities, which in turn may decrease or increase the risk depending on
the correlations between the securities in the portfolio. Here we present an algorithm to
incorporate block trading into the portfolio context by selectively pursuing only favorable blocks.
First, we rank the securities in the portfolio according to their marginal contributions to the
objective function. The marginal contribution ∆Ωi is deﬁned to be the amount that will be
reduced if we remove the security i from the portfolio:
∆Ωi = 1

Since the portfolio can be re-optimized after removing the security, this is in fact an
underestimate.
If the ﬁrst security in the ranking (the one with the highest marginal contribution) has a positive
marginal contribution, we route all of its remaining quantity to dark pools and crossing venues
to pursue blocks. Due to the opportunistic nature of block trading, the ﬁlls can be anywhere
between 0 and 100%. With zero ﬁlls, the change in the objective function is obviously zero. With
100% ﬁlls, the objective function decreases by the amount of the marginal contribution M1 (or
even more with re-optimization). With any ﬁll quantity in between, the objective function cannot
increase because of its convexity with respect to the order quantities.
For the second security in the ranking, if the ﬁrst security was selected for block trading, we need
to examine the two-dimensional space of possible block ﬁlls for the two securities. Because of the
convexity, however, we only need to check the corners of the rectangle: (0%, 0%), (0%, 100%),
