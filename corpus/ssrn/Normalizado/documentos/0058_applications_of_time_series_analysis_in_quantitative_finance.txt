Applications of Time Series Analysis in Quantitative
Finance
Yifan Guo
August 6, 2025
Abstract
Time series analysis is a cornerstone of quantitative finance, underpinning models
for forecasting asset returns, quantifying risk and optimizing portfolios. Classical statistical models such as ARIMA and GARCH, as well as more recent machine learning and
deep learning approaches, have all been applied to financial time series. However, realworld financial markets are non-stationary, regime-switching and highly noisy, which
makes direct application of textbook models fragile.
In this paper we study the applications of time series analysis in quantitative finance
from a unified perspective. First, we formalize a task–model mapping that connects
major financial tasks (return forecasting, volatility prediction, risk measurement and
dynamic portfolio allocation) with appropriate model classes. Second, we propose a
simple Regime-Aware Hybrid Forecasting (RAHF) framework that combines linear time
series models with nonlinear learners under a latent-regime view of the market. We
provide a detailed bias–variance analysis of the hybrid forecast and show that, under
mild conditions, hybridization can reduce mean squared forecast error relative to singlemodel baselines. Finally, we provide an empirical protocol template for evaluating time
series models in equity markets, including recommended figures and tables that can be
instantiated with Python-generated plots in future work.
1
Introduction
Quantitative finance relies heavily on time series analysis. Asset prices, returns, interest
rates, volatility indices and macroeconomic indicators all arrive as sequences over time. These
series exhibit serial dependence, volatility clustering, heavy tails and structural breaks. A
central challenge is to extract predictive structure from such data while acknowledging their
non-stationary nature.
1

Classical financial econometrics has developed a rich toolkit for this purpose. Autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models describe conditional means; generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models describe conditional variances;
state-space models encode latent factors. In parallel, the machine learning community has
introduced flexible nonlinear models such as tree ensembles and recurrent neural networks
(RNNs), which can capture complex patterns at the cost of interpretability and, sometimes,
stability.
Figure 1: Illustrative example of a financial time series: log-price and log-returns of an equity
index over time. This figure can be generated in Python from historical data.
The goal of this paper is not to introduce yet another highly specialized model, but rather
to provide a structured perspective on how time series analysis can be systematically applied
to core problems in quantitative finance, and to show how linear and nonlinear models can
be combined in a principled way.
Problem Setup
We consider a discrete-time financial market observed at times t = 1, 2, . . . , T. Let
Rt = (R1,t, . . . , Rn,t)⊤
denote the random vector of asset returns at time t, and let
Ft = σ(R1, . . . , Rt)
2

be the information set up to time t. A generic time series model produces forecasts of objects
such as:
• conditional mean returns E[Rt+1 | Ft],
• conditional covariance matrices Cov(Rt+1 | Ft),
• risk functionals (e.g., Value at Risk) of a portfolio w⊤Rt+1,
• latent states indicating market regimes (e.g., high-volatility vs. low-volatility).
These forecasts are then used in downstream tasks such as trading, risk management, and
portfolio optimization.
Contributions
The main contributions of this paper are threefold:
(1) Task–Model Map. We provide a concise taxonomy that links key quantitative finance
tasks (mean forecasting, volatility forecasting, risk measurement, dynamic allocation) to
appropriate families of time series models (linear, volatility, state-space, and nonlinear
machine learning models).
(2) Regime-Aware Hybrid Forecasting (RAHF). We introduce a simple hybrid framework that combines linear time series models with nonlinear residual learners under a
latent-regime view of the market. We provide a detailed bias–variance analysis of the
hybrid forecast and give conditions under which it strictly improves mean squared error
over linear baselines.
(3) Evaluation Protocol Template.
We outline an empirical evaluation protocol for
financial time series models, including rolling-window backtesting, risk-adjusted performance metrics and robustness checks, together with suggested figures and tables that
can be populated using Python.

2.3
State-Space and Regime-Switching Models
State-space models introduce latent variables to represent hidden economic factors or regimes.
A simple linear Gaussian state-space model is
xt+1 = Fxt + ut,
(6)
Rt = Hxt + εt,
(7)
where xt is the latent state, F and H are system matrices, ut and εt are noise processes.
Kalman filtering provides optimal state estimates under Gaussian assumptions.
Regime-switching models assume that parameters depend on a discrete latent state St ∈
{1, . . . , K}, which itself follows a Markov chain. For example, in a two-regime model,
Yt = µSt + εt,
St ∈{low-vol, high-vol},
(8)

2.4
Machine Learning and Deep Learning Models
Nonlinear models can flexibly approximate complex dependencies:
5

• Random forests and gradient boosting exploit tree-based ensembles over handcrafted features (lags, technical indicators, macro variables).
• Support Vector Regression (SVR) finds a function f(x) = w⊤ϕ(x) + b that approximates returns with controlled complexity.
• Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and LSTMs model long-range temporal dependencies via hidden states.
Given a feature vector xt summarizing information up to time t, a generic nonlinear model
produces a forecast ˆYt+1 = fθ(xt), where θ denotes parameters learned by minimizing a loss
such as mean squared error.

A Regime-Aware Hybrid Forecasting Framework
We now introduce a simple hybrid framework that will later be linked to concrete applications
in risk management and portfolio allocation.
3.1
Latent Regimes and Hybrid Models
Financial markets often alternate between qualitatively different regimes, such as low-volatility
“normal” periods and high-volatility “stress” periods. We model this with a latent regime
variable St ∈{1, . . . , K}.

Empirical Evaluation Protocol (Template)
In this section we outline a general empirical protocol for evaluating the RAHF framework on
real financial data. The details (dataset choice, model hyperparameters, numerical results)
14

can be instantiated in future work.
5.1
Data and Preprocessing
A typical empirical setup could be:
• Universe: Daily returns for a major equity index (e.g., S&P 500 ETF) or a crosssection of large-cap stocks.
• Sample period: A multi-year horizon (e.g., 10–20 years) including both calm and
crisis periods.
• Preprocessing: Log-returns, alignment of trading days, winsorization of extreme
outliers if desired.
5.2
Rolling-Window Backtesting
We use a rolling-window procedure:
(1) Use a fixed-length estimation window (e.g., 3 years) to fit base linear models and nonlinear residual models.
(2) Generate one-step-ahead forecasts for the next day.
(3) Move the window forward by one day and repeat.
This yields a sequence of out-of-sample forecasts and realized returns.
5.3
Performance Metrics
For point forecasts of returns or volatility, we can use:
• Root mean squared error (RMSE)
• Mean absolute error (MAE)
For portfolio strategies driven by forecasts, relevant metrics include:
• Annualized mean return and volatility
• Sharpe ratio
• Maximum drawdown
• Hit rate for VaR backtesting (e.g., Kupiec test)

Challenges and Limitations
Despite the flexibility of RAHF and modern time series tools, several challenges remain when
applying them in practice.
6.1
Non-Stationarity and Structural Breaks
Even regime-switching models with a fixed number of regimes may fail to capture structural
changes in market dynamics. Models must be regularly re-estimated, and performance can
16

deteriorate rapidly after regime shifts.
6.2
Overfitting and Model Complexity
Nonlinear models, especially deep networks, are prone to overfitting unless strong regularization, careful validation, and meaningful feature engineering are employed. Combining linear
and nonlinear components increases model complexity and must be justified by out-of-sample
gains.
6.3
Data Quality and Microstructure Noise
High-frequency data introduce microstructure noise, while daily data may hide intraday
dynamics. Missing data, corporate actions and survivorship bias can all distort empirical
results if not handled carefully.
6.4
Transaction Costs and Market Impact
In a realistic trading setting, forecast improvements must be weighed against transaction
costs and potential market impact. A strategy with slightly higher forecast accuracy but
much higher turnover may perform worse after costs.
7
Conclusion
Time series analysis provides the quantitative backbone for many tasks in modern finance,
from forecasting and risk measurement to dynamic portfolio optimization. In this paper,
we revisited classical models such as ARIMA, GARCH and state-space models, and situated them alongside nonlinear machine learning approaches within a unified regime-aware
perspective.
We introduced the Regime-Aware Hybrid Forecasting (RAHF) framework, in which a
linear baseline model is complemented by a nonlinear residual learner that exploits latent
information about market regimes. We provided a detailed bias–variance analysis and conditions under which the hybrid model can strictly improve mean squared forecast error over
linear baselines, and we illustrated how RAHF can be instantiated for return forecasting,
volatility and risk estimation, and dynamic allocation.
Future work includes implementing the full empirical evaluation protocol on real-world equity or multi-asset data, exploring more structured latent-regime models (e.g., macro-driven
17

regimes), and extending the hybrid approach to multi-horizon forecasts and reinforcementlearning-based trading strategies. More broadly, combining the interpretability of classical
time series models with the flexibility of machine learning remains a promising direction for
quantitative finance.
