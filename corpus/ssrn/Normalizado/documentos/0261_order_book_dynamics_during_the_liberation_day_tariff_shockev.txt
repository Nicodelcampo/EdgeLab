Order Book Dynamics During the Liberation Day Tariff Shock:
Evidence from 20 NASDAQ Stocks
Boon Chuan Lim
Independent Researcher, Singapore
boonchuan@singapore.to
April 2026
Abstract
Using NASDAQ TotalView Level 3 data for 20 stocks, I document how order book microstructure
changed during the April 2025 Liberation Day tariff shock. Trading volume doubled (+101%), but
order composition shifted dramatically: median order lifetime collapsed from 120ms to 32ms (−73%),
sub-millisecond orders rose 18%, and cancel-to-trade ratios fell 27%. These patterns indicate a regime
shift from patient liquidity provision to aggressive, short-lived order placement. The effect is
heterogeneous: smaller-cap stocks (CMCSA +478%, CSCO +357%) experienced larger surges than
mega-caps (TSLA +21%). Cross-sectionally, the volume surge explains the lifetime collapse (r =
−0.91). This provides nanosecond-resolution evidence on how a trade policy shock transforms limit
order book dynamics.

1. Introduction
On April 2, 2025 — a date President Trump proclaimed “Liberation Day” — the United States announced the 
most sweeping tariff increase since the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, imposing a minimum 10% tariff on 
nearly all imports with higher rates for major trading partners (Executive Order 14257). The announcement 
triggered sharp equity declines, with the S&P 500 losing approximately $5 trillion in market value within two 
days (Reuters, April 4, 2025). A growing literature examines the macroeconomic and asset-pricing 
consequences of this shock (Kaczmarek, Demir, Rouatbi, and Zaremba, 2025). However, existing work has 
mostly focused on macroeconomic and asset-pricing effects rather than order-book microstructure.
This paper fills that gap. Using NASDAQ TotalView Level 3 Market-by-Order data from Databento 
(XNAS.ITCH schema), I analyze the order book dynamics of 20 liquid stocks on three days: April 1 (normal), 
April 4 (first full crisis day), and April 7 (peak crisis). The Level 3 data provides individual order-level 
messages with nanosecond timestamps, allowing measurement of order lifetimes, cancellation behavior, and 
participant composition at a granularity unavailable in standard trade-and-quote data.
I document five key findings. First, trading volume more than doubled (+101%). Second, median order 
lifetime collapsed from 120ms to 32ms (−73%), indicating a shift from patient limit orders to aggressive, 
short-lived strategies. Third, the share of ultra-fast orders (lifetime < 1ms) rose from 26% to 31%, suggesting 
increased high-frequency trading activity. Fourth, the cancel-to-trade ratio fell from 11.3 to 8.3 (−27%), as the 
surge in executed trades (+101%) outpaced the rise in cancellations (+47%). Fifth, the effect was 
heterogeneous: smaller-cap names (CMCSA +478%, CSCO +357%) experienced proportionally larger volume 
surges than mega-caps (TSLA +21%). Cross-sectionally, the correlation between volume surge and lifetime 
collapse is r = −0.91.

These findings contribute to the literature on market microstructure during crises, including the 2010 Flash 
Crash (Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi, and Tuzun, 2017) and European sovereign debt crises (Pelizzon, 
Subrahmanyam, Tomio, and Uno, 2016). This paper provides nanosecond-resolution evidence on how a major 
trade policy shock — rather than a financial or health crisis — transforms the internal dynamics of the limit 
order book.
2. Data
The sample covers 20 NASDAQ-listed stocks spanning technology, consumer discretionary, healthcare, 
communication, and consumer staples sectors. I extract one hour of continuous trading (14:00–15:00 UTC) on 
each of three days: April 1 (normal), April 4 (crisis day 1), and April 7 (crisis day 2). Data is obtained from 
Databento using the XNAS.ITCH feed with Market-by-Order (MBO) schema.
Data limitations: Files exceeding 4 million records are truncated to the first 30 minutes. GOOGL (April 7) and 
NVDA (April 4, 7) are excluded due to memory constraints. Results are robust to using only the first 30 
minutes for all stocks (available upon request). The total sample comprises approximately 45 million orderlevel records across 57 stock-days.

3. Results
3.1 Order Flow Composition
Table 2 compares order book statistics between the normal day and crisis days. The most striking change is the 
collapse in order lifetime: the median order survives only 32 milliseconds during the crisis, down from 120 
milliseconds on the normal day. This 73% reduction indicates that participants submit and cancel orders at 
dramatically higher rates, consistent with a shift from passive liquidity provision to aggressive quote updating 
under heightened uncertainty.

3.2 High-Frequency Trading Activity
The share of sub-millisecond orders rose from 26.2% to 31.0% (+18%). The decline in the cancel-to-trade ratio 
(11.3 to 8.3) reflects the surge in executed trades (+101%) outpacing the rise in cancellations (+47%). In 
levels, cancellations still rose sharply (from ~291K to ~428K per stock), but the efficiency of order submission 
increased as thinner books meant fewer orders expired unfilled.
3.3 Cross-Sectional Heterogeneity
The volume surge was heterogeneous. CMCSA (+478%), CSCO (+357%), and MDLZ (+260%) experienced 
the largest surges; TSLA (+21%), NFLX (+56%), and AMZN (+73%) the smallest. Stocks with normally 
lower trading activity experienced proportionally larger surges, consistent with thinner normal-day order books 
amplifying crisis-driven information flow.

3.5 Mechanism: Volume Drives Lifetime Collapse
Figure 2 plots the cross-sectional relationship between volume surge and order lifetime change. The correlation 
is r = −0.91: stocks with larger volume surges experienced larger lifetime collapses. This is consistent with an 
equilibrium shift where market makers reduce quote duration under heightened uncertainty and volume 
pressure. When order flow doubles, resting quotes face higher adverse selection risk, incentivizing participants 
to shorten exposure.

4. Conclusion
This paper provides nanosecond-resolution evidence on how the April 2025 Liberation Day tariff shock 
transformed NASDAQ order book dynamics. Order lifetimes collapsed 73%, HFT activity increased 18%, 
trading volume doubled, and the effect was heterogeneous across market capitalizations. The cross-sectional 
correlation (r = −0.91) between volume surge and lifetime collapse points to a mechanism: increased order 
flow raises adverse selection risk, forcing market makers to shorten quote duration. These findings 
complement the macroeconomic literature on Liberation Day by documenting the shock’s propagation through 
the microstructure of individual securities.
