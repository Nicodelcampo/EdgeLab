Revisiting the trading activity of high-frequency trading ﬁrms
around ultra-fast ﬂash events
October 1, 2024
Abstract
We investigate the behavior of High-Frequency Traders (HFTs) in the context of the fastest
and most extreme price movements that can be observed in the market, i.e. ultra-fast ﬂash
events, challenging the methodologies employed in academic and practitioner literature for
identifying sudden liquidity black holes. To reﬁne the price shock identiﬁcation methodology,
we introduce a new approach called Sequence-Based Flash Events (SFEs), which relies on
tick sequences instead of relying on predetermined ﬁxed-time intervals inside which all ﬂash
events in the sample are assumed to occur. This alternative methodology oﬀers the advantage of pinpointing the exact time and duration of the crash, which in turn provides a way
to more accurately deﬁne the windows of observation around it. We compare our sample
of Sequence-Based Flash Events (SFEs) with both the so-called ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ (MFCs)
as identiﬁed by the Nanex detection algorithm, and the so-called ‘extreme price movements’
(EPMs) as identiﬁed by Brogaard et al. (2018), using close and open prices as well as high
and low prices. Based on our sample of SFEs, we ﬁnd that there is no evidence that HFTs
trigger extreme price shocks. However, we ﬁnd that HFTs exacerbate SFEs by increasing net
imbalance in the direction of these shocks as they occur. Finally, we show that the choice of
the price shock identiﬁcation methodology is critical so that we urge regulators to exercise
caution and avoid hasty conclusions regarding the contribution of HFTs to price stability in
stressful market conditions.

Introduction
This study introduces a novel methodology whose purpose is both to identify extreme market shocks with greater precision and better analyze the role played by High-Frequency Traders
(HFTs) before, during, and after these events. We focus in particular on ultra-fast ﬂash events,
a subcategory of ‘ﬂash events’ which incorporates so-called ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ (MFCs) and ‘extreme price movements’ (EPMs). Our aim is to explore these events comprehensively, integrating
both industry and academic perspectives, so as to provide deeper insights into the dynamics of
ﬁnancial markets and the contribution of HFTs to market (in)stability.
Practitioners refer to the fastest and most extreme price movements as ‘ultrafast extreme
events’ (Johnson et al., 2013), ‘ﬂash equity failures’ or ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ (Nanex LLC, 2010).
They represent sudden and signiﬁcant changes in trade prices that adhere to predeﬁned rules.
Speciﬁcally, an MFC corresponds to a price change exceeding 0.8%, accompanied by at least
ten consecutive tick movements in the same direction before a change in the opposite direction
occurs, all within a duration of 1.5 seconds (Nanex LLC, 2010).
Academically, these events are referred to as ‘extreme price movements’ (EPMs), and correspond to very signiﬁcant shifts in the National Best Bid and Oﬀer (NBBO) midquotes (Brogaard
et al., 2018). They consist in multiple sequential trades and are retrospectively identiﬁed based
on a statistical approach, speciﬁcally utilizing the 99.9th percentile of the absolute return distribution.
Despite similarities, MFCs and EPMs diﬀer notably in terms of identiﬁcation methods, duration, and statistical properties. For example, there are notable diﬀerences in their identiﬁcation
methodologies (ex-ante for MFCs versus ex-post for EPMs), their duration (10-second time intervals for EPMs versus 1.5-second time intervals for MFCs), and descriptive statistics (lower
mean return for EPMs due to ﬁltering mechanisms). Consequently, MFCs appear to be more
extreme price movements than EPMs, despite the name of the latter. This prompts a need for
a uniﬁed approach.
Our study adopts a systematic approach, scrutinizing ultra-fast ﬂash events through various methodologies, including a novel one proposed herein. This aspect is critical, as existing
studies, typically constrained to using a single methodology, have produced contradictory results
regarding the behavior of HFTs during these events. Our study aims to discern whether these
discrepancies stem from the datasets used or the methodologies employed. We therefore test
whether these divergent perspectives are reconcilable, analyzing HFT behavior on both the same
stocks and over the same period.
2

The research process involves three key steps: (1) replicating practitioner-focused MFCs and
academic-focused EPMs; (2) introducing a new ‘sequence-based ﬂash events’ (SFEs) methodology; (3) comparing SFEs with both practitioner-focused MFCs and academic-focused EPMs.
Our novel methodology distinguishes itself by capturing ﬂash events without relying on ﬁxedtime windows, instead utilizing tick sequences to accurately identify the duration of each ﬂash
event and deﬁne precise observation windows. Our data include tick-by-tick data, millisecondlevel timestamps and a ﬂag indicating whether the liquidity provider or consumer is an HFT or
a non-high frequency trader (NHFT).
This paper contributes to the ﬁnance ﬁeld by systematically comparing diﬀerent price shock
identiﬁcation methodologies, including a more precise methodology that we detail in the next
Section. To the best of our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst study to highlight the inconsistency of
ﬁndings across methods.
Interestingly, we ﬁnd no evidence that HFTs trigger extreme price
shocks, but we also ﬁnd a discernible destabilizing eﬀect during crashes. Our ﬁndings also highlight the methodology-dependent nature of conclusions drawn about the contribution of HFTs
to market stability. Thus, we caution against premature regulatory judgments and advocate for
further research in this area.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a graphical and intuitive
analysis of ﬂash events to ease the understanding of our identiﬁcation methodology. Section 3
reviews the relevant literature on ﬂash events, mini ﬂash crashes and extreme price movements,
further delineating the contribution of our study. Section 4 introduces the data, presents the
diﬀerent identiﬁcation methods we use in the paper as well as the summary statistics, and deﬁnes
the diﬀerent variables. Section 5 is dedicated to the empirical analysis and presents our results.
Section 6 summarizes the diﬀerent robustness checks performed and addresses the limitations of
the paper. Section 7 concludes.

A visual illustration
MFCs are deﬁned as abrupt, extreme, and brief price ﬂuctuations that involve a minimum of
ten tick movements in a consistent direction, followed by a movement in the opposite direction,
partially or fully correcting themselves within a few seconds. These mini ﬂash crashes can be
considered as scaled-down versions of ﬂash crashes. As highlighted by Johnson et al. (2013)
however, they diﬀer from ﬂash crashes in two fundamental aspects. Firstly, MFCs have a considerably shorter duration, typically lasting only a few seconds, in contrast to ﬂash crashes that
can persist for several minutes. This abbreviated timeframe restricts the opportunity for human

To illustrate the concept, Figure 1 portrays an instance of a downward mini ﬂash crash that
impacted the Apple (AAPL) stock on January 22, 2008. This crash unfolded within a remarkably
short time span of 1180 milliseconds (starting at 10:02:24.100 and ending at 10:02:25.280). The
transaction price experienced a rapid decline of 5.06% within this compressed interval before
promptly rebounding to its initial level.
As deﬁned in Johnson et al. (2013) and Nanex (2010), the above-mentioned MFCs are ﬁltered
out based on three rules. There must be a price change exceeding 0.8%, with at least ten tick
movements in the same direction before ticking in the other direction. This price change must
happen within 1.5 seconds.
Brogaard et al.(2018) instead focus on EPMs, which are computed as returns above the
99.9th percentile of the empirical distribution of absolute returns. In total, they identify 45,200
EPMs at 10 second-intervals on the 40 large stocks of the Nasdaq HFT database over the period
2008-2010. The median absolute return of an EPM is 0.436% at pre-speciﬁed 10-second time
intervals.
Both methodologies could be questioned. First, we could question whether Brogaard et al.
(2018) really capture extreme price movements since they use ﬁxed-time windows and potentially

price movements can be extremely short-lived (a few milliseconds). Even when they change the

bottom of a shock since the time window is ﬁxed. In addition, the shorter the time interval, the

To better understand why it may be misleading, let us consider a down crash for example
and take two diﬀerent undesirable elements. First, when there is a larger drop within the interval
(larger than the drop between the open and close prices of the interval), then Brogaard et al.
(2018) underestimate the down crash and include some price correction in their EPM. In other
words, resiliency may already be occurring within the interval of the EPM. Second, when there
is a larger drop just after the close price of the interval, Brogaard et al. also underestimate the
down crash and pollute the next interval since the crash has not ended yet.

Second, we could also question Nanex identiﬁcation methodology, which appears to be arbitrary. For example, a price change of 90% happening in less than 10 consecutive tick movements
in the same direction would not qualify as an MFC while a price change slightly exceeding 0.8%
in 10 consecutive ticks would. In the same way, a price change of 90% happening in a bit more
than 1.5 seconds would not qualify as an MFC while a price change slightly exceeding 0.8% in
less than 1.5 seconds would.
In this paper, we aim to alleviate some of the drawbacks of these two approaches to better
capture ﬂash events. The innovation relies on the fact that we ﬁrst identify the extreme price
shock, that we call ‘sequence-based ﬂash event’ (SFE) and then redeﬁne the window around it
without relying on any predetermined ﬁxed-time interval for the identiﬁcation of the shock. Our
methodology consists in thinking in terms of sequences rather than in terms of interval. To do
so, we rely on the equity curve. As long as the equity curve does not reverse, we stay in the same
sequence. Once the equity curve changes direction, then we move to the next sequence. Figure
3 oﬀers a visual illustration of how SFEs work.

Brief literature review
In this section, we introduce the relevant literature focusing in turn on the identiﬁcation and
measurement of ultra-fast ﬂash events, the impact of HFTs on the market and the role played
by HFTs during ﬂash events and ultra-fast ﬂash events.
Identiﬁcation and measurement of ultra-fast ﬂash events
Our study focuses on ‘ultra-fast ﬂash crashes’ which are shorter in duration when compared
to ‘ﬂash crashes’, as indicated in Figure 4, even though both belong to the category of ‘ﬂash
events’.
Figure 4: Decomposition of ﬂash events
7

Within the body of literature focusing on ultra-fast ﬂash events, these events are referred to
as either ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ (MFCs)2, when they meet (at least partially) the tick, time and
price change criteria deﬁned by Nanex (2010), or ‘extreme price movements’ (EPMs) when they
meet the price change criterion deﬁned by Brogaard et al. (2018).
According to Nanex (2010), an MFC must include at least 10 tick movements in the same
direction before ticking in the other direction, all within 1.5 seconds, and with an absolute price
change exceeding 0.8%. The tick criterion is always set at 10 ticks in the literature as in Golub
et al. (2012), Johnson et al. (2013) and Ozenbas & Schwartz (2018). The duration criterion is
typically set at 1.5 seconds (Nanex, 2010) but sometimes extended as in Braun et al. (2018),
F´elez-Vinas (2018), or Laly & Petitjean (2020). In addition, F´elez-Vinas (2018) adds a recovery
criterion in her identiﬁcation of MFCs since the stock has to recover at least 90% of the initial
price change within 300 seconds.
According to Brogaard et al. (2018), an EPM is identiﬁed as a 10-second interval whose return
belongs to the 99.9th percentile of the absolute return distribution. This 10-second interval has
been chosen to capture brief liquidity dislocations that are relevant enough in terms of magnitude.
To the best of our knowledge, MFCs were ﬁrst mentioned in the literature by Johnson et al.
(2013), who report 18,520 MFCs in the U.S. equity markets alone between January 3, 2006 and
February 3, 2011, i.e. about 15 MFCs per day, while F´elez-Vinas (2018) reports 2,105 MFCs
on the Spanish IBEX35 exchange and 947 MFCs on the Italian FTSE MIB exchange over the
period November 2012-April 2013, i.e. about 17 MFCs per day on the Italian exchange and
about 8 MFCs per day on the Spanish exchange. Both studies reveal that MFCs are a global
phenomenon and not just a U.S. phenomenon. As for EPMs, they were ﬁrst mentioned in the
literature by Longin (2000), who describes them as either market corrections occurring during
ordinary periods or crashes occurring during extraordinary periods, but it is Brogaard et al.
(2018) who put EPMs in the context of high-frequency trading determining the price change rule
to use so as to capture these ‘ultra-fast ﬂash crashes’ ex-post.
Finally, Aquilina et al. (2018) identify extreme events that they call ‘mini ﬂash crashes/rallies’
on the UK equity market over the period January 2014-June 2015. Contrary to Nanex (2010),
Aquilina et al. (2018) deﬁne ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ as large price movements that revert within a
short time window and during which there is a high level of traded volume. They end up with
a total of 40 ﬂash events whose drop or spike duration can last up to 10 minutes. Contrary
to Aquilina et al. (2018), we believe that these intraday market shocks are traditional ﬂash

crashes and do not belong to the universe of ultra-fast liquidity black holes that we study in this

The impact of HFTs on the market
Many beneﬁts have been associated with the arrival of HFTs in electronic markets such as
a decrease in spreads (Stoll 2014, Boehmer et al. 2021), a decrease in transaction costs (Jones
2013), a decrease in short-term volatility (Hagstr¨omer & Nord´en 2013, Hasbrouck & Saar 2013)
as well as an improvement in price eﬃciency (Carrion 2013, Brogaard et al. 2014) so that some of
the trading strategies used by HFTs such as statistical arbitrage, directional trading and market
making strategies are perceived as mostly beneﬁcial in the literature (Zaharudin et al. 2022).
However, some major drawbacks have also been associated with the presence of HFTs and the
most problematic drawback of all is probably the fact that HFTs are also suspected of harming
modern automated markets (Hasbrouck & Saar 2013), thus generating externalities related to
market quality, liquidity and volatility.
Regarding the negative impact of HFTs on market quality and liquidity, Hirschey (2021)
provides evidence that HFTs identify patterns in past trades and orders that allow them to anticipate and trade ahead of the order ﬂow of other investors (front-running them) thus increasing
non-HFT trading costs. Similarly, Goldstein et al. (2023) ﬁnd that HFTs impose a welfare externality by selectively crowding out the most proﬁtable limit orders on the Australian Securities
Exchange. Indeed, the authors show that HFTs strategically use limit order book information
to supply liquidity on the thick side of the order book and demand liquidity from the thin side,
especially during periods of high volatility and when trading speed increases, which echoes both
Menkveld (2014), who argues that the migration to electronic trading coincided with the occasional disappearance of liquidity supply, and Menkveld & Zoican (2017), who claim that a
faster exchange does not necessarily improve liquidity especially when price quotes are met with
‘speculative high-frequency bandits’. Finally, Abreu (2022) shows that while HFTs do improve
market liquidity on average, they do it at the expense of non-HFTs. In other words, HFTs are
the great beneﬁciaries of this enhanced market liquidity but the other market participants have
to satisfy themselves with a worsened level of liquidity.
Regarding the negative impact of HFTs on volatility, Breckenfelder (2019) ﬁnds that the
competition between HFTs increases intraday volatility. Moreover, the author shows that an
increase in HFT competition increases the speculative behavior of HFTs, reduces liquidity and
thus increases short-term volatility (implicitly leading to more frequent intraday price shocks).
9

In the same vein, Boehmer et al. (2018) show that HFTs have both very correlated trading
strategies and a dominating presence so that their actions can exacerbate price movements and
increase short-term volatility, which is especially problematic due to the fact that, as explained
by Goldstein et al. (2014), ‘errant or poorly designed HFT programs without necessary risk
controls can lead to occasional shocks or disruptive events that aﬀect markets and, perhaps as
importantly, the general public’s perception of them’ (p. 198). Finally, Shkilko & Sokolov (2020),
in their study of a series of exogenous weather episodes (rain or snow) that temporarily remove
the speed advantage of the fastest traders (by disrupting their microwave networks) ﬁnd that a
higher HFT participation resulting from the absence of weather precipitation is associated with
a higher level of volatility. Again, the ﬁndings of these authors seem to imply that the more
HFTs being active in the market, the more price shocks are likely to occur. Overall, while some
trading strategies used by HFTs are considered as beneﬁcial most of the time, other strategies are
considered as unequivocally harmful. These strategies include front-running, order anticipation,
quote matching, spooﬁng, layering and quote stuﬃng (Zaharudin et al. 2022).
Role played by HFTs during ﬂash events and ultra-fast ﬂash events
On a microstructural level, HFTs are especially suspected of having a harmful behavior during
sudden intraday shocks as pointed out by Bellia et al. (2022) who show that electronic designated
market makers consume liquidity when it is most needed. As such, there seems to exist a trade-oﬀ
between the greater liquidity and eﬃciency provided by market making HFTs in normal times,
and the disruptive consequences of their quoting and trading activity in times of market stress.
It is important at this stage to put the emphasis on the fact that intraday market shocks can take
two forms, the form of ‘ﬂash crashes’, which can be deﬁned as sudden and extreme (up or down)
price changes that partially or totally self-correct within a maximum of a few dozen minutes,
or the form of ‘ultra-fast ﬂash crashes’ represented by ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ and ‘extreme price
movements’ - sometimes called ‘extreme price movements with reversals’ as in Steﬀen (2023) -
which are faster versions of ‘ﬂash crashes’ that partially or totally self-correct within a few seconds
only. Many such ﬂash crashes have occurred in the past decade, whether in equities (2010, 2015,
2023 U.S. equity ﬂash crashes), in ﬁxed income (2014 Japanese government bond ﬂash crash,
2014 U.S. government bond ﬂash crash, 2015 German bond ﬂash crash, 2018 Italian bond ﬂash
crash), in currencies (2015 USD ﬂash crash, 2016 GBP ﬂash crash), in commodities (as reported
by Massad, 2015)3 or more recently in cryptocurrencies. Despite the implementation of various
protective measures, such as limit-up/limit-down bands and circuit breaker updates in the U.S.

stock market, ﬂash crashes continue to occur and are expected to persist in the future. This is
evidenced by the ﬂash crash that occurred on January 24, 2023 during which highly liquid U.S.
stocks experienced a signiﬁcant drop of approximately 10% at the market open, followed by a
rapid recovery, in spite of the existing safeguards.
Flash events seem to have become more frequent with the emergence of high-frequency trading. For example, Leal et al. (2016) ﬁnd that they do not occur when the market (in their
agent-based model) is populated with low-frequency traders only. While some studies have examined the role played by HFTs during ﬂash events (Kirilenko et al. 2017, Aldrich et al. 2017,
Menkveld & Yueshen 2019), the literature regarding the role played by HFTs during the fastest
and most extreme price shocks (during ultra-fast ﬂash events) is much more limited.
Within the body of literature focusing on the famous May 6, 2010 Flash Crash, ﬁndings
regarding the role played by HFTs point to similar conclusions. In their study of the behavior of
HFTs in the E-mini S&P500 futures market during the Flash Crash, Kirilenko et al. (2017) ﬁnd
that HFTs did not cause the crash, the large automated selling program of a mutual fund later
on identiﬁed as Waddell & Reed did. They also ﬁnd that HFTs did not fundamentally change
their trading pattern during the crash. However, the authors note that just before the market
was paused for 5 seconds, HFTs liquidated 2,000 contracts accumulated earlier (in an already
illiquid market), coinciding with a signiﬁcant additional price decline, i.e. the most abrupt price
decline of the crash. On the contrary, traditional market makers (non-HFTs by extension) did
not liquidate their accumulated inventory. In that sense, HFTs contributed to the extent of the
Flash Crash. Their ﬁndings are in line with the empirical study led by Aldrich et al. (2017).
As for Menkveld & Yueshen (2019), they conclude that the crash cannot be attributed to the
mutual fund alone and that it is rather the result of the interaction between market participants
that degenerated into a ﬂash crash. This interpretation is in line with the ﬁndings of Demirer
et al. (2019) who conclude that ‘the market switched to a herding state just before the crash and
remained in this state over the duration and aftermath of the crash’. In this case, the authors
establish a clear link between ﬂash events and herding among market participants, but nothing
more is said about the role played by HFTs.
In a more recent working paper focusing on ﬂash crashes, Bellia et al. (2022) use the econometric ‘drift burst’ approach of Christensen et al. (2022) to identify 148 ﬂash crashes on 37 liquid
French stocks belonging to the CAC40 index and traded on NYSE-Euronext Paris in 2013. The
‘drift burst’ hypothesis postulates that there exist short-lived locally explosive trends in the price
paths of ﬁnancial assets (in other words periods when the price is trending sharply) as characterized by ﬂash events. They investigate the behavior of diﬀerent groups of traders during the
identiﬁed ﬂash crashes and ﬁnd that designated market makers (DMMs), most of them HFTs, do
11

not provide enough liquidity to avoid ﬂash crashes occurring in isolated stocks and that they even
consume liquidity when crashes aﬀect several stocks at once. Moreover, they ﬁnd that DMMs do
not mitigate the eﬀects of illiquidity shocks during ﬂash crashes, which contradicts somewhat the
ﬁndings of Anand & Venkataraman (2016), Clark-Joseph et al. (2017) and Bessembinder et al.
(2020). Finally, the authors show that in most cases ﬂash crashes are originated by either proprietary trading of investment banks or by client trading from investment banks via sponsored
access. The crash is then ampliﬁed by the reaction of investment bank DMMs, in particular
when several stocks are impacted simultaneously.
Finally, the literature focusing on ultra-fast ﬂash events points to diﬀerent conclusions since,
as pointed out by Steﬀen (2023), there is no consensus regarding the role played by HFTs during
’ultra-fast ﬂash crashes’. On the one hand, Brogaard et al. (2018), in their empirical study of
EPMs, ﬁnd that there is little evidence of HFTs causing EPMs. Indeed, the authors conclude
that HFTs are net suppliers of liquidity to non-HFTs during EPMs on single stocks and that
HFTs do supply liquidity during the most extreme EPMs. However, they also concede that
when several stocks experience simultaneous EPMs, then HFT liquidity demand dominates their
supply so that HFTs de facto become net demanders of liquidity.
On the other hand, the
rare papers focusing on MFCs are more skeptical about the role played by HFTs during these
liquidity black holes and speculate that HFTs may have a disruptive inﬂuence. For example,
Johnson et al. (2013) argue that their ﬁndings are consistent with the emergence of ’crowds of
predatory algorithms’ that destabilize the market and Golub et al. (2012) postulate that ’given
the speed and the magnitude of the crashes, it appears likely that Mini Flash Crashes are caused
by HFT activity’, which echoes Gao & Mizrach (2016) who ﬁnd that HFT trading activity raises
the probability of market breaks. While HFTs are portrayed as having a beneﬁcial behavior
during EPMs, they tend to be portrayed as having a detrimental behavior during MFCs. It is
thus crucial to test whether these conﬂicting views are reconcilable.

4.1
Data and sample
We obtain data from both Tickdata and Nasdaq and work with both datasets together. On the
one hand, we use tick-by-tick data timestamped to the millisecond on the NBBO from Tickdata
12

for 74 stocks.4 The data from Tickdata account for all transactions on U.S. stock exchanges5
for the 74 stocks at our disposal. On the other hand, we use tick-by-tick data timestamped
to the millisecond on trades from Nasdaq OMX for the same 74 stocks, as compared to 40
stocks in Brogaard et al. (2018). This dataset is the so-called Nasdaq HFT dataset provided
to academics under a non-disclosure agreement. The dataset is the same as in O’Hara et al.
(2014), Carrion (2013), Gerig (2015), Brogaard et al. (2017), Brogaard et al. (2018) and Hirschey
(2021). It includes a ﬂag which enables us to know if the liquidity consumer/supplier is a highfrequency trader (HFT) or a non-high frequency trader (NHFT). A code (HH, HN, NH, NN) is
indeed associated with each transaction on Nasdaq. This ﬂag is not provided by the other stock
exchanges. The ﬁrst letter of the code refers to the liquidity seeker and the second letter of the
code refers to the liquidity supplier. For example, when a transaction occurs between an HFT
and an NHFT, the code can either be HN or NH depending on who is on the liquidity demand
side and who is on the liquidity supply side. HN indicates that the HFT demands liquidity while
the NHFT supplies liquidity. Conversely, NH indicates that the NHFT demands liquidity while
the HFT supplies liquidity.
We proceed with these two datasets as follows. We ﬁrst use the aggregated data from Tickdata
to identify price shocks since they originate from successive transactions which are likely to occur
on diﬀerent exchanges. Second, we use the Nasdaq HFT dataset to observe the behavior of HFTs
around and during these shocks. Nasdaq is by far the U.S. exchange with the highest market
share (36%) based on the number of trades for the 74 stocks included in our sample, which makes
a focus on Nasdaq all the more relevant. Third, in terms of window-sizing around the shock, we
use a window of 30 seconds around each extreme price shock, i.e. ten 1.5-second intervals before
and after the shock. Fourth, we use transaction prices in the identiﬁcation of MFCs and SFEs in
order to take into account the full magnitude of each crash (from top to bottom or from bottom
to top), but we still use midquotes in the identiﬁcation of EPMs to be in line with Brogaard et

4.2
Identiﬁcation of ﬂash events
We begin by revisiting the ﬁrst methodology proposed by Nanex (2010), which is used to
identify ‘mini-ﬂash crashes’ referred to as ‘MFCs’ thereafter. According to Nanex (2010), an
MFC is characterized by at least 10 consecutive tick movements in the same direction (excluding
trades with no tick change) occurring within a duration of 1.5 seconds, and involving a price
shock exceeding 0.8%. This procedure is also followed by Golub et al. (2012) and Johnson et al.
(2013). However, the selection criteria, such as the 0.8% absolute return threshold, are arbitrarily
deﬁned.
To address this fact, we propose an alternative identiﬁcation methodology based on ‘sequencebased ﬂash events’ (SFEs). In this approach, we eliminate the price change rule and consider
all sequences, regardless of their return, before ranking them using percentiles.
As with the
‘extreme price movements’ (EPMs) deﬁned by Brogaard et al. (2018), we keep the most extreme
sequences with an absolute log return surpassing the 99.9th percentile of the absolute log return
distribution. Unlike Brogaard et al. (2018) however, we still incorporate the tick rule from Nanex
(2010) to capture fast and abrupt shocks. As long as the equity (or tick) curve remains in the
same direction, we consider it as part of the same sequence.
Importantly, our SFEs are exclusively determined by their duration and are not constrained
by predetermined ﬁxed-time intervals. This is not the case for EPMs. They are not only based on
returns from open to close (OC) prices but are also deﬁned within ﬁxed-time intervals established
at the start of the day. Consequently, these OC-EPMs may not accurately represent the true
magnitude of the price shock, as demonstrated in Section 2. To better capture the magnitude of
these EPMs, we propose to use the high and low (HL) prices observed during the time window.
We refer to these modiﬁed EPMs as ‘HL-EPMs’ in the subsequent analysis. Unlike EPMs based
solely on open and close prices, HL-EPMs capture the true amplitude of the shock while still
being within the same ﬁxed-time interval. It is important to note that even HL-EPMs may not
fully capture the entire magnitude of the shock if it spans across two time intervals. There is
never such an issue in our sample of SFEs.
In our time series analysis, we compare all methodologies (MFCs, SFEs, OC-EPMs, and
HL-EPMs) by using a duration of 1.5 seconds for the windows of observation around the ﬂash
events.6
6See Section 5.5 for the robustness checks.
Among others, we explore alternative time intervals of 1 second,
following Brogaard et al. (2018), and 2 seconds, following F´elez-Vinas (2018), with similar conclusions.

4.3
Variables
We present below how we compute the main variables of the paper. These variables are either
trading activity measures, HFT participation measures, liquidity measures or net HFT activity
measures.
Liquidity measures
We measure the order book liquidity through four diﬀerent metrics: depth at best prices
(Depth), dollar depth at best prices (Dollar Depth), bid-ask spread (Quoted Spread) and relative
bid-ask spread (Relative Spread).
We deﬁne Relative Spread as:
RelativeSpreadi,t = (BestAskPricei,t −BestBidPricei,t)/Midquotei,t
(4.1)
where BestBidPricei,t and BestAskPricei,t represent the best bid and ask prices of the order
book respectively, and where i represents the stock and t represents the time of the quote update.
The Midquote is deﬁned as the average of the best bid and best ask prices.
Trading activity measures
We measure trading activity through three diﬀerent metrics: number of trades (Total Trades),
dollar volume (Dollar Volume) and share volume (Share Volume).
The ﬁrst metric, Total Trades, measures the total number of trades per stock per interval
(<1.5 seconds in Panel B and =1.5 seconds in Panels A and C of Table 1 as well as in panels
A, D and E of Table 2). The second metric, Dollar Volume, measures the average dollar volume
per stock per interval. The third metric, Share Volume, measures the average share volume per
stock per interval.
HFT participation measures
We measure the participation of HFTs through three diﬀerent metrics: proportion of HFT
trades (Proportion HFT Trades), proportion of HFT shares (Proportion HFT Shares), Proportion of HFT volume (Proportion HFT Volume) following Carrion (2013).
15

First, we deﬁne Proportion of HFT Trades as:
ProportionHFTtradesi,t = (HHi,t)/(HHi,t + NNi,t),
(4.2)
based on Nasdaq trades.
Second, we deﬁne Proportion of HFT Shares as:
ProportionHFTsharesi,t = (HHi,t)/(HHi,t + NNi,t),
(4.3)
based on Nasdaq shares traded.
And third, we deﬁne Proportion of HFT Volume as:
ProportionHFTvolumei,t = (HHi,t)/(HHi,t + NNi,t),
(4.4)
based on Nasdaq dollar volume.
Net HFT activity measures
We capture HFT and NHFT trading activity around price shocks via a measure of trade
imbalance (a proxy for their respective behavior) following Brogaard et al. (2018). We compute
trade imbalance for both HFTs and NHFTs in the following way:
(N)HFT D = (N)HFT D+ −(N)HFT D−
(4.5)
(N)HFT S = (N)HFT S+ −(N)HFT S−
(4.6)
where (N)HFT D represents the liquidity demanded by (N)HFTs, where (N)HFT S represents
the liquidity supplied by (N)HFTs, where (N)HFT D+ and (N)HFT S+ represent the liquidity
demanded and supplied in the direction of the crash, and where (N)HFT D−and (N)HFT S−
represent the liquidity demanded and supplied in the opposite direction of the crash.
We then compute the net imbalance for both HFTs (HFT NET ) and NHFTs (NHFT NET ),
HFT NET being the sum of HFT D and HFT S, and NHFT NET being the sum of NHFT D
and NHFT S. Net imbalance informs us on the direction of net trading activity vis-`a-vis the
crash direction. A positive net imbalance implies trading activity in the direction of the crash
on an aggregate basis. This hints at ‘vicious’, destabilizing trading conduct. On the contrary,
a negative net imbalance implies trading activity in the opposite direction of the crash on an
aggregate basis. This points to ‘virtuous’, market-stabilizing activity.

4.5
Methodology
We conduct a three-step empirical analysis. First, we conduct an event study so as to assess
the liquidity dynamics of HFTs and NHFTs around ﬂash events, without controlling for any
factors. Then, we conduct a contemporaneous multivariate regression analysis, zooming in on
the occurrence of these ﬂash events while controlling for additional drivers. Finally, we look into
the variables that potentially conduct to the occurrence of future crashes, implementing a logistic
regression framework (logit). For ease of comparison, we ﬁrst focus on MFCs and SFEs and then
replicate the three-step empirical analysis on EPMs in a separate section in order to compare all
the diﬀerent methodologies more easily (MFCs vs SFEs vs OC-EPMs vs HL-EPMs).
While ﬂash events in ﬁnancial markets can be triggered by a combination of factors generating a sudden imbalance between supply and demand such as algorithmic trading or market
manipulation, we do not control for these trading activity drivers for the following reasons.7 As
in Brogaard et al. (2018), we are unable to control for market manipulation at the HFT ﬁrm
level because Nasdaq data are aggregated. To the best of our knowledge, there is also no speciﬁc
ﬂag for non-HFT algorithmic trades, preventing us from controlling for the speciﬁc behaviors
of algorithmic traders as opposed to those of pure HFT traders. In any case, analyzing HFT
behavior during ultra-fast ﬂash events is relevant, regardless of whether the trigger is an imbalance caused by non-HFT algorithmic traders or some form of market manipulation. Indeed, the
reaction of HFTs and NHFTs during and after the crash is at least as important as what triggers
the crash.
7Technical glitches are ﬁltered out in our SFE methodology since we eliminate the price change rule and keep
the most extreme sequences of consecutive tick movements with an absolute log return surpassing the 99.9th
percentile of the absolute log return distribution. In addition, we focus on liquidity-driven ﬂash events and ﬁlter
out information-driven, news-related shocks since we require a 100% retracement in the second sequence in our
SFE methodology.

5
Empirical analysis
5.1
HFT activity around MFC and SFE events
Our initial approach involves an event study, designed to evaluate the liquidity behavior of
HFTs and NHFTs during MFCs and SFEs.
We aim to understand their respective trading
activity by quantifying net directional trade imbalance, as conceptualized by Brogaard et al.
(2018).8
A positive net imbalance (HFT NET > 0) implies trading activity in the direction
of the crash on an aggregate basis, which can destabilize the market and lead to liquidity black
holes, whether it is observed during the pre-crash phase (crash triggering), the crash phase (crash
exacerbation) or during the post-crash phase (resiliency prevention). On the contrary, a negative
net imbalance (HFT NET < 0) implies trading activity in the opposite direction of the crash on
an aggregate basis, which helps stabilize the market in the pre-crash phase (crash prevention),
the crash phase (crash absorption) or during the post-crash phase (resiliency promotion).
We compute net imbalance for both types of traders (HFTs and NHFTs) at time intervals
t −2, t −1, t, t + 1 and t + 2 for MFCs and SFEs, using ﬁxed 1.5-second intervals. We focus in
particular on time intervals t−1, t and t+1, corresponding to the pre-crash, crash and post-crash
phases respectively. Time intervals t −2 and t + 2 are only included in the analysis so as to see
if the pattern observed at t −1 and t + 1 is persistent when compared to the prior or successive
interval.
We apply a ﬁlter to the MFC and SFE samples in order to prevent consecutive crashes to
pollute the diﬀerent windows from time interval t −2 to time interval t + 2. For example, if a
crash occurs at time interval t and another crash occurs at time interval t + 1 then both crashes
are removed from our sample so as to prevent any bias in the computation of the directional
trade imbalance metrics. Moreover, we remove overlapping crashes (across stocks) with the aim
of preventing imbricated crashes to pollute our results. We end up with ﬁnal samples of 419
MFCs and 5,491 SFEs after the cleaning process.

5.1.1
MFCs
When using the MFC methodology, HFT NET is not statistically signiﬁcant in any time window, which implies that one cannot reject the null hypothesis according to which net imbalance
from both HFTs and NHFTs is zero in the population. As such, the MFC methodology does not
tell us much about the liquidity dynamics during crashes. As a consequence, due to the lack of
statistical signiﬁcance, one cannot conclude who among HFTs and NHFTs triggers MFCs, who
exacerbates the crash and who enables stock prices to recover.
23

5.1.2
SFEs
When using the SFE methodology, HFT NET becomes highly statistically signiﬁcant in all
time windows.
This is due to both the fact we are able to pinpoint the exact time of the
crash while reconstructing the window around the crash, and the fact that our SFE sample
(5,491 crashes) is bigger than our MFC sample (419 crashes), which together improve the overall
signiﬁcance level.
We focus in turn on the pre-crash, crash and post-crash phases. Just before the crash, at time
interval t−1, we ﬁnd that HFT NET is negative (-962.6***) and statistically signiﬁcant at the 1%
level, which implies that HFTs trade on an aggregate basis in the opposite direction of the crash
to come and help stabilize the market price. As such, they do not trigger the crash. During the
crash, at time interval t, HFT NET is again negative (-222.9***) and statistically signiﬁcant at
the 1% level, which implies that HFTs trade in the opposite direction of the crash and contribute
to price stability. As a consequence, HFTs do not exacerbate the crash. On the contrary, NHFTs
are the ones trading in the direction of the crash. We also note that HFTs supply twice more
liquidity during the crash (441.5***/218.6***=2) in the opposite direction of the crash when
compared to NHFTs while also consuming twice less liquidity (=218.6***/441.5***=0.5) in the
direction of the crash when compared to NHFTs. Finally, right after the crash, at time interval
t + 1, HFT NET is again negative (-680.9***) and statistically signiﬁcant at the 1% level, which
implies that HFTs keep trading in the opposite direction of the crash. By doing so, they do
contribute to the price recovery and market stability.
Based on this event study on SFEs, HFTs contribute to price stability in all diﬀerent event
windows.
As revealed in the next section however, we ﬁnd diﬀerent results when we run a
multivariate regression analysis to account for all the key trading activity drivers prevailing at
the time of the ﬂash event.

5.4.2
HFT activity and other key trading drivers during EPM events
In this section, we focus on the trading activity of HFTs during EPMs and control for all the
key trading drivers. We run the same multivariate regressions as in section 5.2. Results of the
diﬀerent regressions are presented in Table 8.
We ﬁnd very little evidence that ﬂash events are related to the trading activity of HFTs. The
only case (out of six) where the net trading activity of HFTs is destabilizing (because it goes in
the same direction as the shock) is in the model speciﬁcation with OC-EPMs and extreme hours.
There is nevertheless no observed destabilizing activity for OC-EPMs that intervene during
extreme hours. On the contrary, the interaction variable (EPMOC ∗EXTREME HOURS) is
signiﬁcant and the size and negative sign of its coeﬃcient tell us that HFTs help stabilize the
market during the price shocks that intervene precisely during extreme hours. Except for this
special case, there is overall insuﬃcient statistical evidence to suggest that HFT activity varies
signiﬁcantly when extreme price movements occur in the market. Finally, we note that the overall
statistical signiﬁcance of the models is lower when the EPM methodology is used instead of ours,
based on SFEs.

5.5
Robustness checks
In the preceding sections, we conducted a parallel analysis on three types of market events:
Mini Flash Crashes (MFCs), Extreme Price Movements (both OC-EPMs and HL-EPMs), and
Sequence-Based Flash Events (SFEs), speciﬁcally examining their similarities and diﬀerences. By
doing so, we checked if our results were similar across methodologies or conversely methodology
speciﬁc.
We found diﬀerent results depending on the price shock identiﬁcation methodology
used, which implies that concluding on the virtuousness or viciousness of HFTs may be primarily
methodology dependent. We also performed additional analyses using alternative time intervals
of 1 second and 2 seconds, in addition to our base time interval of 1.5 seconds. Regardless of the
time interval considered, our results remained highly consistent.
Our study focused on ultra-fast ﬂash events (MFCs, EPMs, SFEs), a subcategory of ﬂash
events, where the proportion of transactions occurring on Nasdaq during the interval constituted
at least 50% of all transactions on U.S. stock exchanges, thus ﬁltering out price shocks that were
not prevalent on Nasdaq. However, as a robustness check, we also conducted regressions on the
full sample, which encompassed ﬂash events with transaction proportions ranging from 0% to
100% on Nasdaq. There was no signiﬁcant change in the results. This is due to the fact Nasdaq
is by far the prevalent stock exchange in our database.
Regarding the event window, we initially employed a 15-second (10 intervals) window before
and after the crash. However, we also conducted regressions on 12-second (8 intervals) and 1837

second (12 intervals) windows. Regardless of the duration of the event window, our ﬁndings
remained consistent.
6
Conclusion
Our study investigates the relationship between High-Frequency Traders (HFTs) and ultrafast ﬂash events, challenging the methodologies employed in academic and practitioner literature
for identifying sudden liquidity black holes.
We question the criteria used for categorizing ‘mini ﬂash crashes’ (MFCs) based on consecutive tick movements and percentage price changes. In particular, we question the requirements
of 10 consecutive tick movements in the same direction of the crash and a price change of 0.8%.
This practically means, for example, that a price change of 90% happening in less than 10 consecutive tick movements in the same direction would not qualify as an MFC while a price change
of 1% in 10 consecutive ticks would.
We also critique the accuracy of estimating ‘extreme price movements’ (EPMs) by relying
on predetermined ﬁxed-time intervals, which may overlook important market dynamics. EPMs
typically underestimate the magnitude of ﬂash events since they rely on predetermined ﬁxed-time
intervals for the identiﬁcation of the shock (mechanically starting at the open of the market).
This implies that price resiliency may already be occurring within the time interval of the EPM
or that the shock may continue and pollute the next time interval.
To reﬁne the price shock identiﬁcation methodology, we introduce a new approach called
‘sequence-based ﬂash events’ (SFEs), which focuses on sequences rather than on ﬁxed intervals.
We pinpoint the exact time and duration of the crash and then build a window around it. As
long as the tick curve does not reverse, we stay in the same sequence irrespective of the length
of the time interval. These SFEs better capture the full extent of the shocks hitting the market.
Furthermore, we compare our sample of SFEs with MFCs and EPMs, employing close and open
prices (OC), which replicates the original methodology, but we also suggest using high and low
(HL) prices, which better capture the extent of the shock within a predetermined time interval.
Our event studies around SFEs do not indicate that HFTs contribute to price instability.
Since these event studies do not account for alternative trading and liquidity drivers such as
relative spread, volatility, or volume. We run a multivariate regression analysis and reveal that
HFTs increase their liquidity demand on aggregate during extreme price shocks and trade in the
direction of the crash when it occurs. Otherwise, in the absence of shocks, they provide more
liquidity than they absorb when spreads widen. Finally, our logit model indicates that SFEs
38

are not related to HFTs consuming more liquidity than they provide during the preceding time
interval. As such, we ﬁnd no evidence that HFTs trigger extreme price shocks.
Our identiﬁcation methodology based on Sequence-Based Flash Events (SFEs) is more accurate and leads to more robust empirical results overall. As they sometimes diverge from those
obtained through Mini Flash Crashes (MFCs) or Extreme Price Movements (EPMs), we therefore emphasize that conclusions regarding the contribution of High-Frequency Traders (HFTs)
to price stability are contingent upon the chosen methodology for identifying price shocks.
We therefore urge regulators to refrain from reaching premature conclusions about the role
played by HFTs in stressful market conditions. Indeed, our ﬁndings show that the choice of
the price shock identiﬁcation methodology is critical so that further research is necessary to test
various identiﬁcation methodologies on an expanded dataset. Stock exchanges should provide
the academic community with such a database free of charge in order to promote greater transparency, while regulators should make sure that such a dataset is made available to the academic
community.
This updated dataset should include at least one of the desirable features that we identify
below. These suggestions for future research aim to address the gaps identiﬁed in the current
research and to expand upon the ﬁndings uncovered. The objective is not only to enhance the
depth and breadth of understanding in this ﬁeld but also to pave the way for future investigations.
First of all, the focus of this study is on HFTs and ﬂash events in the US market, which may
present unique characteristics when compared to other markets. As of the time this study was
conducted, to the best of our knowledge, there appear to be no comparable data sets available
that include a ﬂag on HFT transactions and cover the same time period in Europe and Asia.
When available, they could provide valuable insights and enhance the scope of our analysis.
Second, the Nasdaq HFT dataset we use in this paper is made of aggregated data so that we
are unable to have a granular view of the liquidity supply and demand at the HFT ﬁrm level.
As a consequence, our ﬁndings are relevant at the HFT group level only (which is still very
important), but not at the HFT ﬁrm level which would provide further insight.
Third, large integrated ﬁrms (which may act as HFTs but not always) as well as HFTs routing
their orders through large integrated ﬁrms have not been included by Nasdaq in the database
due to the impossibility for the exchange to identify them precisely. As such, the 26 HFT ﬁrms of
the database can be considered as pure HFT ﬁrms or simply as ‘independent proprietary trading
ﬁrms’ as mentioned in Brogaard et al. (2017). The inclusion of data from larger integrated ﬁrms
and HFTs using their services would undoubtedly enhance the breadth and accuracy of all the
previous studies, providing a fuller picture of the HFT landscape.
39

Fourth, the data set is limited to transactions executed on Nasdaq, leaving us without visibility on HFT transactions executed on other U.S. stock exchanges. Although we account for
this by ﬁltering events based on the proportion of trades executed on Nasdaq, this approach
inevitably results in a partial view. The potential impact of HFTs (or NHFTs) operating outside
of Nasdaq, particularly in instances where they may trigger market ﬂuctuations or crashes, remains unobserved. Therefore, integrating data from these other exchanges would enable a more
holistic analysis, oﬀering deeper insights into the dynamics of HFT activities across the broader
U.S. stock market landscape.
Finally, we are unable to control for market manipulation or the potential imbalance generated
by non-HFT algorithmic traders. To do so, we would need more granular data with an extra ﬂag
on non-HFT algorithmic trades which are not provided by Nasdaq.
