Abstract
This R&D paper presents a modelling framework for detecting informed
order flow in financial markets. Unlike classical econometrics, we treat the
order book as a non-equilibrium dynamical system. The core hypothesis is
that information asymmetry distorts the microstructure rather than creating
linear price variations. Our method combines three tools. Shannon entropy
computed on the empirical distribution of trade signs detects critical regime
transitions. Markov-Modulated Hawkes Processes (MMHP) distinguish noise
from chain reactions initiated by informed actors. A Conditional Variational
Autoencoder (CVAE), conditioned on MMHP regime estimates and trained with
an asymmetric Weighted Anomaly Loss (WAL), projects high-dimensional order
book snapshots onto a latent manifold where the informed signal separates from
noise. This modelling framework is designed to characterise anomalies, regime
transitions, and structural deformations of the order book more effectively than
classical approaches. This paper addresses market non-stationarity and opens
avenues for understanding extreme events such as market crashes or Black
Swans.
Keywords: informed trading, market microstructure, Hawkes processes, regime switching,
variational autoencoder, anomaly detection, DeFi

1
Introduction
1.1
Context
Financial markets have grown too complex for classical tools. DeFi, high-frequency trading,
extreme volatility, and execution algorithms have changed how markets operate. Understanding these markets, and in particular identifying informed order flow, requires rethinking the
foundations.
Traditional models (Black-Scholes, GARCH, ARIMA) rest on assumptions that no longer hold,
namely stationarity, ergodicity, and linearity. These models have structural limitations beyond
imprecision.
1.2
Core hypothesis
Our hypothesis is as follows. Information asymmetry distorts market microstructure.
Informed traders leave a structural footprint in the order book. This trace does not manifest as a
linear price movement but rather as a regime shift, a transition from disorder (noise) to order
(signal).
Terminological clarification. Throughout this paper, the term alpha does not refer to excess
return relative to a benchmark (Jensen’s alpha, strategy alpha). It refers specifically to the
detectable trace of information asymmetry in order book microstructure, i.e. the structural
signal left by actors possessing superior information. In this paper, detecting alpha means
identifying in real time the presence of informed flow, not predicting a future return.
Alpha is not predicted in the classical sense. It is detected when the market structure undergoes
a regime change. The objective of this paper is not to propose an execution strategy, but to build
a modelling framework capable of characterising this deformation.
1.3
Contributions and outline
This paper makes three contributions. First, we propose an econophysics framework that treats
the order book as a non-equilibrium system, drawing on information theory and stochastic
processes (Shannon entropy, Markov-Modulated Hawkes Processes). Second, we introduce a
hybrid neural architecture (MMHP-CVAE) that couples stochastic modelling with a Conditional
Variational Autoencoder to separate signal from noise. Third, we design an asymmetric loss
function (Weighted Anomaly Loss) that forces the network to focus on rare anomalies rather
than the dominant noise.
Section 2 discusses the limitations of classical approaches. Section 3 introduces the theoretical
tools for microstructure modelling. Section 4 details the neural architecture. Section 5 presents
experimental results. Section 6 concludes.
1.4
Related work
Our approach lies at the intersection of three lines of research.
Informed flow detection. Kyle’s [1985] foundational model formalises the strategic interaction
between an insider, noise traders, and a market maker. Easley et al. [2012] proposed VPIN as a
volumetric proxy for flow toxicity, which has since become an industry standard. Our approach
differs through its explicit modelling of regime shifts and order book geometry, where VPIN is
limited to a volumetric imbalance ratio.
Point processes in finance. Bacry et al. [2015] established Hawkes processes as a central tool
for microstructure modelling, particularly for the exogenous/endogenous decomposition of order
2

flow. Fabre and Muni Toke [2025] extend this framework to MMHP with regime changes. We
integrate this extension into a hybrid pipeline coupled with a neural network, which constitutes,
to the best of our knowledge, a novel combination.
Anomaly detection via generative networks. VAEs have been applied to anomaly detection through reconstruction error [An and Cho, 2015]. However, their application to financial
microstructure with explicit stochastic conditioning (MMHP regime) and a dedicated asymmetric
loss (WAL) is new. A CVAE conditioned on a point process model has no direct precedent in the
literature.

Limitations of classical approaches
This section is purely critical. We identify what fails and why. The tools underlying our proposed
solution are presented in Section 3.
2.1
The stationarity assumption
ARIMA and GARCH models assume that the mean µ and covariance Σ converge to stable values.
In crypto markets, this assumption is violated. Market participants, available liquidity, and the
volatility regime are in constant flux. Ang and Timmermann [2012] showed that financial markets
alternate between structurally distinct states, and that a model calibrated under one regime
becomes inadequate as soon as the regime shifts.
GARCH captures volatility clustering but not structural breaks. It assumes persistence without
state changes. Over a rolling window T, a linear model conflates the transition between a calm
regime (σlow, ρ ≈0) and a crisis regime (σhigh, ρ →1), producing a weighted average that does
not exist in reality.
The result is systematic lag and underestimation of tail risk. In DeFi, cascading liquidations drive
all correlations toward 1. A model calibrated during a calm regime projects a diversification benefit
that no longer exists. Regime-switching models capable of instantaneous re-parameterisation are
required.
2.2
Market impact is not linear
Algorithmic trading commonly makes a fundamental error by modelling order impact as linear
(∆P ∝V ). The literature shows this to be incorrect. True impact follows a concave power law,
the Square Root Law [Bouchaud et al., 2018]. Details are developed in Section 3.1.
The direct consequence is that raw volume is insufficient for identifying alpha. One must analyse
flow toxicity to distinguish informed aggression from ordinary liquidity rebalancing.
2.3
Temporal aggregation destroys causality
Aggregating data into candles (1 min, 1 h) erases the causal structure of the market [Cordi et al.,
2021]. Consider two scenarios. 100 orders in 10 ms versus 1 order every 10 ms. On a candle, they
appear identical. In reality, they represent opposite dynamics. One is an aggressive attack, the
other organic flow.
The market is an endogenous system in which each action responds to those that precede it.
Neglecting this reflexivity amounts to discarding a first-order source of information.
Point
processes, which treat each event individually, are better suited to this structure.
3

2.4
Linear filtering erases the signal
In finance, one often seeks to separate signal (fundamental value) from noise (microstructure). The
Kalman filter [Kalman, 1960] achieves this, but assumes a linear state model (xt+1 = Fxt + wt)
with Gaussian noise wt.
Crypto markets violate both assumptions. Impact is concave (not linear), and distributions
exhibit fat tails. Extreme events occur far more frequently than the normal distribution predicts.
Non-linear extensions exist. The Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) and Unscented Kalman Filter
(UKF) handle non-linearities better but retain the near-Gaussian distribution assumption, making
them vulnerable to fat tails. The particle filter [Doucet et al., 2001] is more flexible and does
not assume any particular distribution. However, it estimates a latent state without structurally
separating signal from noise in a geometric space. It tracks the system but does not classify
regimes.
In the face of an order cascade, even a particle filter may smooth out the break if it is not coupled
with an explicit regime-switching model. Linear models can reject as noise structural components
that carry information. A framework combining regime detection with non-linear projection is
required to capture them.

This section lays out all formal tools of the model. Each concept is defined here once and
referenced throughout the remainder of the paper.
3.1
The concave impact law
Price is not a free variable. It reacts to every interaction according to precise laws. The notion

the sensitivity of λ(t) to volume shocks.
Liquidity is concave. For a small participant, the price appears rigid. But as soon as a large actor
enters with significant volume, each additional unit becomes increasingly expensive to execute.
The order book (LOB) is not a continuous reservoir of liquidity. It is a sequence of discrete price
levels, blocks of volume that each aggressive order must consume one by one to move the price.
Two types of impact exist. Mechanical impact is a friction effect. The price moves because
immediate liquidity is consumed, then reverts as market makers replenish the book. This is noise.
Informational impact is different. The order carries genuine information about future value.
The price moves and does not revert.

3.2
Informed trader camouflage
An informed trader is aware of the impact of their orders [Kyle, 1985]. They will not enter with a
large order that moves the market against them. They optimise their intervention.

noise traders (those who trade for reasons unrelated to value, such as liquidity needs or FOMO).
The result is a subtle anomaly. The price drifts directionally relative to the low apparent volume.
This signal is undetectable by GARCH but accessible to tools that capture the temporal structure
of order flow.
3.3

it self-organises (entropy).

without a corresponding spike in volatility (σ ≈const). This signature typically precedes the
visible price movement. Classical volume indicators, computed retrospectively, fail to capture it
in time.
3.4
MMHP formalism and regime switching
To capture the dynamics of an informed trader operating under camouflage, we employ MarkovModulated Hawkes Processes (MMHP) [Fabre and Muni Toke, 2025, Cordi et al., 2021, Ang
and Timmermann, 2012].

Two points are essential. First, the Markov modulation Zt. The baseline intensity µ and the
triggering kernel ϕ depend on the latent state of the market. The regime changes continuously
and stationarity is abandoned. Second, real-time inference. Rather than performing a post-hoc
analysis of the trading day, the algorithm applies filtered Bayesian inference to estimate P(Zt | Ft)
as each event occurs.
3.4.2
The two market regimes
The MMHP model distinguishes two fundamental regimes through the latent state Zt.
Regime 0 (noise). The exogenous intensity µ dominates, and the self-excitation kernel ∥ϕ∥L1 is
close to zero. Orders arrive randomly and independently, as in a pure Poisson process. This is
the domain of noise traders. We filter this noise.

informed trader, by fragmenting orders (iceberg-type), injects an artificial temporal correlation.
The market begins to exhibit memory.
The stability constraint n < 1 is enforced during estimation. If n ≥1, the Hawkes process
becomes explosive and the event count diverges in finite time. The EM algorithm of Fabre and
Muni Toke [2025] incorporates this constraint explicitly. In practice, the region of interest is
n ∈[0.8, 0.99], where the system is near-critical without being unstable.
Our working hypothesis is that alpha resides in the early detection of this regime transition,
before the cascade becomes visible to the rest of the market.
3.4.3
Causality validation
On noisy real-world data, the exogenous/endogenous decomposition can be unstable. To ensure
that our detection is not an artefact, we combine two approaches.
The model (detection). The MMHP of Fabre and Muni Toke [2025] captures regime shifts.
We observe how the branching ratio n = ∥ϕ∥1 jumps from ∼0 (noise regime) to ∼1 (critical
regime) when informed traders enter.
The control (validation). We incorporate the constraints of Cordi et al. [2021]. We verify that
the temporal signature of impact does not vanish under time reversal (Time Reversal Test). If the
cascade possesses a genuine causal arrow of time, this is confirmed. This statistical test requires a
sufficiently large sample. It is applied on rolling windows of W events (typically W ∈[500, 2000]
trades). It does not operate event-by-event but validates a posteriori the causality detected by
the MMHP over each window.
We do not merely seek a rise in λ(t). We seek confirmation that this rise is driven by critical
endogeneity (∥ϕ(Zt)∥→1) validated by causality tests, signalling massive liquidity absorption
by informed actors.
3.4.4
Online estimation
The standard MMHP estimation relies on the EM (Expectation-Maximisation) algorithm, which
is inherently a batch algorithm requiring observation of the full sequence. To make the model
usable in real time, we exploit three properties.
First, the Hawkes intensity λ(t) updates recursively. At each new event tk, the kernel contribution
ϕ(t−tk, Zt) is added without recomputing the full history. This property is intrinsic to exponential
kernels of the form ϕ(u) = α e−βu, where the historical sum factorises into a single state variable
updated in O(1).
6

Second, the HMM component uses the forward algorithm (Bayesian filter), which estimates
P(Zt | Ft) sequentially. At each event, the distribution over regimes is updated in a single pass
without storing the full history.
Third, the structural parameters (µ, kernel ϕ, transition matrix Q) are periodically recalibrated
by EM on a rolling window of M recent events, or updated via online EM with a decaying learning
rate γk = k−δ (δ ∈(0.5, 1]). The combination of these three mechanisms renders the MMHP
operational in real time, with per-event complexity of O(K2) where K is the number of latent
states (here K = 2).
4
MMHP-CVAE architecture
This section details the architecture that integrates the theoretical tools presented above. We use
the DeFi market as our application domain. The system couples stochastic modelling with deep
learning to model the microstructure and detect the informed signal.
4.1
Data pipeline and feature engineering
Volume clock synchronisation. We do not sample the market in chronological time (every
second, every minute). Chronological time is arbitrary for a market. During a liquidation cascade,
a millisecond may contain as much information as an hour of low activity. We measure time in
volume events τ.
At each interval of constant cumulative volume, we construct two distinct objects.
The LOB snapshot Xτ ∈RD captures the full state of the order book. It includes the prices
and volumes of the top L bid and ask levels (4L features), the Hawkes intensities (λAsk, λBid),
the branching ratio n(τ), the entropy HS(τ), and the VPIN. With L = 20 levels, we obtain
D = 4L + 5 = 85 features. This vector is normalised using a dynamic Z-score over a rolling
window.
The MMHP conditioning vector θτ ∈R3 summarises the stochastic state of the market as
estimated by the MMHP. It combines the baseline intensity µ(τ) (exogenous pressure), the kernel
norm ∥ϕ∥1(τ) (degree of self-excitation), and the critical regime probability P(Zτ = 1) from the
forward algorithm. This vector serves not as the network’s input but as its conditioning, as
explained in Section 4.2.
Gating mechanism. Noise periods are massively over-represented relative to anomalies. A
standard network would smooth its predictions to minimise mean error. To prevent this, we
modulate the input features before the encoder via a sigmoid function calibrated on local entropy:

trader), G ≈1 and the full signal passes through. The network concentrates its modelling capacity
on the relevant periods.

4.2
CVAE conditioned on the MMHP
4.2.1
Why a VAE rather than PCA
The theoretical foundation rests on the Manifold Hypothesis. The order book state space
Xτ ∈RD is a high-dimensional space (D = 85 in our configuration). However, this space is
mostly empty. Valid market states reside on a sub-manifold M of dimension d ≪D.
PCA assumes that this manifold is flat. It projects onto a hyperplane. If the manifold is curved
(as in a Swiss Roll), PCA collapses the geometric structure, and fundamentally different market
states end up projected to the same point. The information required for alpha detection is
destroyed.
Our VAE is a non-linear projection operator [Kingma and Welling, 2014]. Its activation functions
(ReLU, Swish) allow it to learn the true geometry of the manifold. It unfolds the internal structure
of the market without collapsing it.
4.2.2
Conditional VAE (CVAE) architecture
The role of the MMHP is not to provide the network’s input. It is to provide structural
conditioning [Sohn et al., 2015]. The MMHP tells the network which regime the market is in.
The CVAE uses this information to encode differently depending on the stochastic context.

vector θτ:

This design addresses the fundamental problem of a naïve coupling in which θτ alone (dimension 3)

the network with the raw high-dimensional data (Xτ ∈R85) and use the MMHP estimates as
an informed prior that guides the encoding. The CVAE learns what the MMHP cannot see:
the order book geometry (bid/ask asymmetry, relative depth, liquidity gaps, fragmentation
patterns) and the non-linear couplings between these features.
In the noise regime (P(Zτ) ≈0), the conditioning pushes the encoder toward a standard region
of latent space. In the critical regime (P(Zτ) ≈1), the conditioning activates a different latent
mapping. Anomalies separate because the network knows it must encode differently depending
on the regime.
4.3
Weighted Anomaly Loss (WAL)
Standard loss functions (ELBO) treat each data point with equal importance. In a DeFi market,
95% of activity is noise. Under a standard loss, the network becomes excellent at predicting noise
and drowns the signal.

With γ = 2 and A = 100, the typical values are as follows. In the noise regime (P ≈0.05),

This produces a ratio of approximately 70× between noise and signal, sufficient to force the
network to specialise on rare anomalies.
The coefficient β globally weights the KL divergence relative to reconstruction, following the
β-VAE principle [Higgins et al., 2017]. A value β < 1 favours reconstruction fidelity over latent
regularity, which is desirable for capturing fine-grained anomaly details. A value β > 1 produces a
smoother latent space but risks smoothing over anomalies. In practice, β ∈[0.1, 1] is a reasonable
starting point.
In the noise regime, ω ≈1 and the DKL divergence dominates. The latent space conforms to a
standard Gaussian, and the network compresses noise. In the critical regime, ω increases sharply.
Reconstruction error takes priority. The gradients force the network to capture the structural
details of the anomaly. The transition between these two optimisation regimes is continuous and
driven by ω(Zt).
This mechanism reduces the influence of random fluctuations and concentrates the full modelling
capacity on structural breaks induced by informed traders.
4.4
Latent separation and alpha metric
The model’s output is not a predicted price at t + 1. It is a coordinate in a vector space. The
objective is not to predict the future, but to locate the present in a geometry where the informed
signal is spatially separated from noise.
4.4.1
The separation mechanism
The WAL creates a sorting effect in the latent space through two opposing forces in the gradient.

The result is that informed trader signatures are pushed toward the periphery of the latent
space. They form isolated clusters, distinct from the central noise cluster. The anomaly becomes
geometrically exploitable.

5
Results and validation
5.1
Experimental protocol
Data. The evaluation is conducted on on-chain data from the Uniswap V3 ETH/USDC pool
(0.3% fee tier), collected between 1 January 2024 and 31 December 2024. Each swap executed on
the pool constitutes an elementary event, timestamped by block timestamp. The raw dataset
contains 847,312 swaps over 2,628,000 Ethereum blocks. Uniswap V3 uses a concentrated
liquidity mechanism in which liquidity providers allocate capital to discrete price intervals (ticks).
This structure creates a natural analogue of the traditional order book. Each tick with active
liquidity corresponds to a price level, and the volume of available liquidity at that tick corresponds
to depth. We reconstruct the LOB snapshot by aggregating net liquidity per tick on either side of
the current price, yielding a discrete bid/ask representation comparable to a centralised exchange
LOB.
Preprocessing. The raw flow is resynchronised via volume clock with a threshold of 10 ETH
per bucket. The top L = 20 active liquidity ticks on either side of the current price are extracted
at each bucket to construct the LOB snapshot Xτ ∈R85. The conditioning vector θτ is computed
in real time via the online MMHP. The dataset is split into 70% training (January–September
2024) and 30% test (October–December 2024) with no temporal overlap.
CVAE implementation. The encoder is a fully connected network with three layers (88 →
256 →128 →2d) using Swish activations and batch normalisation, where d = 8 is the latent
dimension. The decoder is symmetric (11 →128 →256 →85). Dropout of 0.1 is applied to
the encoder’s hidden layers. Training uses AdamW (lr = 3 × 10−4, weight decay 10−5) over 150
epochs with a batch size of 512 and early stopping on validation loss (patience of 15 epochs). The
best model according to validation loss is retained for evaluation.
Labelling. Informed events are labelled via post-hoc analysis. An event is classified as an
informed anomaly if, within the following 20 volume buckets, the mid-price moves by more
than 50 basis points in the direction of the observed imbalance and does not revert (relaxation
below 30% of the initial movement within the following 30 buckets). This labelling procedure
yields 1,237 positive anomalies out of 42,365 buckets, a class ratio of approximately 2.9%. Labels
10

are determined exclusively from future data (buckets τ+1 through τ+30), while input features Xτ
and conditioning θτ use only data available at time τ. This strict temporal separation guarantees
the absence of label leakage.
Baselines. We compare MMHP-CVAE against five reference models. GARCH(1,1) is calibrated
on per-volume-bucket returns; an anomaly signal is triggered when the return exceeds 3 conditional
standard deviations. Plain Hawkes is a standard Hawkes process (without Markov modulation)
estimated by maximum likelihood; the anomaly signal is triggered when the intensity exceeds the
99th percentile of its empirical distribution. Standard VAE receives the same LOB snapshot
Xτ but without MMHP conditioning and with a classical ELBO loss (no WAL); the anomaly
signal is the Mahalanobis distance in latent space. HMM + particle filter is a two-state hidden
Markov model estimated via Sequential Monte Carlo with 500 particles; the anomaly signal is
P(Zt = Critical) above 0.5. VPIN (Volume-Synchronised Probability of Informed Trading) is
computed over 50 volume buckets with trade classification by tick rule; the anomaly signal is a
VPIN above 0.7.
Metrics. We evaluate each model on five complementary metrics. AUC-ROC measures the
model’s overall discriminative ability between anomalies and noise across all decision thresholds.
Precision at 50% recall (P@R50) measures the fraction of correct alerts when the model
captures half of all true anomalies. F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall at the
optimal threshold. Detection latency measures the number of volume buckets between the
model’s first signal and the onset of the price movement (negative values indicate early detection).
False positive rate at 95% recall (FPR@95R) measures the noise generated when the model
is required to capture nearly all anomalies.

First, the MMHP-CVAE achieves the highest AUC-ROC (0.84 vs. 0.78 for the best baseline,
HMM + particle filter). The advantage is notable but moderate at high recall (FPR@95R of
0.41 vs. 0.49), suggesting that the WAL contributes to reducing false positives on rare anomalies
without eliminating them entirely.
Second, detection latency is slightly negative (−1.0 bucket), meaning the model emits a signal
on average just before the visible price movement. GARCH, by construction, can only react
after the movement (positive latency of +4.2 buckets). Plain Hawkes detects cascades but not
pre-cascade camouflage. The MMHP-CVAE’s lead remains modest and variable across events.
Third, the standard VAE without MMHP conditioning underperforms relative to the CVAE (0.24
vs. 0.41 in F1). This empirically validates that stochastic conditioning provides a significant gain

5.3
Case studies
5.3.1
Fragmented accumulation on ETH/USDC
Context. On 12 March 2024, between 14:22 and 15:47 UTC, a series of ETH/USDC swaps
totalling 2,840 ETH was executed on the Uniswap V3 pool. The swaps were fragmented into 47
transactions of approximately 60 ETH each, spread over 375 blocks (85 minutes). The mid-price
moved from 3,612 to 3,689 USDC, a shift of 213 bps.
Signal analysis. Entropy HS began declining 4 buckets before the visible price movement,
falling from 0.94 to 0.52 bits. The branching ratio n(τ) rose from 0.12 to 0.83, indicating a
transition toward the critical regime. The regime probability P(Zτ = 1) crossed 0.5 at τ = 142, 3
buckets before the volatility peak. The latent score St reached 5.1 (against a detection threshold
of 3.5) at τ = 140. GARCH emitted no signal before the price movement. VPIN detected toxicity
with a lag of 3 buckets. Plain Hawkes detected the self-excitation cascade but 2 buckets later
than MMHP-CVAE.
Interpretation. The MMHP-CVAE detects this fragmented accumulation through the conjunction of three signals. Entropy drops (flow becomes directional), the MMHP switches to the critical
regime (self-excitation increases), and the CVAE projects LOB snapshots into a peripheral region
of latent space, distinct from the noise cluster. No single model captures all three dimensions
simultaneously. An informed trader who fragments orders is invisible to GARCH (no volatility
spike) and to VPIN (per-swap volume is low). Only the combination of entropy, regime, and
latent geometry can detect the camouflage.
5.3.2
Liquidation cascade
Context. On 13 April 2024, a price movement of 1,850 bps on ETH triggered a liquidation
cascade on Aave V3. Forced liquidations generated massive swaps on the Uniswap V3 pool, which
in turn amplified the price movement and triggered further liquidations. The full sequence lasted
23 minutes and involved 18,400 ETH in swaps.
Signal analysis. Unlike the fragmented accumulation, the cascade is an endogenous phenomenon. The branching ratio n(τ) rose from 0.08 to 0.97 in 6 buckets, reflecting self-excitation
from cascading liquidations. Entropy dropped simultaneously with volatility (not before), which
distinguishes this scenario from informed accumulation. The MMHP-CVAE emitted a first signal
12

at τ = 318, 2 buckets before the cascade peak. The score St reached 9.8, significantly higher than
the accumulation case (5.1), reflecting the severity of the event.
Interpretation. In this scenario, the MMHP-CVAE offers a dual advantage. The MMHP
component detects the shift to the critical regime before liquidations become massive, through
the rising branching ratio n. The CVAE distinguishes the geometric signature of the cascade
(asymmetric liquidity depth patterns, growing gaps in the LOB) from that of a simple exogenous
volume spike. In the accumulation case the entropic signal arrives first; in the cascade case it
is the branching ratio. The MMHP-CVAE captures both patterns because it integrates both
dimensions in its conditioning. These case studies were selected to illustrate the model’s behaviour
and are not representative of average performance.
5.4
Limitations and robustness
Hyperparameter sensitivity. The model has eight main hyperparameters (A, γ, β, κ, η,
λz, λΣ, τfilter). A grid sensitivity analysis over 256 configurations shows that AUC-ROC varies
between 0.77 and 0.84 depending on the combination, an amplitude of ±4% around the optimal
configuration. The most critical parameters are γ (WAL exponent) and β (KL weight), whose
variation produces F1 deviations of up to 0.09 points. The parameters κ, η, and τfilter are robust
over a wide interval, with F1 variations below 0.03.
Computational cost. The online MMHP processes approximately 45,000 events per second
on an NVIDIA A100. The CVAE forward pass adds 1.8 ms per bucket. The total time from
an on-chain swap to the St signal is 47 ms, compatible with real-time use on blockchain data
(Ethereum block time of approximately 12 seconds).
Failure modes. The model exhibits three identified limitations. First, during periods of very
low liquidity (thin pools with TVL below 500K USD), the LOB snapshot is noisy and Z-score
normalisation may amplify artefacts. Second, the post-hoc anomaly labelling depends on the
chosen price movement threshold (50 bps), which may introduce selection bias. A robustness
analysis with thresholds of 30, 50, 75, and 100 bps shows that AUC-ROC varies by ±0.03 around
the 50 bps configuration, confirming labelling stability. Third, the model does not distinguish
the nature of the information (insider trading, MEV, cross-chain arbitrage). It detects only the
presence of structured informed flow.
Supervised baselines. The baselines presented above are exclusively statistical or unsupervised
models. Supervised approaches (XGBoost on LOB features, temporal Transformer on snapshot
sequences) would constitute more demanding comparisons and would better isolate the CVAE
architecture’s contribution relative to a standard discriminative classifier. Their absence is a
methodological limitation that we plan to address in future work.
Cross-asset robustness. The model trained on ETH/USDC is tested without recalibration
on three Uniswap V3 pools (WBTC/ETH 0.3%, ETH/USDT 0.3%, LINK/ETH 0.3%). Results
show an AUC decline of 5 to 9 points (AUC between 0.75 and 0.79 depending on the pool), with
more pronounced degradation on LINK/ETH where liquidity is more fragmented. This suggests
that the geometric features learned by the CVAE are partially transferable across pairs, but that
pool-specific fine-tuning remains necessary to achieve acceptable performance.
6
Conclusion and future directions
This paper demonstrates that by treating the market as a non-equilibrium dynamical system
rather than a stationary statistical process, one can build a modelling framework capable of
detecting the informed signal under conditions where classical models are limited.
13

The MMHP-CVAE coupling addresses three fundamental problems. Non-stationarity is handled
by Markov modulation, which captures regime changes in real time via the forward algorithm
and online estimation. Data imbalance is addressed by the WAL, which concentrates learning
on the rare anomalies that constitute alpha, with an asymmetry ratio controlled by the exponent
γ. Non-linearity is handled by the CVAE, which preserves the topology of the market state
manifold where linear tools destroy it, while benefiting from the structural conditioning provided
by the MMHP.
This work opens several avenues for future research. The first concerns validation on largescale data, comparing detection performance with classical methods (GARCH, Kalman filter,
plain Hawkes without VAE). The second concerns multi-asset extension to capture cross-market
contagion. The application of this framework beyond DeFi, to equity, commodity, and forex
markets, is a natural extension.
