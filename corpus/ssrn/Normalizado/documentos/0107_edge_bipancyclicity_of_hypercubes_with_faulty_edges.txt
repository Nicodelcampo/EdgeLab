Abstract
4
Temporal graphs are a class of graphs defined by a constant set of vertices and
5
a changing set of edges, each of which is known as a timestep. These graphs
6
are well motivated in modelling real-world networks, where connections may
7
change over time. One such example, itself the primary motivation for this
8
paper, are public transport networks, where vertices represent stops and
9
edges the connections available at some given time. Exploration problems
10
are one of the most studied problems for temporal graphs, asking if an agent
11
starting at some given vertex v can visit every vertex in the graph.
12
In this paper, we study two primary classes of temporal graphs. First,
13
we study temporal graphs with frequent edges, temporal graphs where each
14
edge e is active at least once every fe timesteps, called the frequency of the
15
edge. Second, temporal graphs with regular edges, graphs where each edge e
16
is active at any timestep t where t ≡se mod re, with se being the start time
17
of the edge, and re the regularity.
18
We show that graphs with frequent edges can be explored in O(Fn)
19
timesteps, where F = maxe∈E fe, and that graphs with regular edges can
20
be explored in O(Rn) timesteps, where R = maxe∈E re.
We provide ad21
ditional results for public transport graphs, temporal graphs formed by the
22
union of several routes, corresponding to the schedules of some modes of
23
transit, for sequential connection graphs, temporal graphs in which each ver24
tex has a single active in-edge per timestep, iterating over the set of edges in
25
some order, and for broadcast networks, a representation of communication
26
within distributed networks where each vertex broadcasts a message either
27
to all vertices, or none at each timestep.

1. Introduction
29
In many real world settings, networks are not static objects but instead
30
have unstable connections that vary with time. Such examples include public
31
transport networks [1] and infection control [2]. Temporal graphs provide
32
a model for such time-varying networks. Formally, a temporal graph G is
33
a generalisation of (static) graphs containing a common vertex set V and
34
ordered sequence of T edge sets (E1, E2, . . . , ET), each called a timestep (also
35
known as a snapshot), with the lifetime of the graph defined by the number
36
of edge sets. Unlike some models of dynamic graphs, we assume that we have
37
full knowledge of the graph, i.e. that we are given every edge set as part of
38
the input.
39
Much work on temporal graphs has focused on the problem of exploration
40
of temporal graphs. In this problem, some agent (or set of agents) must
41
visit every vertex in a temporal graph.
The agent is restricted in being
42
allowed only a single move per timestep, thus as the graph changes, the agent
43
movement may become restricted. This was motivated by extending the well
44
known Travelling Salesman to temporal graphs by Michail and Spirakis
45
[3]. Since then, there has been a broad span of work covering reachability
46
[4, 5, 6] and exploration [1, 3, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].
47
The decision version of TEXP in which one has to decide if at least one
48
exploration schedule exists in a given temporal graph from a given starting
49
vertex is an NP-complete problem [3]. Indeed, this problem remains NP50
complete even if the underlying graph has pathwidth 2 and every snapshot
51
is a tree [14], or if the underlying graph is a star and the exploration has to
52
start and end at the center of the star [15].
53
In the optimisation version of this problem, we ask for the quickest ex54
ploration of a given temporal graph, that being the schedule ending at the
55
earliest timestep, formally defied in Section 2.
56
On the positive side, we mention the landmark paper by Erlebach et. al.
57
[10], who show that any always connected temporal graphs (temporal graphs
58
where each timestep is connected) can be explored in O(n2) timesteps, where
59
n is the number of vertices. For graphs where the underlying graph (the
60
static graph corresponding to the union over all edge sets) is planar, they
61
reduce this to O(n1.8 log n) timesteps, strengthened by Adamson et al. [7]
62
to O(n1.75 log n) timesteps. If the underlying graph has treewidth at most k
63
then the temporal graph can be explored in O(k1.5n1.5 log n) timesteps, later
64
strengthened to O(kn1.5 log n) timesteps [7].
If the underlying graph is a

82
In this paper, we focus on the exploration of temporal graphs with fre83
quent and regular edges. We define such graphs by how often each edge
84
appears and, more importantly in this work, the maximum number of con85
secutive timesteps such that a given edge is not active. More formally, given
86
a edge, e, we define the frequency of e, fe, as the smallest value such that
87
∀t ∈[1, T −fe], e ∈S
t′∈[t,t+fe] Et′, with T being the lifetime of the graph.
88
The regularity of an edge, e, is defined as the smallest value, re such that
89
∀t ∈[1, T −re], e ∈Et iff e ∈Et+re. We are motivated primarily by public
90
transport networks, where an edge represents a connection between stations.
91
In such systems we assume, or hope, see [1], that the connections between
92
stations in the system have some known frequency or preferably regularity.
93
The notions of frequency and regularity, by our definitions, have been
94
studied previously with a particular focus on graph properties [17, 10, 18,
95
19, 20], Cops and Robber games[21, 22]. Of particular relevance to us are
96
the papers by Bellitto et al. [23] and Erlebach et al. [10]. In [10], the authors
97
study a similar definition where each edge has a known, constant, frequency,
98
and there exists a constant c such that every cut contains at least one edge
99
every c timesteps. The authors show that there exists an exploration schedule
100
requiring at most O(m) timesteps, where m is the number of edges in the
101
underlying graph, and further that this holds even if the graph is unknown
102
in advance. In [23], Bellitto et al. study restless exploration, a restriction of

Our Results. We show that graphs where each edge has a frequency of at
109
most f can be explored in f(2n −3) timesteps, and that graphs where each
110
edge has a regularity of at most r can be explored in r(2n −3) timesteps.
111
We note that is generalises the result of Erlebach et al. [10] by removing the
112
requirement that the frequency is a constant, or that the edges in the cuts
113
appear at least once per constant number of rounds, at the cost of potentially
114
slower exploration, particularly for sparse graphs. Further, as our definition
115
of regularity generalises the definition of periodicity for temporal graphs given
116
in [23] and of edge periodicity given in [22], thus can be directly applied to
117
these settings.
118
Further, we explicitly introduce and study the following sets of temporal
119
graphs, applying our main results to each in order to get an upper bound on
120

121

122

123

124

125

126

127

128

129

131
underlying graph.
132

134
timestep, with the additional constraint that all in-edges to a given
135
vertex must be active between each activation of the out-edges. We
136
show that such graphs can be explored in O(dn2) timesteps, where d is
137
diameter of the graph and, further, in O(δn) timesteps if the graph is

2. Preliminaries
141
We first define the notation used in this paper. Let [i, j] = i, i + 1, . . . , j
142
denote the (ordered) set of integers between some pair i, j ∈N, where N is
143
the set of natural numbers. Note that if i = j, then [i, j] = {i}, and if i > j,
144
[i, j] = ∅.
145
We define a graph G = (V, E) by a set of vertices, by convention V =
146
(v1, v2, . . . , vn), and set of edges, by convention E ⊆V × V , each a tuple
147
of vertices. Note that we may write the edge e between the vertices vi and
148
vj as either e = (vi, vj) or e = (vj, vi). When an edge is given explicitly as
149
(vi, vj), we call vi the start point and vj the end point. A walk in a graph
150
is an ordered sequence of edges W = (vi1, vi2)(vi2, vi3) . . . (vim−1,vim), where
151
the end point the the jth edge is the start point of the (j + 1)th edge. The
152
start point of the first edge in a given walk is the start point of the first
153
edge in the walk and, analogously, the end point of the walk is the end point
154
of the last edge in the walk. The length of a walk W, denoted |W| is the
155
number of edges in the walk.
A graph G = (V, E) is connected if there
156
exists, for every pair of vertices v, u ∈V a walk starting at v and ending
157
at u. Note that a single walk W may contain multiple copies of the same
158
edge without contradiction. The distance between two vertices v, u ∈V in
159
a graph, denoted dist(v, u) is the value such there exists some walk W of
160
length dist(v, u) with the start point v, the end point u and ∀W ′ ∈{W ′′ is a
161
walk in G starting at v and ending at u}, |W ′| ≥dist(v, u). The diameter of
162
a graph G is the maximum distance between any pair of points in the graph,
163
formally the value maxv,u∈V dist(v, u).
164
The neighbourhood of a vertex v, denoted N(v) is the set of vertices
165
N(v) = {u ∈V | (v, u) ∈E}. The degree of a vertex v, denoted ∆(v) is the
166
number of vertices in the neighbourhood of v, formally, ∆(v) = |N(v)|.
167
A directed graph is a graph where each edge has an orientation, with a
168
fixed start point and end point. Thus, the edge (vi, vj) is not equivalent to
169
(vj, vi). A walk on a directed graph is defined in the same way as a walk on an
170
undirected graph. In this paper, we restrict ourselves to symmetric directed
171
graphs. A directed graph G = (V, E) is symmetric if the edge (vi, vj) ∈E
172
iff (vj, vi) ∈E. The in-edges of a vertex v is the set of edges in E with the
173
end point v, formally, in(v) = {(u, v) ∈E}. Analogously, the out-edges of a

Temporal Graphs. A temporal graph is a generalisation of a graph, herein
188
called a static graph whenever confusion may otherwise arise, where, rather
189
than having a single edge set, the graph contains an ordered sequence of
190
timesteps, by convention E1, E2, . . . , ET, while maintaining a shared set of
191
vertices. We call each edge set a timestep (also known as a snapshot). An
192
edge e is active at timestep t if e ∈Et. We assume, without loss of generality,
193
that each timestep contains at least one active edge. In our definition, an
194
edge e may be active in any number of timesteps.
195
By default, we assume that the temporal graph is undirected, with a
196
temporal graph referred to as a directed temporal graph if the edge sets are
197
directed. We assume that either every edge set is directed, or none are. Given
198
a temporal graph G the lifetime of the graph is the number of timesteps, thus,
199
if G = (V, E1, E2, . . . , ET), then the lifetime of the graph is T.
200

203

205

206

208

209

260

263

4.1. Public Transport Graphs
378
We first consider public transport graphs. We use such temporal graphs to
379

380

383

386

4.3. Broadcast Networks
415
In distributed computing, the broadcast model of communication is one
416
in which, at each communication round, every vertex either sends the same
417
message to every neighbour, or sends no message [25]. We can use a tem418
poral graph to model communication in these networks by representing each
419
communication round as a timestep in the temporal graph, with messages
420
from vertex v to vertex u in timestep t represented by having the edge (v, u)
421
in Et. We call these graphs Broadcast Networks.
422
We have two additional restrictions on these temporal graphs. First, to
423
model the restrictions on the broadcast model, we require that each time a
424
vertex broadcasts a message, it does so to all neighbours. Formally, ∀v ∈
425
V, t ∈[1, T], either {(v, u) | u ∈N(v)} ⊆Et or {(v, u) | u ∈N(v)} ∩Et = ∅.
426
Second, we assume that a vertex will only send the next message once it
427
has received some sort of acknowledgement from every neighbour, either
428
in the form of a simple acknowledged message, or a full message from the
429
neighbour containing the round in which the neighbour has sent the message.
430
This avoids a desynchronisation of the network, where one vertex progresses
431
despite no follow-up information being given.
To simplify the model, we

5. Conclusion
495
In this paper, we have studied the set of temporal graphs defined by
496
having frequent edges, both in the general case and in several motivating
497
settings, including public transport networks and the broadcast communica498
tion model. The primary result of this paper is that any such graph with n
499
vertices, where each edge is active at least once every F timesteps, can be
500
explored in F(2n −3) timesteps.
