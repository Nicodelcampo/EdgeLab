Observation Frequency as a Trading-Environment Design 
Variable in Cointegration-Based Deep Reinforcement 
Learning for Cryptocurrency Statistical Arbitrage
Abstract
This study examines observation frequency as a methodological design variable in cointegrationbased deep reinforcement learning (DRL) cryptocurrency statistical arbitrage. Financial DRL 
studies often treat the intraday sampling interval as a fixed preprocessing choice, although it 
determines the number of market states, reward transitions, exposure-adjustment opportunities, 
turnover events, and formation observations available to the agent. To test whether temporal 
discretization affects trading performance, the same economic scenario is reconstructed across five 
observation frequencies: 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour, 2 hours, and 4 hours. Each scenario 
preserves a six-day formation window and a one-day trading horizon, while the number of 
observations varies with the sampling interval. The empirical design evaluates DQN, PPO, and 
A2C agents across four fixed cryptocurrency asset panels, two action-space specifications, and 
three random seeds, producing 360 matched DRL runs. Results show that observation frequency 
significantly affects total return, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, annualized volatility, and 
turnover. Friedman repeated-measures tests indicate significant frequency effects for total return, 
χ² (4) = 99.43, p < .001, and Sharpe ratio, χ² (4) = 77.07, p < .001. The strongest effect appears in 
turnover, χ² (4) = 270.88, p < .001, indicating that sampling frequency changes trading behavior 
as well as measured performance. The 15-minute setting produces the largest average losses and 
highest turnover, whereas the 2-hour and 4-hour settings reduce losses and trading intensity. 
However, mean returns remain negative across all five frequencies. The findings show that 
observation frequency is not a neutral data-formatting decision; it is part of the DRL tradingenvironment design. 
Keywords
Deep reinforcement learning; cryptocurrency statistical arbitrage; observation frequency; 
temporal discretization; cointegration; trading-environment design; turnover; intraday trading; 
DQN; PPO; A2C.

1. Introduction
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) trading systems are often evaluated as if performance is 
mainly an algorithmic outcome. In practice, however, the agent does not interact with the market 
directly. It interacts with an engineered trading environment defined by empirical design choices 
such as observation frequency, state construction, reward timing, transaction costs, action 
constraints, and evaluation horizon. These choices determine what the agent observes, how often 
it can act, and how rewards are accumulated. As a result, DRL trading performance should not be 
interpreted only as a property of the learning algorithm; it is also shaped by the structure of the 
environment in which learning occurs (Sutton & Barto, 2018; Henderson et al., 2018; Liu et al., 
2022; Hambly et al., 2023).
Observation frequency is one of the most fundamental but least explicitly tested of these design 
choices. In intraday cryptocurrency trading, the same market can be represented using 15-minute, 
30-minute, 1-hour, 2-hour, or 4-hour observations. Each representation creates a different 
sequence of states, reward transitions, trading opportunities, and exposure-adjustment points. A 
shorter interval gives the agent more observations and more decision opportunities, but it may also 
increase short-horizon noise, turnover, and transaction-cost exposure. A longer interval may 
reduce noise and trading intensity, but it also provides fewer decision points and fewer realized 
reward intervals. Therefore, observation frequency is not a neutral preprocessing decision. It 
changes the temporal discretization of the trading problem.
This issue is especially important in cointegration-based statistical arbitrage. Classical statisticalarbitrage designs separate the empirical process into a formation period, where historical 
observations are used to estimate a tradable relationship, and a trading period, where that 
relationship is tested out of sample (Engle & Granger, 1987; Johansen, 1988; Gatev et al., 2006). 
In a cointegration-based DRL framework, the formation window is used to estimate the relativeprice relationship and construct synthetic arbitrage portfolios, while the trading horizon is used by 
the agent to make sequential trading decisions. Observation frequency affects both stages. It 
determines how many observations are available for estimating cointegration and how many state 
transitions are available for trading and reward realization.
Existing financial DRL research has shown that trading performance is sensitive to environment 
design, reward specification, transaction-cost treatment, action-space construction, and stochastic 
training variation (Henderson et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Hambly et al., 2023). 
Recent high-frequency reinforcement-learning studies also emphasize the difficulty of learning 
from long, noisy, and rapidly changing market trajectories (Qin et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2024). In 
cryptocurrency statistical arbitrage, Vergara and Kristjanpoller (2024) demonstrate that DRL can 
be combined with cointegration-based portfolio construction. Yet much of the literature evaluates 
trading systems at a fixed sampling interval. This leaves open an important methodological 
question: do reported DRL trading results reflect the trading framework itself, or are they partly 
driven by the observation frequency used to construct the environment?

This paper addresses that gap by treating observation frequency as the primary methodological 
treatment in cointegration-based DRL cryptocurrency statistical arbitrage. The study evaluates 
whether performance differs across 15-minute, 30-minute, 1-hour, 2-hour, and 4-hour observation 
frequencies while holding the economic scenario length constant. Each scenario contains a six-day 
formation window and a one-day trading horizon. Thus, all frequencies represent the same sevenday economic structure, but the number of observations, reward intervals, and trading 
opportunities changes with the sampling interval.
The central hypothesis is that observation frequency materially affects the performance of 
cointegration-based DRL statistical arbitrage. More formally, the study tests whether performance 
differs significantly across the five observation frequencies. The empirical design evaluates DQN, 
PPO, and A2C agents across four fixed cryptocurrency control panels, two action-space 
specifications, and three random seeds, producing 360 DRL experiment runs. The matched design 
allows each frequency to be compared under the same asset-panel, algorithm, action-space, and 
seed configuration. Friedman repeated-measures tests are used to evaluate whether performance 
differences across frequencies are statistically significant.
The results show that observation frequency significantly affects the DRL trading environment. 
Total return, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, annualized volatility, and turnover differ 
significantly across frequencies. The 15-minute setting produces the largest average losses and the 
highest turnover, while the 2-hour and 4-hour settings reduce losses and trading intensity. 
However, the findings do not imply consistent profitability. Mean returns remain negative across 
all five frequencies. The main result is therefore methodological: observation frequency changes 
the performance, risk, and trading behavior of the DRL system, but it does not by itself generate 
profitable arbitrage.
This paper makes three contributions. First, it formalizes observation frequency as a core design 
variable in DRL trading research. Rather than treating the sampling interval as a fixed 
preprocessing choice, the study shows that temporal discretization changes the number of states, 
reward transitions, turnover opportunities, and formation observations available to the agent. 
Second, it introduces a frequency-sensitive scenario design that holds the economic horizon 
constant while allowing the number of observations to vary across frequencies. This makes it 
possible to compare trading environments that cover the same calendar period but differ in 
temporal resolution. Third, it identifies turnover as a key mechanism linking observation frequency 
to performance. Shorter intervals create more opportunities to revise exposure, which can increase 
transaction-cost sensitivity and weaken net returns.
The study contributes to the broader methodological literature on financial DRL by showing that 
performance is jointly determined by the learning algorithm and the trading environment. A DQN, 
PPO, or A2C agent trained at 15-minute frequency is not solving the same decision problem as the 
same agent trained at 2-hour or 4-hour frequency. The state sequence, reward density, action 
opportunities, and transaction-cost exposure all differ. Therefore, empirical DRL trading studies 
should report, justify, and test the selected observation frequency rather than assuming that results 
obtained at one temporal resolution generalize across others.

2. Literature Review and Methodological Motivation
2.1 DRL Trading as an Engineered Environment
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) models trading as a sequential decision-making problem in 
which an agent observes a market state, selects an action, receives a reward, and updates its policy 
through repeated interaction with the environment. In financial applications, however, the trading 
environment is not naturally given. It is constructed through empirical design choices such as 
observation frequency, state representation, reward timing, transaction-cost treatment, actionspace specification, and evaluation horizon. These choices determine what the agent observes, how 
often it can trade, and how rewards are accumulated. This distinction is important because DRL 
trading performance cannot be interpreted as an algorithm-only outcome. The learned policy 
depends on the structure of the environment in which the agent is trained and evaluated. Prior 
studies show that DRL performance is sensitive to reward specification, action constraints, 
transaction costs, state design, and stochastic training variation (Henderson et al., 2018; Zhang et 
al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Hambly et al., 2023). Recent financial reinforcement-learning studies 
also emphasize that market noise, long trajectories, non-stationarity, and rapidly changing trading 
conditions complicate policy learning in high-frequency and cryptocurrency markets (Hambly et 
al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2024).
The present study focuses on one environment-design variable that is often fixed before empirical 
analysis: observation frequency. In intraday trading, the same market can be represented using 15minute, 30-minute, 1-hour, 2-hour, or 4-hour observations. Each representation creates a different 
sequence of states, reward transitions, and trading opportunities. Observation frequency is 
therefore treated in this paper as a core component of the DRL trading environment, rather than as 
a neutral data-preprocessing choice.
2.2 Observation Frequency as Temporal Discretization
Observation frequency determines how continuous market activity is converted into discrete 
decision states. In reinforcement learning, the state sequence, action timing, transition process, and 
reward feedback define the agent–environment interaction (Sutton & Barto, 2018). In financial 
trading, the sampling interval therefore determines not only how prices are observed, but also how 
often the agent can revise exposure and receive reward feedback. Shorter intervals provide more 
observations, more decision states, and more opportunities for position adjustment. This may 
increase the feedback available for learning, but it can also increase exposure to short-horizon 
noise, weak signal persistence, bid–ask effects, turnover, and transaction costs. Marketmicrostructure research shows that high-frequency financial data are affected by an information–

Longer intervals create the opposite trade-off. They may smooth transitory price movements and

Recent high-frequency and cryptocurrency RL studies reinforce this concern by showing that

the number of states, reward transitions, position-revision opportunities, and transaction-cost 
events available to the agent. 
2.3 Frequency Choice in Cointegration-Based Statistical Arbitrage
Cointegration-based statistical arbitrage requires a formation period to estimate a stable relativeprice relationship and a later trading period to evaluate deviations from that relationship out of 
sample. This formation–trading separation is consistent with classical cointegration and pairstrading frameworks (Engle & Granger, 1987; Johansen, 1988; Gatev et al., 2006). Observation 
frequency affects this structure because it changes the number of observations available for both 
estimation and trading. In the fixed six-day formation window used in this study, the 15-minute 
setting provides 576 formation observations, whereas the 4-hour setting provides only 36. Both 
settings cover the same calendar period, but they provide very different statistical samples for 
estimating cointegration. This distinction is important because sampling frequency affects the 
information–noise trade-off in financial data: shorter intervals provide more observations but may 
contain more microstructure noise, while longer intervals may smooth noise but reduce estimation 
information (O’Hara, 1995; Dacorogna et al., 2001; Hansen & Lunde, 2006; Bandi & Russell, 
2008). The exact observation counts follow from the fixed-horizon scenario design reported in 
Table 1.
The same logic applies to the trading horizon. A one-day trading window contains 96 observations 
at 15-minute frequency but only 6 observations at 4-hour frequency. Since DRL rewards are earned 
over transitions between consecutive observations, each frequency creates a different number of 
decision states, reward intervals, and possible position revisions. Therefore, observation frequency 
affects both the econometric layer, where cointegration is estimated, and the reinforcementlearning layer, where deviations are traded. Recent DRL and reinforcement-learning statisticalarbitrage studies support the relevance of this combined econometric–learning structure, but they 
generally do not isolate observation frequency while holding the economic scenario length 
constant (Vergara & Kristjanpoller, 2024; Yang & Malik, 2024; Guijarro-Ordonez et al., 2025).

Existing financial DRL research recognizes that trading performance depends on environment 
design, including state construction, reward shaping, action constraints, transaction costs, and 
stochastic training variation. However, observation frequency is commonly fixed before empirical 
analysis and is rarely tested as a formal design variable. This creates a methodological gap: if a 
DRL trading strategy is evaluated at only one sampling interval, it remains unclear whether the 
observed performance reflects the trading framework itself or the temporal discretization used to 
construct the environment. This study addresses that gap by treating observation frequency as the 
main experimental variable in a cointegration-based DRL statistical-arbitrage framework. The 
design holds the economic scenario length constant across frequencies by preserving a six-day 
formation window and a one-day trading horizon. The number of observations, decision states, 
reward transitions, and possible position revisions is then allowed to vary with the sampling 
interval. This structure isolates the effect of temporal discretization from the effect of calendar 
horizon.
The study tests the following hypothesis:
H1: The performance of cointegration-based DRL statistical arbitrage differs significantly across 
15-minute, 30-minute, 1-hour, 2-hour, and 4-hour observation frequencies.
Support for H1 would imply that observation frequency is not a neutral implementation detail. 
Instead, it would show that temporal discretization materially affects profitability, risk, turnover, 
transaction-cost exposure, and the learning environment faced by DRL statistical-arbitrage agents.
The methodological contribution of this paper is therefore to reposition observation frequency as 
a core design variable in financial DRL research. Rather than asking only which DRL algorithm 
performs best in a fixed environment, the study asks whether the same cointegration-based DRL 
framework remains stable when the temporal structure of the environment changes. This 
contributes to soft-computing research by providing a frequency-sensitive evaluation framework 
that links trading performance to interpretable mechanisms such as noise exposure, reward density, 
turnover, transaction-cost accumulation, and cointegration-estimation stability.

4.1 Frequency-Sensitive Evaluation Framework
This study develops a frequency-sensitive evaluation framework for cointegration-based deep 
reinforcement learning (DRL) cryptocurrency statistical arbitrage. The framework treats 
observation frequency as the primary methodological treatment rather than as a fixed 
preprocessing choice. This design is consistent with the reinforcement-learning view that an 
agent’s behavior depends on the environment in which states, actions, rewards, and transitions are 
generated (Sutton & Barto, 2018). In financial DRL, this issue is especially important because the 
trading environment is engineered through choices about sampling frequency, state construction, 
reward timing, transaction costs, and action constraints (Henderson et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022; 
Zhang et al., 2020; Hambly et al., 2023).
The empirical unit is a fixed economic scenario consisting of a six-day formation window followed 
by a one-day trading horizon. The formation window is used to estimate the cointegration 
relationship and construct the synthetic arbitrage portfolios, while the trading horizon is used to 
evaluate out-of-sample DRL decisions. This preserves the formation–trading separation used in 
statistical-arbitrage evaluation, where historical observations identify the trading relationship and 
later observations test its tradability (Gatev et al., 2006; Vergara & Kristjanpoller, 2024). The key 
design principle is that calendar time is held constant, while the number of observations varies 
with the sampling interval.

4.3 Scenario Generation and Data Alignment
For each observation frequency, asset prices are synchronized by timestamp within each control 
panel. Scenario blocks are then generated from contiguous observations so that the formation 
window immediately precedes the trading horizon. This structure prevents look-ahead bias 
because the cointegration relationship is estimated only from observations that occur before the 
trading period. The same scenario-generation rule is applied across all frequencies. The only 
element that changes is the number of observations implied by the sampling interval. Therefore, 
the comparison is driven by temporal discretization rather than by changes in calendar length, asset 
composition, or evaluation period.
Training scenarios are generated from the training sample, while final performance is evaluated 
on the out-of-sample testing period. A separation window between training and testing is retained 
to reduce immediate temporal carry-over between the final training observations and the first 
testing scenarios. This structure follows standard out-of-sample evaluation logic in statisticalarbitrage and financial machine-learning research, where model construction and model evaluation 
are temporally separated (Gatev et al., 2006; López de Prado, 2018).

4.6 Action-Space Specifications
Two action-space specifications are evaluated. The binary action space allows the agent to choose 
between the two full directional arbitrage positions:
𝒜binary = { ― 1, + 1}
When 𝑎𝑡= + 1, the agent is long portfolio A and short portfolio B. When 𝑎𝑡= ― 1, the agent is 
short portfolio A and long portfolio B. 
The granular action space allows full, half, and flat exposure:
𝒜granular = { ― 1, ― 0.5, 0, 0.5, + 1}
The granular specification allows the agent to reduce exposure when the signal is weak or when 
transaction costs make position changes less attractive. This design is consistent with the broader 
financial DRL literature, which shows that action-space construction can materially affect trading 
behavior and risk exposure (Zhang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022). In this paper, action-space design 
is not the main treatment variable. It is retained as a control dimension to test whether the frequency 
effect persists under different exposure structures.

4.8 DRL Agents and Training Protocol
Three DRL agents are evaluated: Deep Q-Network (DQN), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), 
and Advantage Actor–Critic (A2C). DQN represents a value-based method for discrete action 
spaces (Mnih et al., 2015). PPO and A2C represent actor–critic approaches that combine policy 
learning with value estimation (Mnih et al., 2016; Schulman et al., 2017). These agents are widely 
used in financial DRL applications and allow comparison between value-based and policy-gradient 
learning approaches (Liu et al., 2022; Hambly et al., 2023).
For each frequency, asset panel, algorithm, action space, and seed, the agent is trained using 
scenarios from the training sample and evaluated on the out-of-sample testing sample. The same 
feature set, reward function, transaction-cost rule, and evaluation metrics are used across all 
frequencies. This consistency is necessary because the objective is to isolate the effect of 
observation frequency.
Random seeds are included because DRL training is stochastic. Model initialization, scenario 
sampling, and exploration behavior can affect the learned policy. Using multiple seeds reduces 
dependence on a single successful or unsuccessful training run and allows frequency effects to be 
tested using matched experimental units. This is important because prior DRL reproducibility 
research shows that single-run results can be unstable and that seed variation should be reported 
when evaluating reinforcement-learning algorithms (Henderson et al., 2018).

4.11 Robustness and Sensitivity Checks
Several robustness checks are used to strengthen the methodological interpretation. First, the 
frequency test is repeated after excluding the 15-minute setting. This checks whether the result is 
driven only by the highest-frequency and potentially noisiest environment. Second, the test is 
repeated after excluding the 4-hour setting. This checks whether the result is driven by the lowestfrequency environment, where the formation window contains relatively few observations. Third, 
the Friedman test is repeated separately by algorithm. This evaluates whether frequency sensitivity 
is present for DQN, PPO, and A2C individually. Fourth, the test is repeated separately by actionspace specification. This evaluates whether the frequency effect holds under both binary and 
granular exposure rules.
These checks are important because the methodological claim is not simply that one frequency 
performs better than another. The stronger claim is that observation frequency changes the 
structure of the DRL trading environment in a way that affects performance, risk, and trading 
behavior across model configurations. The robustness results are reported in the empirical section 
after the main Friedman and pairwise tests. This placement allows the robustness analysis to 
function as a validation step rather than as a replacement for the primary hypothesis test.
5. Empirical Results
This section evaluates whether observation frequency affects the out-of-sample performance of 
the cointegration-based DRL statistical-arbitrage framework. The analysis uses a matched design 
of 360 DRL runs across five observation frequencies, four asset panels, three DRL agents, two 
action-space specifications, and three random seeds. Because each matched configuration is 
evaluated at all five frequencies, the results isolate the effect of temporal discretization from 
asset-panel, algorithm, action-space, and seed variation.

5.5 Turnover and the Trading-Intensity Channel
The turnover results provide the clearest mechanism behind the frequency effect. Mean turnover 
falls sharply as the observation interval lengthens: from 516.48 at 15 minutes to 28.97 at 4 hours. 
This pattern is expected because shorter frequencies create more decision points and more 
opportunities for the agent to revise exposure. To make this turnover channel clearer, Figure 3 
plots mean total turnover by observation frequency across the matched DRL configurations.

The turnover channel is central to the interpretation of the results. A shorter sampling interval does 
not only increase the amount of data; it also increases the number of possible trades. When 
transaction costs penalize exposure changes, a high-frequency environment can reduce net 
performance through overtrading. This explains why the 15-minute environment, despite 
providing the most observations, produces the weakest average return and Sharpe ratio. The result 
is consistent with financial DRL research showing that reward design, transaction-cost treatment, 
and environment construction can materially affect learned trading behavior (Zhang et al., 2020; 
Liu et al., 2022; Hambly et al., 2023).

5.7 Summary of Findings
The empirical evidence supports H1. Cointegration-based DRL statistical-arbitrage performance 
differs significantly across observation frequencies. The 15-minute setting produces the largest 
losses, weakest Sharpe ratios, and highest turnover. Lower-frequency settings, especially 2 hours 
and 4 hours, reduce losses and trading intensity. However, the findings should not be interpreted 
as evidence of consistently profitable arbitrage. Mean returns remain negative across all five 
frequencies.

The main result is methodological: observation frequency changes the DRL trading environment. 
It alters the number of market states, reward intervals, exposure-adjustment opportunities, and 
transaction-cost events available to the agent. Therefore, a DRL trading result obtained at one 
intraday frequency should not be assumed to generalize to other temporal discretization’s. In 
cointegration-based DRL statistical arbitrage, the sampling frequency is part of the model design, 
not a neutral data-formatting choice.
6. Discussion
6.1 Observation Frequency and the Structure of the Learning Problem
The results show that observation frequency changes the decision environment faced by the DRL 
agents. A shorter sampling interval increases the number of states, reward transitions, and 
opportunities for exposure adjustment, while a longer interval compresses the same economic 
period into fewer decision points. This means that agents trained at different frequencies are not 
solving the same reinforcement-learning problem, even when the asset panels, algorithms, state 
variables, reward function, and transaction-cost rule are held constant. This finding supports the 
broader view that DRL trading performance is conditional on the environment in which learning 
occurs. In financial applications, the environment is engineered through design choices such as 
state representation, action constraints, reward timing, transaction costs, and sampling frequency 
(Sutton & Barto, 2018; Henderson et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022; Hambly et al., 2023). The present 
evidence extends this argument by showing that temporal discretization is itself a first-order 
environment-design variable.
6.2 Why Higher Frequency Does Not Necessarily Improve Performance
The 15-minute setting provides the largest number of observations and reward intervals, but it 
produces the weakest performance. This suggests that more frequent observations do not 
automatically provide more useful learning information. Shorter intervals may increase exposure 
to transient price movements, unstable spread deviations, and short-horizon noise. The agent 
receives more state transitions, but those transitions may contain weaker or less tradable signals.
By contrast, the 2-hour and 4-hour settings reduce the number of decision points and appear to 
limit the severity of losses. This does not imply that lower frequency is universally superior. 
Rather, it indicates a trade-off between information density and signal quality. Higher-frequency 
environments may improve responsiveness, but they can also increase noise and overtrading. 
Lower-frequency environments may reduce noise and turnover, but they also reduce the number 
of opportunities for the agent to adapt. This trade-off is consistent with recent high-frequency RL 
research, which emphasizes the difficulty of learning under noisy, fast-changing market states (Qin 
et al., 2024; Zong et al., 2024).
6.3 Turnover as the Main Transmission Channel
The clearest mechanism linking frequency to performance is turnover. Shorter observation 
intervals create more opportunities for the agent to revise exposure. When transaction costs 
penalize exposure changes, more decision points can translate into higher cost accumulation.

Therefore, the weaker performance of the 15-minute setting is not only a return effect; it reflects a 
trading-intensity effect. This interpretation is important because it connects the statistical result to 
an economic mechanism. A high-frequency DRL environment does not simply offer more data. It 
also offers more chances to trade, switch exposure, and incur costs. In noisy settings, that flexibility 
may become harmful if the agent reacts too frequently to weak or unstable signals. The turnover 
result therefore supports the methodological argument that observation frequency changes how the 
agent interacts with the market, not only how performance is measured.
6.4 Cointegration-Estimation Trade-Off
Observation frequency also affects the econometric layer of the framework. Because the formation 
window is fixed at six days, the number of observations available for estimating cointegration 
differs sharply across frequencies. The 15-minute setting provides many formation observations, 
while the 4-hour setting provides relatively few. This creates a second trade-off: higher frequencies 
may provide more data for estimation but with greater short-horizon noise, whereas lower 
frequencies may reduce noise but weaken the statistical basis for estimating a stable cointegration 
relationship. The 4-hour results should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The lower turnover and 
smaller average losses are informative for frequency sensitivity, but the short formation sample 
may increase estimation uncertainty. In cointegration-based statistical arbitrage, trading quality 
depends on the reliability of the relationship estimated during the formation period (Engle & 
Granger, 1987; Johansen, 1988; Gatev et al., 2006). The results therefore do not identify a 
universally optimal frequency; they show that frequency choice changes both the learning problem 
and the estimation problem.
6.5 Implications for DRL Trading Research
The findings have direct implications for empirical DRL trading studies. A strategy evaluated at a 
single sampling interval may produce conclusions that are specific to that temporal discretization. 
If frequency is not tested, it is difficult to know whether the result reflects the algorithm, the reward 
function, the asset structure, or the sampling design. This matters for model comparison. A DQN, 
PPO, or A2C agent trained at 15-minute frequency is not directly comparable to the same agent 
trained at 2-hour frequency unless the change in environment structure is explicitly recognized. 
The state sequence, reward density, turnover opportunities, and transaction-cost exposure all 
change. Therefore, observation frequency should be reported, justified, and tested as part of DRL 
trading-environment design.
6.6 Practical Interpretation
For practitioners, the results caution against assuming that higher-frequency trading environments 
are automatically preferable. More frequent observations can increase responsiveness, but they can 
also raise turnover and transaction-cost exposure. Lower-frequency settings can reduce trading 
intensity, but they may also provide fewer observations for cointegration estimation and fewer 
opportunities for adaptation. The practical implication is therefore not to choose the 2-hour or 4hour setting mechanically. Instead, frequency should be selected jointly with signal stability, 
transaction costs, liquidity, execution assumptions, and risk controls. The central lesson is that 
sampling frequency is part of the trading-system design, not a neutral data-formatting decision.

7. Conclusion
This study examined observation frequency as a methodological design variable in cointegrationbased DRL cryptocurrency statistical arbitrage. By reconstructing the same six-day formation 
window and one-day trading horizon across 15-minute, 30-minute, 1-hour, 2-hour, and 4-hour 
observations, the analysis isolates the effect of temporal discretization on the DRL trading 
environment. The evidence shows that performance differs significantly across observation 
frequencies. The 15-minute environment produces the largest losses and highest turnover, while 
the 2-hour and 4-hour environments reduce losses and trading intensity. However, the results do 
not show consistent profitability across frequencies. Mean returns remain negative overall, so the 
contribution is methodological rather than performance-promotional.
The main contribution is to show that observation frequency changes the structure of the learning 
environment. It affects the number of states, reward intervals, exposure-adjustment opportunities, 
turnover events, and formation observations available to the agent. As a result, DRL trading 
performance should not be interpreted as an algorithm-only outcome. It is jointly determined by 
the learning algorithm and the environment in which that algorithm is trained and evaluated.
The study has several limitations. The analysis uses Binance USDT-denominated spot data and 
OHLCV observations, so it does not fully capture order-book depth, realized slippage, market 
impact, or exchange-specific execution frictions. The results are based on one recent 
cryptocurrency sample period and may differ across bull, bear, crisis, or low-volatility regimes. 
The 4-hour setting also contains relatively few formation and trading observations, which may 
affect cointegration-estimation stability.
Future research should test frequency sensitivity across additional exchanges, market regimes, 
transaction-cost assumptions, and asset classes. A useful extension would be to develop adaptive 
or multi-frequency DRL environments in which the agent can combine short-horizon information 
with lower-frequency mean-reversion signals. Overall, the findings suggest that DRL trading 
studies should report, justify, and test their selected observation frequency rather than assuming 
that results obtained at one temporal resolution generalize to others.
