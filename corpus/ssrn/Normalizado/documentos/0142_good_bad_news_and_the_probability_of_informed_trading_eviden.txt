Good (bad) news and the probability of informed trading: Evidence from illegal insider 
trading
Shunyu Su1 and Yezhou Sha1*
1: School of Finance, Capital University of Economics and Business
*: Corresponding author. Email: shayezhou@cueb.edu.cn
Abstract
We present a closed-form solution connecting the probability of informed trading (PIN) to the 
overlooked parameter that signaling private information is good or bad. Estimating PIN using illegal 
insider trading data, we find it sensitive to the certainty of positive private information in addition 
to the existed explanations, offering a new explanation for PIN's limitations in prior literature.
Keywords: Probability of informed trading; liquidity; information asymmetry; insider trading

G12; G14

1.
Introduction
The Probability of informed trading (PIN, see Easley, Keifer, O’Hara and Paperman, 1996) is 
frequently employed in financial market research to measure how private information affects asset 
prices. By considering the size of trades, PIN models order imbalances triggered by informed traders 
who trade stocks based on the private information signal. The model has been widely applied in the 
literature as a proxy of informed trading, such as buy and sells around M&A announcement (Aktas, 
de Bodt, Declerck and Oppens,2007; Na and Kim 2021), self-reported trading by firm’s top manager 
or blockholders (Cai, He and He, 2010), and insider trading (see for example, Lee, Lemmon, Li and 
Sequeira 2014; Ahern 2020). Although these terms are often used interchangeably in accounting 
and finance research, few question whether PIN can truly capture different types of informed trading.
When private information is not market-wide observable, the probability that such information 
signals a good or bad news is intuitively equiprobable. However, this intuition faces two challenges. 
The first is the certainty of informed information. Na and Kim (2021) and other studies have found 
that PIN is higher when the signal of private information become clearer. Second, PIN is sensitive 
to the market’s microstructure, such as short selling (Henry, 2019), and bid-ask spread (Lenkey, 
2021). This research calls for a systematic review on PIN’s suitability when these parameters are 
set by market conditions.  
In this paper, we first examine the relation between the private information’s signal and PIN by 
giving a numeric solution. Starting from the estimation of Easley’s PIN, we differentiate the PIN 
function and show there is a partial derivative between PIN and the probability that private 
information is good or bad, denoted 𝛿 in relevant literature. The function of partial derivatives 
gives direct supporting evidence that any given 𝛿 is relating to a unique PIN value. We then assign 
𝛿 with different value and visualize its economic implications by plotting the value of PIN under 
different scenarios.
We have two findings from empirical anlaysis. First, by using real-market data, the estimation of 
PIN following Griffin, Oberoi, Oduro (2021)’s approach is particularly sensitive to whether 𝛿 is 
non-zero (i.e. the private information is a sure thing and is a bad news). Even a very small change 
of 𝛿 leads to a kinked PIN estimate. Second, the determinative power of 𝛿 is independent from 
stock’s market value and bid-ask spread, two proxies for investor’s attention and market 
microstructure. These findings suggest that the relation between private information signal and 𝑃𝐼𝑁 
supplements the literature of understand informed trading by offering a new possible underlying 
channel.

3.
Insider trading and estimation
We use illegal insider trading from China’s stock market in the year of 2011-2018 as a sample to 
estimate 𝑃𝐼𝑁 and then verify the implication of Equation 3 and Equation 4. Insider trading cases 
are manually collected from China’s legal documents i.e. court verdicts, administrative orders. 
Legal documents reveal some important trading-level information, such as the date of insider buy, 
the transaction volume, spot price and the characters of insider information. All of which is to gauge 
a precise framework when likelihood of informed trading is clustered.
Using China as the test sample has some important advantage. First, due to the long lasting shortselling ban in China, only positive insider information is exploitable for profit and hence the 
following insider action is to buy the target firm’s stock. This implies that the parameters are predetermined: 𝛼= 1, and 𝛿= 0. Second, comparing with informed trading, insider trading is purely 
driven by the particular private information and is traded on the dates prior to the firm’s 
announcement. This alleviates the cofounding effect around announcement day, where other 
information publicized in the announcement may drive the trading behavior to the other way (Batten 
et al., 2021).
We obtain order information from RESSET, a Chinese data vendor that supplies 10-minute 
frequency trading information for stocks that traded at China’s Shenzhen Stock Exchange (SZSE). 
The high-frequency trading data is then used to estimate 𝑃𝐼𝑁 at daily level. We estimate each 
stock’s 𝑃𝐼𝑁 15 days before and after the first buy from insiders. After removing observations with 
key information missing, we got 2,787 𝑃𝐼𝑁 observations from 91 court fillings of insider trading. 
The estimation method strictly follows Griffin et al., (2021)’s method. Table 1 reports the estimation 
result and relevant parameters.

4.
Conclusion
We demonstrate that in the Probability of Informed Trading (𝑃𝐼𝑁) model, the implied 
probability of private information being good or bad plays an independent role not extensively 
investigated in prior literature. To test this relationship, we estimate 𝑃𝐼𝑁 using data from illegal 
insider trading, a type of informed trading driven by a clear price signal. Our findings confirm our 
model's proposition and reveal a new channel for understanding insider trading through the

Our findings do not invalidate the PIN model. Our motivation stems from the confusion 
between the terms "informed trading" and "insider trading." The simplification of these concepts 
leads to PIN's misuse, as it should not be employed to gauge the probability of insider trading. Future 
research should clarify the distinction between informed trading and insider trading from the outset. 
A new model for capturing insider trading is needed, but it should not be based on the order 
imbalance theory. We believe that this is where our contribution lies.
