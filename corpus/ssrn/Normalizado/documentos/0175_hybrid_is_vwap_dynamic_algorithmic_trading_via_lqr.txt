Abstract
For optimal trade execution, static models are useful for pre-trade estimation or benchmarking, but real trading must be based on dynamic decision making or dynamic programming
(DP). DP algorithms can adjust execution automatically to real-time market price waves as
well as inventory positions, and thus are more responsive and realistic. Development of DP
models however faces a few notorious obstacles including the curse of dimensionality, absence of
closed-form solutions for general formulations, and diﬃculty in incorporating hard constraints.
The DP approach based on linear-quadratic regulators (LQR) was ﬁrst introduced to algorithmic trading by Hora [6]. In Dynamic Control Theory, LQR models allow closed-form solutions
thanks to the Bellman equations and quadratic value functions. The resulted optimal policies
are aﬃne functions of the state variables. The current work improves the LQR model of Hora [6]
in several important areas, including (a) the unconditional stability (or convexity), (b) retiring of the second-order small term of stagewise risk aversion, (c) quasi risk aversion achieved
by time-varying delay costs, (d) incorporation of the bid-oﬀer spreads, and (e) hybridizing the
implementation shortfall (IS) algorithm with the VWAP approach to softly enforce the constraint of completion. The impact models and price dynamics still follow the earlier framework
of Huberman and Stanzl [7]. The motivations behind every component of the improved model
are elaborated, and the closed-form LQR solutions are derived (Theorem 1 and 2). Numerical
simulation of the LQR trading paths is presented, which conﬁrms several desirable properties
of the hybrid IS-VWAP model.
Keywords:
dynamic programming, LQR, IS, VWAP, slippage, spread, delay cost, impact
cost, Bellman equation, value function, optimal policy, positive matrix, stability.

Introduction
In a complex and yet constantly evolving trading environment, algorithmic execution has to be
dynamic in order to rationally swim through the dynamic waves of market prices or volumes, as
well as one’s own inventory positions. This has been discussed or demonstrated in a number of
existing works, e.g., Bertsimas and Lo [4], and many others [1, 7, 6, 10, 5], just to cite a few.
The main tigers along the path of dynamic-programming (DP) based trading include the curse
of dimensionality and the absence of closed-form solutions for most DP models. This has baﬄed
any serious attempts of formulating self-contained and rigorous DP trading models that both allow
tractable solutions and make actual economic sense. For example, there have been even no rigorous
DP models with tractable solutions that could solidly implement the two basic client requests:
• Completion: to successfully acquire or liquidate a requested position Q0 by a pre-determined
ending time T, and
• PoV Limit: to trade within the participation-of-volume (PoV) limit range often speciﬁed
by a savvy client, e.g., PoV ≤15.0%.
Let Qt denote the residual position at time t that still needs to be ﬁlled.
A naive scheme for
completion, such as requesting on the last trading bin [T −1, T) to trade xT−1 = QT−1, could be
highly risky. It could temporarily deplete the market liquidity and stir up the ﬂash-crash kind of
market instabilities. Neither market regulators (e.g., SEC, FINRA, or CFTC in the US) nor clients
would like to see this to happen.
Among all the DP trading models that allow explicit closed-form solutions and whose solutions
also make actual ﬁnancial sense, is the LQR approach ﬁrst proposed by Hora [6]. Recall in Dynamic
Control Theory that the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) is a classic type of control problems and
solutions under linear state transitions and additive quadratic cost functions [3].
Hora’s LQR model is built upon the price dynamics initially proposed by Huberman and
Stanzl [7], i.e., linear instantaneous price impacts settled into linear permanent impacts. Unlike
Huberman and Stanzl [7] who targeted at the global mean-variance cost function (as by Almgren
and Chriss [2]), Hora formulated the DP model using stagewise or localized mean-variance costs
instead. The stagewise costs also incorporated a new quadratic term dubbed “the holding cost.”
In combination, Hora showed that under certain conditions the proposed LQR model can result in
well-behaved solutions that actually react to price waves.
The current work attempts to improve Hora’s model [6] in a number of important areas: (a)
choice of working variables, (b) reformulation of holding costs, or what we call the “delay” cost,
(c) incorporation of a new quadratic term to penalize market price slippages caused by cumulative
permanent impacts, (d) inclusion of the bid-oﬀer spread cost, and (e) hybridizing the improved
IS algorithm with the popular VWAP algorithm. In combination, these improvements result in a
novel hybrid IS-VWAP DP model
• that for each bin t directly outputs the optimal PoVs, instead of absolute shares;
• that softly enforces the completion constraint;
• that has an unconditionally stable LQR solution; and
• that trades in a style hybridizing both the IS and VWAP algorithms.
The rest of the paper has been organized as follows. The general model assumptions and trading
settings are introduced in Section 2. In Section 3, we elaborate further on areas where Hora’s model
in [6] needs further improvements. We then develop in details the improved IS model based on
the LQR framework in Section 4, and the hybrid IS-VWAP model in Section 5. Computational
examples and simulation results are demonstrated in Section 6. The work is concluded with several
disclaimers and cautions in Section 7.

Basic Modeling Assumptions and Trading Settings
The basic trading settings and key assumptions for the current work are listed as follows.
In
summary, the current work focuses on the optimal execution of a single name at the parent-slicing
level, assuming an in-house child order placement (COP) scheme is already available.
(i) (Single Name) The current work focuses on single-name execution problems, preferably
in the equity space where a single liquid stock or ETF is to be acquired or liquidated. The
security must be liquid enough so that a continuous and positive intraday volume proﬁle
exists.
(ii) (Running Example: Buy) The running example throughout is a buy trade. For a sell
trade, simply ﬂip the signs of the residual positions Qt’s and trading shares xt’s or the PoVs
ut’s. In actual implementation, one can introduce a new side variable, i.e., side = +1 for a
buy trade and side = −1 for a sell trade.
(iii) (Position Size) We assume that the initial position Q0 to be acquired or liquidated can be
executed during an intraday window (i.e., “the horizon”) without much risk of disrupting the
market. This means that if the VWAP algorithm is to be adopted, the associated PoV rate
over the execution horizon sits somewhere below 20% or 30%. PoV rates at the lower end enjoy
more eﬀective linear approximations, and thus allow us to adopt the linear impact models by
Huberman and Stanzl [7].
This choice is the same as in Hora [6] and is fundamental for
generating both linear state transitions and quadratic costs required by the LQR framework.
(iv) (COP and SOR) It is assumed that a child order placement (COP) scheme has been in
place. The current work only deals with the parent slicing problem, and the output is a series
of optimal trading policies that specify how many (child) shares xt to trade on any incoming
time bin [t, t+1). The COP then manages the actual execution of the xt shares over [t, t+1),
by a combination of sophisticated order placement strategies and a smart order routing (SOR)
algorithm. The SOR is key for eﬀectively accessing diﬀerent liquidity venues. We refer to our
earlier works in [10, 9] for related discussion on COP and SOR, and their potential impact on
in-house model calibration.
(v) (Child Bin) We assume that an intraday execution horizon [0, T] has been discretized to N
equal-length child bins with duration ∆t. For a liquid name, for instance, one can set ∆t = 30
seconds or 1 minute.
To compress and simplify notations, unless a confusing scenario arises, the time variable t
is used to represent both the continuous time and the associated discretized bin index. For
example, t+1 means the next bin index from t, while t+∆t means the same in the continuous
setting. The same practice applies to the terminal time T, which could represent the actual
continuous time or the discretized terminal index N = T/∆t.
Finally, we have listed on one of the cover pages both the terms and variables used throughout
the paper frequently and consistently.

Working Variables
Most existing DP models, including Hora [6] and Huberman and Stanzl [7] for example, work with
the trading shares xt directly. But working with the PoV variable ut is more natural and beneﬁcial
for the following reasons.
First, the market impact caused by shares xt is always relative to a security’s liquidity level.
The total market volume vt is a good proxy for gauging market liquidity (though it could be
deceptive during disruptive trading sessions as cautioned in SEC’s investigation reports). Then
market impacts can be better formulated on the PoV variable:
ut = xt
vt
,
for any time bin t.
When the PoVs are moderate, both the permanent and instantaneous market impacts can be
reasonably approximated by linear functions of ut.
Another advantage of working with the PoV variable ut is that model parameters can remain
invariant for diﬀerent trades of a same security. Ideally model parameters should depend only on
the market or security information, but not on speciﬁc trade requests such as the position size Q0.
The PoV control ut also allows the solutions to integrate seamlessly with any in-house volume
predictor ˆVt, which is an indispensable component in any heuristic implementation of a DP model.
The trading position at t is then given by xt = ut ˆVt, and clearly reacts to the volume ˆVt.
Finally, in Section 5 we shall demonstrate that the PoV variable ut serves as a bridge between
IS and VWAP that results in a more eﬀective hybrid IS-VWAP model.

4
The Improved IS Model via LQR
Throughout we assume that the main task is to execute Q0 shares of a given single name during
a designated intraday horizon. For convenience, the horizon is normalized to a time interval [0, T],
and T is called the terminal time. Notice that in general t = 0 does not have to be the start of the
open trading session, e.g., 9:30am for the equity market in the USA. In fact, a savvy client would
usually prefer to stay away from the volatile opening minutes.
As in Section 2, it is assumed that a child window length ∆t has been chosen (e.g., 2 minutes),
and the horizon is discretized to time bins indexed by n = 0, 1, . . . , N with N∆t = T. As explained
earlier, to compress notation usage, we shall use the same symbol t for both the continuous time
and the associated discrete bin index n (= t/∆t). Thus for example, we will denote the bins also
by 0, . . . , t, . . . , T, and t + 1 refers to the next bin following the t-bin.

Delay Costs
We now discuss how to properly design the delay cost weights βt’s, which must be time varying.
In the extreme case if one sets βT = +∞at the terminal, completion is then enforced, i.e.,

On the Soft Implementation of the Completion Constraint
In terms of softly enforcing the completion constraint, both λ and θ play a key role. A stronger λ
enhances risk aversion and front loading, and hence in general helps complete the execution even
before the ending time T. However, such early completion may come at the potential cost of missing
later opportunities of price improvement.
Thus a better way to honor completion is to employ both a moderately strong λ and a moderately strong θ. The resulting moderate front loading helps execute a major portion in the beginning
phase. At the later stages of execution, the moderately strong θ ensures that the hybrid model
trades in a neighborhood of the VWAP model. This means that the optimal PoV rate u∗
t will stay
in the vicinity of the VWAP PoV rate (i.e., 10.8% for these examples), and hence the algorithm
virtually trades above some minimum PoV rate. In the three examples in Figure 1, 2, and 3, for
instance, the algorithms all trade above the PoV of 5% or 6%. This eﬀectively prohibits idling and
promotes timely completion during the later stages of execution.

(3) While the outputs (i.e., the optimal PoV policies u∗
t ’s) can be heuristically applicable to even
stochastic volumes, the current model itself is not built upon stochastic volumes nor stochastic
volatilities. Interested readers are refer to works such as Almgren [1] for related explorations.
(4) As the current work focuses mainly on the closed-form solutions of the LQR approach, any
actual implementation must take necessary post-processing steps to make the solutions more
practical and operationally safer.
For example, one may have to ﬂoor or cap the optimal
PoV policies u∗
t ’s using a minimum or maximum PoV rate. Similarly, the DP nature of the
algorithm in general cannot guarantee completion before the horizon ends, though many of our
simulation experiments have shown that completion is generally achieved when the quasi risk
aversion parameter λ is strong enough, or when λ and θ are both moderately strong.
(5) Execution houses who are interested in the current work and plan to implement it in their
systems should be aware of any other securities or operational risks that are not speciﬁcally
addressed in the current work, including for examples, IPO’s (initial public oﬀerings) without
much trading histories, complex market opens or closes, trading halts, or any unexpected
disruptive market events, etc.
