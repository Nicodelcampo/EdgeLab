Abstract 
This paper examines Indian electricity markets through the analogy of traditional 
agricultural spot markets — locally known as sabzi mandis — to illuminate fundamental 
dynamics of price discovery, bidding behavior, volatility, and allocation efficiency in 
formal power exchanges. While electricity markets operate through sophisticated exchange 
mechanisms such as the Day-Ahead Market (DAM), Real-Time Market (RTM), and Green 
Day-Ahead Market (GDAM), many of their underlying behavioral and structural dynamics 
bear a striking resemblance to informal commodity markets characterized by perishable 
goods, fluctuating supply, demand shocks, strategic bidding, and time-sensitive clearing 
decisions. 
Drawing on market microstructure theory, auction design literature, and practitionerinformed perspectives on Indian power markets, this paper develops a four-proposition 
analytical framework that maps key behavioral drivers — perishability-induced urgency, 
information asymmetry, behavioral volatility, and flexibility premia — from the mandi 
context onto power exchange outcomes. The study argues that informal market analogies, 
far from being mere rhetorical devices, offer structurally valid insights into improving 
efficiency, procurement strategy, and market design in emerging power markets. 
The paper proceeds in eight sections: following this introduction, Section 2 establishes the 
sabzi mandi as a rigorous economic analogy rather than a stylistic metaphor. Section 3 
maps the structure of Indian power exchanges. Section 4 develops the four core 
microstructure propositions. Section 5 presents illustrative case examples. Section 6 
discusses implications for utility procurement strategy. Section 7 offers policy 
recommendations. Section 8 concludes with reflections on what formal markets can learn 
from informal ones. 
 
1. Introduction 
Electricity is, in most respects, unlike any other commodity. It cannot be stored cheaply at 
scale, flows at the speed of light, requires continuous balancing of supply and demand in 
real time, and its failure can cascade into catastrophic grid instability. These characteristics

have long been used to argue that electricity markets require unique institutional structures 
— formal exchanges, regulatory oversight, and engineering-driven dispatch mechanisms 
— that set them apart from ordinary commodity markets. 
This paper proposes that despite these differences, the behavioral dynamics of electricity 
markets share deep structural similarities with one of the oldest forms of commerce: the 
informal vegetable market, or sabzi mandi. This is not a superficial comparison. Both 
markets are fundamentally governed by the same economic forces: perishable goods that 
cannot be inventoried overnight, supply that varies with weather and nature, demand that 
spikes at predictable intervals, buyers and sellers with asymmetric information about 
market conditions, and price signals that must clear the market in real time. 
The Indian electricity market, administered primarily through the Indian Energy Exchange 
(IEX) and Power Exchange India Limited (PXIL), has grown substantially over the last 
two decades. Exchange-based trading now encompasses multiple market segments — the 
Day-Ahead Market (DAM), Real-Time Market (RTM), and Green Day-Ahead Market 
(GDAM) — each reflecting a different time horizon for price discovery and delivery. Yet 
despite their formal, algorithm-driven design, these markets exhibit patterns of volatility, 
strategic behavior, and inefficiency that mirror precisely what one observes in a busy 
wholesale vegetable market in Delhi or Mumbai on a monsoon morning. 
The contribution of this paper is threefold. First, it develops the sabzi mandi analogy as a 
formal market microstructure framework, not merely a pedagogical device. Second, it 
identifies four empirically motivated propositions about bidding behavior and price 
dynamics in Indian power exchanges. Third, it derives practical implications for utility 
procurement strategy and power market policy design. The paper situates these insights 
within the broader literature on market microstructure, auction theory, and emerging 
market design.

The sabzi mandi, in its most sophisticated form, is not a chaotic bazaar — it is a 
sophisticated price discovery mechanism. Prices in mandis respond to supply signals, 
aggregate information about quality and availability, and balance the interests of multiple 
sellers and buyers with heterogeneous costs and valuations. This is precisely what a wellfunctioning power exchange is supposed to do. The analogy thus serves as a lens through 
which to examine where Indian power exchanges succeed and where they fall short.

Akerlof's (1970) insight about information asymmetry in markets — originally developed

experienced mandi trader who arrives at 5 AM and knows yesterday's prices at three other 
mandis has a decisive informational advantage over the small retailer arriving at 8 AM. 
Similarly, a large DISCOM with sophisticated load forecasting and market intelligence has 
structural advantages in the power exchange that smaller or less-resourced participants 
cannot easily replicate. 
 
3. Structure of Indian Power Exchanges 
India's electricity market has evolved from a purely centralized planning model to one 
incorporating competitive exchange-based trading. The transformation began with the 
Electricity Act of 2003, which introduced open access and enabled third-party trading. 
Power exchanges were subsequently established — the Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) in 
2008 and Power Exchange India Limited (PXIL) — and the Central Electricity Regulatory 
Commission (CERC) has progressively expanded market segments. 
Today, the Indian power exchange operates across three primary time-window markets, 
each serving a distinct purpose in the price discovery and dispatch sequence.

The DAM is analogous to buying vegetables a day in advance — the mandi equivalent of 
bulk wholesale procurement where prices are relatively stable and both buyer and seller 
can plan around the clearing price. The RTM, by contrast, is the equivalent of the last-hour 
rush at the mandi: prices are volatile, participants are under time pressure, and information 
asymmetries are most acute. The GDAM is the equivalent of a specialty organic stall — a

dedicated segment for a differentiated product that trades at a premium to reflect its 
distinctive attributes. 
Market clearing in both the DAM and GDAM follows a double-sided auction mechanism: 
generators submit supply bids (quantity and price) and utilities (DISCOMs) submit demand 
bids. The exchange algorithm matches these bids using a merit-order approach, 
establishing a uniform Market Clearing Price (MCP) at which all accepted transactions 
settle. The RTM uses a similar mechanism but with far shorter timelines, creating 
heightened urgency and more volatile clearing prices. 
 
4. Market Microstructure Insights: Four Propositions 
This section develops four propositions that represent the core analytical contribution of 
the paper. Each proposition maps a behavioral or structural feature of the sabzi mandi to a 
testable hypothesis about power exchange dynamics. 
 
Proposition 1: Perishability Creates Urgency-Driven Pricing 
In markets with perishable goods and no storage, sellers face a binary outcome: sell now or 
lose value entirely. This creates a structural downward pressure on prices as the clearing 
deadline approaches and unsold inventory must be liquidated at any available price. 
 
Electricity is the most perishable commodity in existence. A megawatt of power that is not 
injected into the grid at its scheduled time is simply lost — there is no storage, no inventory 
to carry forward, no possibility of returning to the market tomorrow with the same product. 
This is precisely the economics of the vegetable vendor at 7 PM facing leftover tomatoes. 
The urgency is identical; only the physical form of the perishable good differs. 
In the RTM, this dynamic manifests clearly. Generators who have committed to generation 
— particularly thermal plants with minimum technical load constraints — face mounting 
pressure to sell as the 15-minute window closes. This creates predictable patterns of bid 
adjustment, with supply bids often declining toward the close of the bidding window as 
sellers reveal their true reservation prices. The implication for market design is that

monitoring the intraday price trajectory of RTM bids can reveal information about 
generators' true cost structures that is not apparent from their initial bids. 
 
Proposition 2: Information Asymmetry Affects Bidding Efficiency 
When participants have unequal access to market information — prices at other exchanges, 
demand forecasts, weather data, and grid congestion signals — those with superior 
information extract rents from those without, reducing aggregate market efficiency. 
 
The experienced mandi trader who has built relationships with truckers, mandis in other 
states, and retail buyers has a decisive advantage. She knows when a glut is coming before 
prices reflect it. The power market analog is equally clear: large utilities with sophisticated 
meteorological forecasting, real-time monitoring of neighboring state load, and experience 
in reading CERC congestion signals are systematically better positioned than smaller 
DISCOMs or new entrants. 
This information asymmetry is particularly consequential in the GDAM, where renewable 
energy generation is inherently more uncertain and forecast-dependent than thermal. A 
DISCOM that has invested in accurate solar irradiance forecasting can bid more precisely 
— neither over-procuring (leading to costly surplus) nor under-procuring (leading to 
expensive real-time balancing purchases). The gap between sophisticated and 
unsophisticated participants in forecast quality may thus be the single most important 
determinant of procurement cost differences across otherwise similar utilities. 
 
Proposition 3: Volatility is Often Behavioral, Not Purely Fundamental 
Extreme price movements in electricity markets frequently exceed what fundamental supplydemand imbalances would predict. Behavioral factors — herd bidding, anchor effects, and 
strategic overreaction — amplify fundamental volatility, creating both risks and opportunities 
for sophisticated participants. 
 
A vegetable market in which one major buyer suddenly raises her bids can trigger a cascade 
of price revisions by other buyers who interpret this as a signal of scarcity — even if the 
underlying supply has not changed. This herding behavior is well documented in financial

8 
markets (Shiller, 2000) and agricultural spot markets alike. Power exchanges are not 
immune to similar dynamics. 
In the RTM, where clearing occurs every 15 minutes and participants can observe prior 
clearing prices, behavioral anchoring to recent prices can produce systematic over- and 
under-reactions. A spike in one clearing window — driven, say, by a sudden thermal unit 
outage — may cause utilities to overbid in the subsequent window even as the outage is 
resolved. Conversely, a period of low prices may lull participants into underbidding, 
leaving them short when demand unexpectedly rises. These patterns suggest that a portion 
of observed RTM price volatility is an artifact of participant behavior rather than 
fundamental supply-demand conditions. 
 
Proposition 4: Flexible Participants Capture Disproportionate Value 
In markets with high price volatility and time-sensitive clearing, participants who can rapidly 
adjust their demand or supply positions capture a disproportionate share of the gains from 
trade relative to those locked into fixed schedules. 
 
In the mandi, the trader with a refrigerated truck can afford to wait for a better price. The 
vendor with perishable goods on a bicycle cannot. Flexibility commands a premium. This 
logic applies directly to power markets: a utility with demand-response capability, storage 
assets, or flexible generation can strategically time its market participation to procure at 
low-price moments and sell or defer at high-price moments. 
The emergence of storage as a tradeable commodity — and the potential for AI-driven 
dispatch optimization — suggests that this flexibility premium will grow substantially in 
the coming decade. The market is, in this sense, evolving from a mandi that only trades 
vegetables to one that also trades refrigeration capacity, shelf space, and logistics — 
precisely the "beyond electricity" dimension identified in recent IEX market reports. 
 
5. Illustrative Case Examples

The following three illustrative scenarios, drawn from the structural dynamics of Indian 
power markets, demonstrate how the four propositions manifest in practice. Names and 
specific quantities are illustrative. 
5.1 The Demand Surge Event 
Consider a northern state utility facing an unexpected heatwave in May. Temperature 
forecasts underestimated peak cooling demand by 15%. The utility, having procured its 
expected requirement in the DAM, finds itself 800 MW short as afternoon temperatures 
exceed 44°C. It enters the RTM as an emergency buyer. 
Proposition 1 operates in reverse here: it is the buyer, not the seller, who is urgently 
perishable — the demand deficit must be met within the 15-minute window or grid 
instability follows. Prices in that block spike sharply. Proposition 4 is illustrated by the fact 
that a neighboring utility with excess contracted hydro capacity, able to increase its RTM 
supply offer quickly, captures the majority of the rent from this event. 
5.2 The Renewable Forecast Deviation 
A solar-heavy state utility schedules 2,000 MW of solar generation for a clear summer 
afternoon in the GDAM. Unexpected cloud cover reduces actual generation to 1,400 MW. 
The utility faces a 600 MW shortfall, triggering deviation settlement under the CERC's 
Deviation Settlement Mechanism (DSM). 
Proposition 2 (information asymmetry) is central: a utility with access to satellite-based 
cloud cover tracking had already reduced its GDAM schedule by 400 MW, limiting its 
deviation penalty. The less-informed utility bears the full cost. This case illustrates why 
investment in forecasting infrastructure is not merely operationally useful but financially 
material — and why regulators should consider whether forecasting quality should factor 
into deviation penalty structures. 
5.3 The Price Spike Event 
In a particularly tight evening peak period, RTM prices spike to Rs 20/unit — 
approximately 6x the prevailing DAM price for the same period. Post-event analysis 
reveals that the spike was not driven by a fundamental shortage: total available capacity 
exceeded demand by a small margin. Rather, the spike was driven by a combination of

conservative bidding by generators anticipating continued tightness (Proposition 3) and the 
absence of large demand-side buyers willing to bid below Rs 20/unit. 
The mandi analogy is vivid: this is the equivalent of a late-evening market where buyers 
have all left and the remaining seller faces a single desperate retail buyer. The "clearing 
price" reflects the bilateral power of that interaction, not the aggregate supply-demand 
balance of the day. Designing mechanisms to prevent such thin-market spikes — through 
minimum liquidity requirements or price caps with backstop supply obligations — is a 
direct policy implication. 
 
6. Implications for Utility Procurement Strategy 
The four propositions and illustrative cases carry direct implications for how utilities — 
particularly distribution companies (DISCOMs) — should approach power procurement in 
an exchange-based environment. 
6.1 Procurement Portfolio Design 
Just as a sophisticated mandi buyer maintains a mix of advance contracts (for staples), spot 
purchases (for day-specific needs), and premium channels (for special requirements), 
utilities benefit from a layered procurement portfolio. Long-term Power Purchase 
Agreements (PPAs) provide cost certainty for baseload. DAM procurement provides 
flexibility for planned demand variation. RTM access should be reserved for genuine 
balancing needs — not treated as a default procurement channel, where behavioral and 
urgency-driven price premiums are highest. 
6.2 Forecasting as a Core Competency 
Proposition 2 implies that forecasting quality is a source of competitive advantage in power 
procurement. Utilities that invest in load forecasting, renewable generation forecasting, and 
real-time market monitoring will systematically outperform those relying on static 
schedules or rule-of-thumb procurement norms. The financial savings from a one 
percentage point improvement in forecast accuracy, translated into reduced RTM exposure 
and DSM penalties, can be substantial at scale.

6.3 Behavioral Awareness in Bidding 
Proposition 3 suggests that utilities should monitor their own bidding behavior for evidence 
of anchoring or herding. If procurement teams systematically overbid in RTM windows 
following a price spike — even when fundamental conditions have normalized — this 
represents avoidable cost. Building structured review processes for post-event bid analysis 
can help identify and correct such behavioral patterns over time. 
6.4 Flexibility Investment as Market Strategy 
Proposition 4 has direct capital allocation implications. The premium commanded by 
flexible assets in volatile markets — whether demand response programs, battery storage, 
or flexible generation capacity — is not merely an operational benefit but a financial return 
on the option value of flexibility. Utilities should evaluate flexibility investments explicitly 
in terms of their expected market participation value, not only their technical grid support 
function. 
 
7. Policy Recommendations 
The analytical framework developed in this paper suggests several directions for regulatory 
and market design improvement in Indian power exchanges. 
7.1 Transparency and Information Disclosure 
Reducing information asymmetry (Proposition 2) requires systematic improvements in 
market transparency. The CERC and IEX should consider publishing real-time and nearreal-time data on renewable generation deviations, grid congestion, and available 
generation capacity in a format accessible to all participants — not merely large, 
sophisticated players. Market transparency is a public good; its provision should not 
depend solely on participants' ability to purchase premium data services. 
7.2 Forecasting Infrastructure Support 
Given the critical role of forecasting quality in market outcomes, there is a case for 
regulatory support for forecasting capacity building among smaller DISCOMs. This might 
take the form of CERC-mandated minimum forecasting standards, shared forecasting

infrastructure at the state level, or technical assistance programs for utilities with limited 
analytical capacity. 
7.3 Thin Market Protections in RTM 
The price spike case (Section 5.3) highlights the risk of thin-market manipulation in the 
RTM. Regulatory mechanisms to address this might include: minimum liquidity 
requirements in each RTM block, backstop supply obligations for generators above a 
certain installed capacity threshold, and ex-post review processes for RTM clearing events 
exceeding a defined price threshold above the concurrent DAM price. 
7.4 Expanding the Tradeable Commodity Set 
The mandi analogy ultimately suggests that a mature market trades not just the primary 
commodity but the services that support reliable commodity delivery — logistics, storage, 
quality assurance, and risk management. India's power market is at an inflection point 
where the tradeable commodity set is expanding beyond energy (MWh) to include ancillary 
services, flexibility products, and potentially AI-driven dispatch optimization services. 
Regulatory frameworks should anticipate and enable this evolution, as it represents the next 
frontier of market efficiency. 
 
8. Conclusion 
This paper has argued that Indian electricity markets — despite their technical 
sophistication, formal exchange mechanisms, and algorithmic clearing — operate 
according to behavioral and structural dynamics that are deeply familiar from informal 
commodity markets. The sabzi mandi is not merely a colorful analogy; it is a structurally 
valid lens through which to understand price discovery, bidding strategy, volatility, and 
efficiency in Indian power exchanges. 
Four propositions — on perishability-induced urgency, information asymmetry, behavioral 
volatility, and flexibility premia — were developed and grounded in the market 
microstructure literature. Illustrative cases demonstrated their practical manifestation. 
Policy and strategy implications were derived for utilities and regulators.

The deeper insight of this paper is captured in its framing: a mandi does not just sell goods. 
It reveals what is scarce. Power markets do not just trade electrons. They price system 
stress. The MCP is not the price of electricity — it is the price of certainty, of reliability, 
of the option to consume power at will in a system where supply and demand must balance 
in real time across millions of simultaneous transactions. 
Formal markets have much to learn from informal ones. The institutional memory 
embedded in the operating practices of experienced mandi traders — about how to read a 
market, time a bid, manage information asymmetry, and price flexibility — is a form of 
market wisdom that formal exchange design has yet to fully incorporate. As India's power 
market continues to mature and expand, drawing on that wisdom may prove as valuable as 
any algorithmic innovation.
