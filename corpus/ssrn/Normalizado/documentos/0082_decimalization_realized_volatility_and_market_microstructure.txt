Abstract
This paper carefully examines the e¤ect of decimalization on volatility and
market microstructure noise. We apply several nonparametric estimators in
order to accurately measure volatility and market microstructure noise variance before and after the …nal stage of decimalization which, on the NYSE,
took place in January, 2001. We …nd that decimalization decreased observed
volatility by decreasing noise variance and, consequently, increased the significance of the true signal especially in trade price data for the highly active
Dow Jones stocks. This study also reveals some di¤erences between volatility and noise variance estimators’ capability to handle changes in tick size and
strategic order placing that are relevant in the evaluation of the decimalization
e¤ects. The ability of the realized kernel estimator to adapt to more complex
data dependency than the traditional realized volatility estimator is useful.
The two-scale and multi-scale realized volatility estimates turn out to be more
variable especially with midquote data where a couple of outlying dates a¤ect
their volatility estimates and consequently the test results in a signi…cant way.

Introduction
The New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) completed its long anticipated change from
fractional pricing to decimal pricing on January 29, 2001. The decimalization process
is accompanied by a reduction in the minimum price variation called the tick size
that in this case is from a sixteenth of a dollar to one cent ($1@16 ¡! $1@100).
This removes constraints on pricing and makes the cost of obtaining price priority
smaller, which should attract greater trading volume and increase liquidity. On the
other hand, the market participants may become more careful in executing their
trading strategies. The e¤ect of decimalization on quoted bid-ask spread, trading
volume, and alike are well documented in the literature. The e¤ect on less easily
observable variables such as volatility are not, however. Although volatility is almost
unanimously reported to decrease [see, e.g., Ronen and Weaver (2001)], most of
the decimalization studies use unprecise estimation methods and sparsely sampled
data [a point stressed also by Hau (2006)]. Market-wide movements due to news
announcements around the decimalization date, such as an interest rate cut, can
also produce misleading results of the true decimalization e¤ects. Finally, the earlier
studies do not decompose volatility in any meaningful way, and thus their volatility
estimates include potentially signi…cant amount of noise [He and Wu (2005) is an
exception]. It is in these respects that we …rstly want to improve over the literature.
Stock market prices live on a grid de…ned by the tick size.
In theory, price
discreteness forces the observed price to deviate from the (unobserved) true price [see,
e.g., Gottlieb and Kalay (1985) and Cho and Frees (1988)]. As a consequence, the
observed volatility is upward biased by amount that depends on the tick size [Harris
(1990a)] and the sampling frequency [e.g., Gottlieb and Kalay (1985)]. Because the
decimalization process is expected to alleviate these rounding errors, the di¤erence
between the true and the observed price should narrow. This, on the other hand,
should decrease the observed volatility but leave the true volatility intact (ceteris
paribus). Some preliminary evidence along these lines already exists [see, e.g., Hansen
and Lunde (2006)] but the main goal of this paper is to analyze it more carefully. It
helps that the NYSE decimalization was done in steps, which allows for a controlled
natural experiment. We complement the standard statistical tests with mixed-e¤ect
panel regressions to ensure that the results are not due to confounding factors. Such

regressions have not been used in the previous decimalization studies.
Another improvement is that we assume the observed volatility to consist of two
components: true volatility and market microstructure noise variance. Otherwise
the volatility estimates would depend on the sampling frequency (through the noise
term) and the estimates would of observed volatility instead of true volatility. The
"signal+noise" type of decomposition is closely related to a class of nonparametric econometric estimators that allow e¢cient extraction of information from ultrahigh-frequency (UHF) data. These estimators can handle irregularly spaced data
and they have impressive statistical properties especially when the data are contaminated by simple forms of market microstructure noise. The two-scale realized
volatility (TSRV) estimator [Zhang, Mykland, and Aït-Sahalia (2005)] is consistent
and (asymptotically) unbiased if the noise component is independent and identically distributed (IID). The multi-scale realized volatility (MSRV) estimator [Zhang
(2006)] weakens the IID requirement. The realized kernel (KERN) estimation approach [Barndor¤-Nielsen et al. (2008)] also allows for some dependence between
true volatility and noise. Similarly, we estimate the noise variance component of
observed volatility with three di¤erent nonparametric estimators.
The design of the analysis makes it possible to compare the relative performance
of volatility and noise variance estimators around the decimalization date. Accuracy
is potentially very important in the evaluation of decimalization e¤ects.
In this
respect, the data type (trade price or midquote) sometimes becomes crucial. For
example, although the popular realized volatility (RV) estimator produces badly
upwards biased estimates with trade price data before decimalization, afterwards its
accuracy is close to the more advanced estimators. The estimates are more robust
with midquote data. These …ndings are important because a change in estimation
accuracy can a¤ect any empirical study that uses data over the decimalization period
(or another tick size change related period). This is not only of statistical interest
but economic as well as both true volatility and noise variance carry economically
relevant information [see Bandi and Russell (2006)].

estimators can be argued not to be jump-robust, we consider the e¤ect of jumps on

large jumps (A 3%)= Whether the analysis is done in event or calendar time is also

We use Trades and Quotes (TAQ) data supplied by the NYSE. The last stage of the
decimalization process was completed on January 29, 2001, which we refer to as the
decimalization date. We analyze two periods of approximately equal length before
and after it: [November 6, 2000 – January 19, 2001] (before decimalization) and

or trade price is a "bounceback" if a (logarithmic) return larger than a prespeci…ed
threshold is followed by a return of similar magnitude but of opposite sign (so that
the two returns approximately sum to zero). We use the following ad hoc threshold
rule based on a careful data inspection: for stocks priced below $10, in between $10
and $50> in between $50 and $100, and larger than $100 we set the threshold to
0=01> 0=0083> 0=0067, and 0=005, respectively. In the before decimalization period, we
further multiply the thresholds by 1=5 for stocks trading in fractions (although this
adjustment turns out to be insigni…cant). There tend to be more bouncebacks in
trade price data, suggesting that these data are inherently more noisy (bouncy) than
the quote data. However, the bouncebacks in the quote data can be "sticky" in the
sense that an error can repeat itself for a while. We detect such errors by comparing
the return and the corresponding spread to the daily standard deviation and spread,
respectively. If the spread increases only temporarily from its daily average and is
followed by a midquote returning to its previous level, a sticky bounceback is detected
and deleted unless it is easy to correct for.11 These procedures detect the errors well.

Conclusions
In this paper, we have carefully examined the e¤ects of decimalization on volatility
and market microstructure noise.
For this purpose, we have used accurate nonparametric UHF estimation methods.
Statistical inference by standard tests are
complemented by mixed-e¤ect panel regressions. The main result is that decimalization decreased observed volatility by reducing noise variance especially for the highly
active stocks. Because the pricing grid got …ner, the evolution of the price process
got smoother. Consequently, the true signal became stronger, especially in the trade
price data at the highest frequencies where the bid-ask bouncing e¤ect is known to
dominate. For the less active stocks, the data type plays a much less signi…cant role.
We would like to point out that smaller tick sizes do not always have to decrease
volatility. As the stock markets have become less transparent and market making
costs have increased as a consequence of the smaller tick size, computerized (algorithmic) trading has gained more popularity. Nowadays, automatized trading makes
a large part of the daily volume. This may be problematic in a crisis period, when
computer algorithms can create unwanted sell-o¤ pressure that ends up increasing,
and not decreasing, volatility. Although we do not study this problem in this paper,
we note that the test results on decimalization are subject to the condition that the
time period must not be abnormally turbulent. Related to this, we do analyze the
e¤ect of certain highly volatile (outlying) days. We …nd that removing such outlying
days does have an impact on the test results with two advanced volatility estimators.
Another, closely related, aim of this paper is to see how well four commonly
used nonparametric volatility estimators perform when the tick size is changed. We
…nd some important di¤erences between the estimators that can have important
consequences for empirical studies covering a period including a tick size change. We
…nd, in particular, that the simple RV estimator is badly upwards biased before the

decimalization date. Its estimates fall much more in line with the more advanced
estimators afterwards. The KERN estimator is the most robust with respect to the
data type and tick size, which is probably because it can account for dependence
between true volatility and noise. Changes in the estimation accuracy are due to
decreased (increased) linear dependence in the trade price (midquote) data, following
from smaller bid-ask bouncing but more active order placing. Potentially related to
order placing, the TSRV and MSRV estimators are more clearly a¤ected by the most
volatile dates (e.g., Jan/03). On the other hand, they are more robust than KERN
in that their trade price data estimates are on average only a few percents higher
than their midquote data estimates irrespectively of the period. The di¤erences in
estimation accuracy call for more research, especially because changes in tick sizes are
expected to be commonplace in future due to the adoption of new market regulations.
We robusti…ed the test results by using di¤erent data types and time concepts and
found the data type to be particularly relevant. The event time results are in line with
the (less accurate) calendar time results. The test results are also studied in relation
to jumps, which we do not …nd to be critical. It appears that pre…ltering data for
obvious data errors, such as "sticky bouncebacks," is far more important. Compared
to the "true" jumps (triggered by news announcements, say), the data errors tend
to be bigger, more frequent, systematic, and harder to correct for especially in the
quote data. If such errors are not excluded, they may end up seriously biasing the
noise variance estimates. The RSRV estimator and its modi…cation are both sensitive
to jumps. The e¤ect of jumps is not the whole story, however, as RSRV remains
upwards biased even after decimalization. The noise variance estimators may also
su¤er from the assumed independence between the di¤erent components of volatility.
In future, it would be useful to decompose volatility into even smaller components
and study their behavior when the tick size is changed. Separating small components
from each other would make the e¤ect of decimalization even more transparent. This
is relevant in practice because frequent changes in tick sizes are expected to happen;
tick sizes are already linked to the price level in many stock exchanges, especially
in Europe. Decomposing market microstucture noise however requires us to decide
what constitutes a jump and how to separate it from other noise sources. This is a
topic of both theoretical and practical interest and likely to become more important
as in…nitely active jump processes gain more popularity in stock price modeling.
