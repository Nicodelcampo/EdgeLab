The effect of fat tails on rules for optimal pairs trading
Abstract
Pairs trading, a popular investing strategy, is often carried out using the meanreverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation as a cornerstone. The random variables
which determine the underlying stochastic process are customarily assumed to
follow a normal distribution, despite the well-known fact that in Finance random
variables are more prone to reach extreme values than Gaussians. In this article we check the reliability of the referred normality assumption, and we analyse
the impact that fat-tailed (L´evy stable, generalized hyperbolic and non-centered
t-student) distributions have on the optimal orders for pairs trading of stocks and
cryptocurrencies. We ﬁnd that the normal distribution suits very poorly to the
spread residuals, being the t-student and hyperbolic distributions more appropriate choices. We also ﬁnd that the optimal trading rules are strongly inﬂuenced by
the distribution chosen to ﬁt the residuals, as are the risk measures (the impact on
the expected performance being moderate). In addition, we investigate the effect
of Poisson events on the optimal trading rules of pairs and single products, which
has a decisive inﬂuence on the stop-loss orders for the former and proﬁt-taking for
the latter. 1
Keywords: Pairs trading, fat-tailed distributions, stocks, cryptocurrencies,
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation, optimal limit orders, non-normality, non-centered
t-student distribution, generalized hyperbolic distribution, L´evy stable
distribution, Poisson events, Monte Carlo simulation.

1. Introduction
The uncertainty that is inherent to nearly all quantities that emerge from human behaviour is still modelled to a great extent using Gaussian (normal) probability density functions. However, ﬁnancial data insists in disproving the normality assumption [1]. In contrast to Gaussians, fat-tailed distributions [2] can usually
give account of extreme events (i.e. outcomes lying relatively far away from the
median) in an accurate manner. Yet the fact that they frequently lack a closed
form, together with other properties (e.g numerical complexity2), has traditionally
precluded its wide usage. However, the powerful presently available computing
capabilities [3], which allow to perform millions of calculations a second with
even cheap (affordable for individuals) computers, make using fat-tailed distributions feasible for many purposes. Moreover, extreme events in Finance are not
uncommon, as we heuristically show in this paper and is otherwise pervasively
observed.
The central topic of this article is therefore to analyse the effects of extreme
price changes (associated with fat tails of probability density functions) on a relevant quantity in investing: optimal trading rules. In this article we focus on pairs
trading. This is a widely used trading strategy which relies on the assumption
that a function of the price of the portfolio is a stationary, mean-reverting process (this is, it must tend to a mean value after having moved away from it). Our
goal is to investigate the effect of the chosen ﬁtting distribution on the optimal
trading rules and on the expected performance and risk of the trading strategies.
Fat-tailed distributions together with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation have already been tackled by other authors in the past. Among them we highlight the
work of [4]. Though their work has an outstanding quality, it was –as any other
research work– subject to limitations. In particular they did not consider remarkable fat-tailed distributions like t-student or L´evy stable, they did not perform a
comparison between the effects of normal and non-normal distributions on trading rules, they considered no Poisson events, stop-loss orders or Sharpe ratios,
and their analysis focused on futures of commodities. This work can therefore be
viewed as an advance in their research line.
This paper is structured as follows. In the next section we present the ﬁnancial products and datasets (time series) used in our analysis. In sec. 3 we
brieﬂy present our calculation methods, including stochastic modelling and trad2Also the frequent inﬁniteness of their variance and high moments, which requires some truncation or dampening post-processing to be ﬁxed.

3. Theory and methods
3.1. Stochastic models
In this section we brieﬂy present the mathematical methods behind the calculations of this paper. Detailed explanations of our methods for pairs trading can
be found in the Supplemental Material.
3.1.1. Pairs trading
Pairs trading is a trading strategy aimed at reducing market risk, where the
investor builds a portfolio consisting in just two ﬁnancial products, being one of
the positions long (i.e. the investor bets that the price of the product will increase)
and being the other position short (i.e. the investor bets that the price will drop).

There exists freedom of choice of the type of products for pairs trading: They can
be stocks, bonds, commodities, futures (e.g. with different maturity), etc. The
pair, however, must be appropriately selected, so that a function of the price of the
portfolio is a stationary, mean-reverting process (this is, it must tend to a mean
value after having moved away from it). Such a process presents a fantastic opportunity for earnings (usually mostly alpha), for an expected evolution of the
prices is known. The fact that the portfolio is long-short widely protects the investor from sudden changes in the market, because the dip in the price of the long
position is expected to be compensated by the short position. The most severe risk
of this method is that the portfolio price ceases to be mean-reverting.
One of the most popular models to give account of mean-reverting processes is
the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation [5, 6, 7]. This equation was originally applied in
the context of Physics; afterwards, it was used by [8] to simulate interest rates, becoming very popular in Finance (that is the reason why sometimes it is called the
Vasicek equation). The continuous time form of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation
can be written as follows:

the spread of a long-short portfolio, i.e. X(t) := s(t)), E0, ϕ and σ are real numbers. Though more sophisticated variations exist (e.g. the Hull-White model [9],
where the parameters are time-dependent), for simplicity’s sake –which is essential for illustrative purposes like the ones of this article– we assume that E0 and ϕ
are constant, and that the probability distribution of σ dW(t) remains unchanged
in certain periods. E0 (long-term mean level of the spread) represents the mean
towards X(t) tends, the mean-reversion parameter (1 −ϕ) –which is positive–
represents the speed of such trend, and σ is the multiplicative factor of the random
variable dW(t); σ is sometimes called the volatility of the process [4]. The latter is
frequently deﬁned as a Brownian motion (i.e. its probability density function is a
Gaussian) [5], though it can also represent fat-tailed random variables [4, 10, 11].
In the latter case X is sometimes called a generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process or a L´evy-driven Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, if dW follows a L´evy stable
distribution.

portfolio formed by two ﬁnancial products (e.g. stocks) which presents the meanreverting property. We deﬁne the spread [12, 4, 13] as:

Building a short position of stocks and cryptocurrencies is frequently not as
straightforward as building a long position. The investor may not ﬁnd anybody
from whom borrowing the shorted product, and if found it would carry additional
transaction costs. Since the aim of this article is to provide an intuitive picture of
the tackled phenomena, it is worthwhile not to make the formalism excessively
complicated. Therefore we will assume that the investor has a very large long
position in both products which form the pair; therefore the investor can build the
short position by merely reducing one of his own long positions. Such trading
strategy, though not market-neutral, is totally meaningful: The large long positions are expected to provide proﬁts in the long term (trend-following) while the
long-short portfolio is expected to provide proﬁts by taking advantage of the oscillations of the prices in a shorter term (trend-reversion) [15].

where σ ε(t −1) are the residuals, being σ their scaling parameter. We will later

squares linear regression of s(t) (i.e. y) vs s(t −1) (i.e. x) provides the E0 and ϕ

As already mentioned, if the analysed data presents the appropriate properties
(e.g. stationarity of the spread, low autocorrelation of residuals) then the selected
pair (products A and B can be traded with proﬁt expectations. To evaluate the
optimal rules for such trading we set a maximum horizon, which is deﬁned as the
maximum number of days that a portfolio is held before selling. We then perform
a Monte Carlo (MC) analysis for different values of the thresholds (trading rules)
which determine the strategy. These are: i) the enter threshold, which determines
the minimum absolute value of the spread to build a position (i.e. to enter the
trade); ii) the proﬁt-taking threshold, which determines the value of the spread to
unwind (ﬁnish) the portfolio having a proﬁt; and iii) the stop-loss threshold, which
determines the value at which the portfolio is unwinded having a loss.

threshold, or when the horizon is reached, we close the position. The proﬁt is then
calculated as p(t) −p(0) = p(t) −1. We repeat this procedure numerous times
(MC iterations). From the average, standard deviation and semideviation of this
collection of proﬁts we calculate the Sharpe ratios, which inform on the optimal
choice of trading rules. The results of our numerical experiments are displayed in
sec. 4.2.
3.1.3. Probability distributions
The spread residuals are σ ε in eq. (5). For the spread of each pair we ﬁt them
to four different distributions: normal, L´evy stable, generalized hyperbolic and
non-centered t-student [2]. All four distributions have a loc and a scale parameters, which give account of the location and scale (size) of the residuals. The
three fat-tailed distributions also have two parameters each to give account of the
skewness and the fatness of the tail (note that these parameters are ﬁnite, though
the skewness and Kurtosis of the distributions can be inﬁnite). The generalized
hyperbolic distributions that we have considered do also have a ﬁfth parameter
(p-parameter) which gives account of the form. We call pd the set of two, four
or ﬁve parameters of the distribution. For all four distributions we use the implementation present in the scipy.stats [16] Python module (functions norm,
levy stable, genhyperbolic and nct).
For the normal distributions, the loc and scale parameters are the mean and
standard deviation of the spread residuals. The former is always zero (by consequence of the deﬁnition of ordinary least squares linear regression), and hence
the only actual parameter of the distribution is the scale. The parameters of the
three fat-tailed distributions are calculated by maximum likelihood, which is the
standard method to this end [17]. Maximum likelihood is also the method used to
obtain parameters of stable distributions by Borak et al., though they warn about
its computational cost (other authors [10] have adviced against the usage of max9

year, i.e. 252 trading days). Each of these synthetic time series is one path of our
Monte Carlo simulations, and corresponds to the evolution of s(t) of eq. (5) or
p(t) of eq. (6b). The time-series of s(t) are generated with up to four different
probability distributions for σε: normal, L´evy stable, generalized hyperbolic and
non-centered t-student. The trading rules consist in four numbers, which determine the thresholds for entering and exiting (unwinding) our positions. The enter
value is the value of the spread s(t) at which we build our long-short portfolio (i.e.
we buy the product A and sell the product B or vice versa). The limit orders are
the proﬁt-taking and stop-loss thresholds [7]. If our position is long in the spread,
i.e. if the enter value is negative because we bet that the spread will increase, then
the proﬁt-taking is a value of the spread which is above the enter value, and the
stop-loss is a value of the spread which is below the enter value. The converse
happens if our position is short in the spread, i.e. if the enter value is positive because we bet that the spread will decrease; in this case the proﬁt-taking is below
the enter value, and the stop-loss is above the enter value. As soon as either the
proﬁt-taking or the stop-loss threshold is reached, we determine that the portfolio
is unwinded, obtaining a proﬁt (if the proﬁt-taking was reached) or a loss (if the
stop-loss was reached). The last trading rule is the maximum horizon, which determines the uppermost time for holding a pair. If after such period holding our
pair we have reached neither the proﬁt-taking nor the stop-loss thresholds, then
the position is anyway unwinded (at a proﬁt or a loss). Therefore, each of the (e.g.
20,000) synthetic paths provides a proﬁt (or loss), which is deﬁned as the difference between the spread when unwinding the position minus the spread when the
position was built (times −1 if we are short in the spread). Hence the proﬁt is
measured in units of spread. If the maximum duration of the strategy is set to a
period different from one year, we annualize it by simply multiplying the proﬁt
by 252 and dividing it by the maximum duration.
When we test each set of parameters corresponding to a given trading strategy
(horizon and enter, proﬁt-taking and stop-loss thresholds) we use the same set of
generated random variables which give account of the evolution of prices. This
is aimed at avoiding statistical error [4, 19]. Otherwise the differences between
the synthetic price time series may lead to distorted results (a given strategy might
bogusly appear as optimal simply because the generated prices used to analyse it
were abnormally favourable).
Note that for simplicity’s sake we assume that at the initial time (t = 0) of
each MC path the spread lies exactly at E0. Therefore it may take a while until
the enter value level is reached (it could even not be reached before the speciﬁed
maximum horizon for a given path).

The generation of synthetic time-series that we employ is a remarkable method
to mitigate the backtest overﬁtting [7]. Making an analysis based on just the observed price time-series would give excessive weight to the realised values of the
random variable, neglecting altogether other values which were equally likely, but
were not realised [20].
Note that we do not consider transaction costs in our analysis, which contrasts
with other research works [4, 13, 21], where high values (e.g. 2% per round-trip
transaction) were applied. The bulk of transaction costs can be classiﬁed into three
main components: fees and commissions, slippage, and market impact [15]. We
assume that the volume of our trading is low enough to make its market impact
negligible; in addition, we consider that the orders are performed fast enough to
avoid signiﬁcant slippage. Regarding costs of interest (for borrowing), in single
product trading we assume that we have enough liquidity to avoid them; in pairs
trading, the long-short nature of our strategy does also justify our approach. As
already mentioned, we omit the costs which are usually associated with short
positions by assuming that we have a previous long position in both products of the
pair. We set other fees and commissions to zero as well, since at the time of writing
this article several popular electronic exchanges (e.g. Fidelity, eToro, Robinhood)
offered very low fees ($0 or $0.02 per trade) in their platforms. Despite the fact
that the results presented in this article do not consider transaction costs, note that
our open source program for doing calculations (August) permits to include them.
In addition, also for simplicity’s sake, we do not consider any discount factors,
as it is sometimes done to take into account the time value of the money or simply
to prioritise early earnings (e.g. in reinforcement learning [22]).
3.3. Performance measures
For each set of trading rules (proﬁt-taking, stop-loss, maximum horizon) our
software calculates eight measures of performance and risk: the Sharpe ratio of the
proﬁts (SR), the rescaled Sharpe ratio calculated from the semi-deviation (SR′),
the standard deviation, the semi-deviation, the proﬁt average, the probability of
loss, the Value-at-Risk and the Expected Shortfall. In this paper we focus on the
ﬁrst two ones to give account of the risk-adjusted returns, and on the last one
for measuring the risk, but it is a matter of taste choosing other criteria. For
example, the hierarchical risk-parity method [23] focuses on standard deviations
of the returns, and other authors prefer to simply use the expected proﬁt to measure
the performance [4].

spread) in Tab. 3. The values of the ﬁtting parameters of this table indicate that
the tails of the distribution are notably fat; for example, the average value of the
number of degrees of freedom of t-student distributions is 3.54 (the heavy-tailed
Cauchy distribution has 1, and the normal has inﬁnite), the average a-param of
the generalized hyperbolic distribution is 0.342, and the average value of the α
parameter of the stable distribution is 1.66 (note that the Cauchy distribution has
α = 1 and the normal distribution has α = 2). Tab. 3 also indicates that the loss
function, which indicates the accuracy of the ﬁtting, is maximal (i.e. worst) for
the normal distribution. The loss function takes similar values for the three fattailed distributions, though among them the stable is always worst. Non-centered
t-student and generalized hyperbolic distributions present nearly equal values of
the loss function; the former is optimal in two of the selected cases, while the latter
is optimal in the other four cases. This feature is intuitive: Since the generalized
hyperbolic distribution function has one more parameter than the non-centered tstudent distribution, it also has more ﬂexibility to adapt to the data. The lowest
value of the loss function appears in bold font in Tab. 3. The values of the loss
function presented in Tab. 3 manifest that t-student and generalized hyperbolic
distributions should in principle be preferred to normal and stable distribution
when ﬁtting spread residuals using the methods described in this article. However,
it may not be fully clear which one between these two distributions should be
preferred. In many cases the difference in their loss functions is low enough to
keep reasonable doubts about it. If the difference between their loss functions
is considered negligible, the non-centered t-student distribution may be preferred
due to its higher numerical efﬁciency in scipy.

4.1.2. Trading rules
Purely mean-reverting. In Figs. 6 to 13 we present heatmaps which inform on the
proﬁtability of the trading strategies for spreads of the six selected test cases; all
they were carried out using 20,000 Monte Carlo paths. Most of them present the
rescaled Sharpe ratio from semideviation (SR′). For brevity’s sake we provide enhanced information just on the oil companies pair (BP-vs-SHEL). The maximum
horizon of all ﬁgures corresponds to one year. The enter values of all Figs. 6 to 13
correspond to a range with absolute values between 0 and 0.04 with grid spacing

of 0.001 (except Fig. 12, where the values are 0.02 and 0.0005, respectively). In

(i.e. the stop-loss parameter is set to ±∞). Despite the fact that the celebrated
book [7] does include them in the presented heatmaps, we deem it not fully appropriate. Our analyses indicate that farther stop-loss parameters lead to better
values of the Sharpe ratios, and thus the optimal trading rules consist in setting
them to −∞(if the bet is long in the spread) or to +∞(if the bet is short in the
spread). A non-trivial choice of the stop-loss parameter emerges when Poisson
events which change E0 arise; we discuss this issue in sec. 4.1.2.
Note that in all the heatmaps we write ($) in the axes to indicate monetary
value though, strictly speaking, the thresholds correspond to units of the spread
(which is adimensional). The dollar sign thus indicates that we have taken one
dollar (or the corresponding currency unit, e.g. e for MBG.DE/BMW.DE) in the
denominators of the arguments of the logarithms (log[pI/1$] of eq. (2)).
In Fig. 6 we present 8 heatmaps of SR′, which correspond to the four analysed distributions with positive enter values (left) and negative enter values (right).
The x and y axes of the heatmaps represent the enter and proﬁt-taking thresholds,
respectively. The asymmetries between the two heatmaps of each row for the fattailed distributions are a consequence of the fact that the distributions are skewed.

the fact that these distributions are more prone than the rest to reach extreme values. This property also manifests in other results, like the relatively large Expected
Shortfalls of the stable distribution presented in Tab. 4. The heatmaps of Fig. 6
indicate that different distributions lead to different values of the optimal trading
rules (clearest pixels in the heatmaps); in addition, they also lead to differences
in the maximum value of the Sharpe ratios (SR′). Therefore, this ﬁgure indicates that the choice of the distribution to ﬁt the spread residuals has non-trivial
consequences on the trading strategy.

Fig. 7 exempliﬁes the effects of time. Its four top subplots correspond to the
case where the duration of the strategy is 5 years (in contrast to the duration of
one year which corresponds to the rest of the heatmaps of this paper). The Sharpe
ratios present a clear increase, and the optimal thresholds are also displaced with
respect to their values from Fig. 6-right. The four bottom subplots of Fig. 7 display the Sharpe ratios for different values of the maximum horizon (the duration
is one year in all cases). Again, a longer survival in time corresponds to higher
values of the SR′. This is intuitive: longer investment periods provide further
earning opportunities, and hence higher proﬁtabilities. The four bottom subplots
also indicate that the different distributions, though calibrated with an identical
dataset, lead to different optimal enter levels. Moreover, they reveal the presence
of local optima. The existence of such local optima led us to present many of the
results of this paper as heatmaps, rather than as unique optimal solutions obtained
using the gradient descent method.

In order to compare the effect of the chosen distribution on the optimal trading
rules we simplify the data from the heatmaps as follows. For every distribution,
for every enter value we ﬁnd the maximum Sharpe ratio (SR from eq. (12)) and

other hand, the optimal enter value can strongly differ for different distributions.

Nevertheless, the fact that the optimal enter value strongly differs as a function
of the chosen distribution can have a non-negligible effect on the likelihood of
reaching a proﬁtable trade in a situation of real trading. The farther the chosen
enter value from the mean E0, the less likely to ﬁnd an opportunity to enter. The
plateau in the Sharpe ratios may make it advisable to choose an enter value whose
SR (or SR′) is not the maximum one, but whose probability of entering a trade is
higher, or any other consideration.
The choice of a fat-tailed distribution does also have an impact on the difference between the optimal enter and proﬁt-taking parameters. For the case of
PEP/KO using SR, for the t-student and the generalized hyperbolic distribution
the enter value equals the proﬁt-taking parameter (0.014 for nct and 0.011 for g.
hyp.); however, for the normal distribution the optimal enter value is 0.014, which
is different from the optimal proﬁt-taking parameter (0.011).
The results of further examples are displayed in Tabs. 5 to 4. In the ﬁrst
three ones (Tabs. 5 to 7) we present the optimal trading rules for all six example
cases, all four distributions, and three optimized quantities, which are the average
proﬁt (µr), the Sharpe ratio (SR) and the rescaled Sharpe ratio from semideviation
(SR′), respectively. All three tables include a column called norm. par. (normal
parameters), which presents the value of the optimized quantity calculated using
fat-tailed distributions to simulate the spread residuals (which is deemed realistic),
yet using the optimal thresholds found when using a normal distribution (which
is a sub-optimal approximation). The difference between this column and the
column at the left of it provides an estimate of the drop in proﬁtability that one
would make by using the normality assumption of residuals. Our results indicate
that the differences are very low in nearly all cases, which is a consequence of the
plateaus mentioned in the paragraph above. The only cases where this difference
is above 10% appear in bold font in the tables; all they correspond to the stable
distribution, which, as discussed, presents sudden changes and whose usage is
therefore not recommended by us. Despite these low differences, and though
Tabs. 5 to 7 display no clear trend on the enter value / proﬁt-taking difference as
a function of the distribution, they quantitatively conﬁrm that the optimal trading
rules do strongly change as a function of the chosen distribution.

4.1.3. Out-of-sample calculations
For the sake of completeness we also present the out-of-sample proﬁts of trading rules calculated using the methods presented in this paper. These proﬁts are
measured in units of currency, in contrast to the results of the rest of the sections, which present the proﬁts measured in units of spread (adimensional). For
each of the analysed spreads corresponding to stocks we set the duration of the
in-sample period for stocks to 5 years. We use these data to obtain the parameters
of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation (including residuals ﬁt using a given distribution type). These obtained data determine the optimal trading rules; we calculate
them maximizing the Sharpe ratio (SR) using 10,000 Monte Carlo paths. We
set the out-of-sample period for stocks to 4 years (except for credit card companies, where we set it to 3 years). In the out-of-sample period we apply the
trading rules, which eventually results into a proﬁt. We recalibrate the OrnsteinUhlenbeck equation and the trading rules after one quarter (i.e. we calculate them
12 times for V/MA and 16 times for the rest of the stocks). Each time, we use

Preprint not peer reviewed
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4595289

the data of the 5 years immediately previous to the ﬁrst date of the out-of-sample
period. For example, we use the data between 2013-11-30 and 2018-11-30 (insample) to calculate the parameters of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation, ﬁt the
spread residuals to a chosen distribution, and determine the optimal trading rules.
We use that trading rules in the period between 2018-12-01 and 2019-11-30. The
trading rules specify when we enter and when we unwind the pair; the proﬁt is
calculated as the difference of the observed prices on both dates (note that if the
spread reaches a proﬁt-taking threshold but unwinding the position results in a
loss, then we do not unwind the position, because that would be contradictory
with the concept of proﬁt-taking). In order to simplify our model, we do not
set a maximum horizon. For cryptocurrencies we set an in-sample period of one
year; the out-of-sample period is 2022-05-01 to 2022-12-20, and we recalibrate
the parameters every month. Our calculations are based on building dollar-neutral
portfolios [4]. This is, we buy (or sell) one unit of the ﬁnancial product A, and
sell (or buy) Γ units of the product B, where Γ = pA(tin)/pB(tin), being pA and pB
the respective prices, and being tin the time (date) when the portfolio is built.
Our results are displayed in Tab. 8, which shows the proﬁt and the average
cost of the long position, as well as the number of entered (created) pairs. They
indicate that the differences in the out-of-sample proﬁts for the four distributions
are often very similar, but non-negligible differences do occur. For example, for
car manufacturers the proﬁt is $20 if ﬁtting the residuals to a normal distribution,
yet it is $27 and $28 (i.e. 34% and 37% above, respectively) if ﬁtting the same
residuals to a non-centered t-student distribution or to a generalized hyperbolic
distribution, respectively. The average absolute value of the difference between
the mean proﬁt obtained if using the normal distribution and its value when using
the fat-tailed distributions is 11%. The number of times that the position is entered
and unwinded does also depend on the chosen distribution (for instance, for car
manufacturers it ranges between 14 and 23).

5. Conclusion
In this article we have analysed the assumption that the residuals of spreads in
pairs trading follow a normal distribution, carrying out our investigation through a
heuristic evaluation of stocks and cryptocurrencies. We have found that fat-tailed

have also analysed the effect of the chosen ﬁtting distribution to trading rules, ﬁnding that the choice has a limited impact on the proﬁtability measures, but a strong
impact on the size of the trading rules themselves and on risk measures. Finally,
we have indicated that ﬁnite stop-loss thresholds arise from considering Poisson
events which give account of the change of the mean in the mean-reverting process, while their absence makes it advisable to ignore stop-loss orders.

S.1) An example of the appearance of fat tails in Finance
In this section we analyse a simple, intuitive model to illustrate how fat-tailed
distributions arise when we avoid too simplistic assumptions.
The Gaussian probability density function is the limit for inﬁnite events (e.g.
coin tosses) of the binomial distribution (where events can have only two outcomes). Assuming Gaussian distributions for random variables can be thought to
be equivalent to assuming that their outcome is the addition of inﬁnite independent binomial random variables with the same possible outcomes. Let us consider
the price p of a stock. We want to calculate the distribution of this price after
one year. Without loss of generality, let us consider that at the beginning (e.g. on
January 1) p = $1. We assume that the variation of the value of p after one year is
the addition of 2000 independent, identically distributed binomial variables. For
simplicity we will assume that their two possible outcomes have equal probability
0.5 (this is equivalent to a sampling of variations each approximately 49 minutes,
if we consider 252 trading days a year and 6.5 trading hours per day). We consider three cases for the value of the outcomes of the independent variables whose
summation determines p:
i) These values are constant, equal to +∆0=$1/2000 and −∆0 = −$1/2000.
ii) They are variable and asymmetrical: this is the two possible outcomes
(with equal probability) are as follows. If p ≥1, then they are −∆0 and
+∆0 · (1 + 6|p −1|); if p < 1 then they are −∆0 · (1 + 6|p −1|) and +∆0.
This is a simple way to model the momentum: If the price of the stock
strongly increased (decreased) after j (with j ≤2000) variations, then the
investor thinks that this trend will continue, and hence he will be willing to
pay more (less) for the stock. Therefore the size of the variation of the price
with strongly increase for high deviations from the initial price (one dollar).
iii) They are variable and symmetric: The two possible outcomes at a given
value of p are ±∆0 · (1 + 12|p −1|). This models the expectations of the
investors that a price which strongly deviated from its initial value can continue its trend or, conversely, come back towards its initial value, because
such large variation could be either over- or undervaluation.

S.2) Procedure for pairs trading
In this section we present all the steps of the pairs trading strategy employed
in our calculations. The calculation methods are based on those presented in [1, 2]
3
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4595289

and [3]. The procedure can be summarized with the steps that follow:
1) Identiﬁcation of the pair.
2) Downloading the time series.
3) Calculation of correlation.
4) Deﬁnition of the spread.
5) Stationarity check.
6) Fitting to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation.
7) Autocorrelation test.
8) Fitting of the residuals.
9) Calculation of the optimal enter level and exit thresholds.
The steps above are aimed at reaching the ninth step, i.e. to calculate optimal thresholds. Alternatively, one can be interested in calculating out-of-sample
proﬁts from observed time-series. To this end, the steps listed above are also used
(and repeated), and one adds a further step in which the proﬁt from observed data
is calculated.
Identiﬁcation of the pair
Generally speaking, the identiﬁcation of the potential pair of products to include
in our long-short portfolio can be carried out by using either quantitative or fundamental information [1]. In the former case, one selects pairs of ﬁnancial products
whose time series (of returns) present high correlations. In the latter case –which
we follow– one analyses the fundamentals of companies (e.g. the kind of product,
industry/sector, geographical region, liquidity, etc.). For example, one can select
candidates for his pairs trading strategy by selecting stocks of integrated petrol
companies with large market cap (e.g. BP, Shell, Eni, TotalEnergies, Chevron,
Conocophillips and ExxonMobil; the Big Oil companies provide up to 21 pairs of
stocks).
Downloading the time series
Once potential candidates are selected, the time series of their prices are obtained from one of the numerous online data sources available, e.g. Bloomberg,

round the prices to the nearest second decimal place (cents). Taking into account
the available data we choose a time spacing for our calculations of returns and updated prices. For simplicity’s sake we take that horizon to be one day. So we avoid
the distorting effect due to the strongly different trading volume at the beginning
and end of the trading sessions. We redeﬁne the prices to take into account the
dividends, which are assumed to be reinvested on the stock on the day they are

Fitting to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation
Here we repeat what is stated in the main paper; we include it here for completeness of the present document. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation [7] is a celebrated short rate equation which gives account of mean-reverting processes. Its
continuous time form can be written as follows:
dX(t) = ( E0(1 −ϕ) −(1 −ϕ) X(t) ) dt + σ dW(t) ,
(5)
where X(t) is a mean-reverting stochastic process (e.g. an interest rate or the
spread of a long-short portfolio), E0, ϕ and σ are real numbers. E0 (long-term
mean level of the spread) represents the mean towards X(t) converges, the meanreversion parameter (1−ϕ) –which is positive– represents the speed of such trend,
and σ is the multiplicative factor of the random variable dW(t) (σ is sometimes
called the volatility of the process [3]). The latter is frequently deﬁned as a Brownian motion (i.e. its probability density function is a Gaussian) [8], though it can
also represent fat-tailed random variables [3]. For simplicity’s sake we assume
that E0, ϕ and σ are constant. In order to obtain them, we perform a discretization of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation as indicated in Refs. [[3, 2]]. We make
dt →∆t := 1 and dX(t) := X(t) −X(t −1), and we rename dW(t) →∆W(t)
as ε(t). Eq. (5) hence becomes:
X(t) = (1 −ϕ)E0 + ϕ X(t −1) + σ ε(t −1) .
(6)
Alternatively one deﬁnes ˜X(t) := X(t)−E0 and the discretized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
equation is expressed as [3, 2]:
˜X(t) = ϕ ˜X(t −1) + σ ε(t −1) .
(7)

obtained by other means and ϕ is obtained as the slope of the linear regression to
eq. (7)). For us the variable X(t) in eq. (6) is s(t) from eq. (3).

Calculation of the optimal enter level and exit thresholds
As stated in the main article, we generate numerous synthetic time-series (Monte
Carlo paths) to evaluate the optimal trading rules avoiding the risk of backtest
overﬁtting.
We analyse numerous values of the enter value and proﬁt-taking
thresholds (in some cases also stop-loss and maximum horizon) and calculate the
average Sharpe ratio of the trades [2]. The analysis of the proﬁt-taking and stoploss pairs of thresholds makes it possible to plot insightful heat-maps which give
account of the relative performance of strategies. Alternatively, we also calculate
the optimal thresholds using the method of gradient descent, rather than calculating outputs for equispaced grids of threshold values. Both calculation methods
(heat-maps and optimal solution) are implemented in the August open source code
that we share. However, as mentioned in the main article, the presence of local
minima of the optimized quantities advises to use heatmaps.
S.3.) Overview of assumptions
In this section we brieﬂy review the main approximations performed, which
were introduced in the corresponding sections of the article.
When using synthetic time series (to calculate optimal strategies and heatmaps)
we consider that the parameters which characterise them are constant (this includes ϕ, E0 in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation (6), as well as the 4 parameters
10
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4595289

which deﬁne a t-student or L´evy stable distribution and the 5 parameters which
deﬁne a generalized hyperbolic distribution). Time-dependent effects like volatility clustering could be easily included through a slight modiﬁcation of our code.
On the other hand, we assume independent variations of the random variable ε(t),
which is supported (at least for linear contributions) by the low autocorrelations
for the analysed time-series (see the Supplemental Material).
The weights of both products forming a pair are always ±1 and ∓γ, which implies that we are assuming that the products are inﬁnitely divisible (as frequently
done in calculations based on replicating portfolios, e.g. in the Black-Scholes
method).
Most of our Monte Carlo calculations consisted in 20,000 paths. A brief discussion on the convergence can be found.
We consider that at t = 0 of each Monte Carlo path the spread is E0. This is
useful for illustrative purposes (it avoids complicating the model), but may not be
optimal for actual proﬁtability calculations if the spread one wants to trade on a
given date lies far away from its E0. In such cases one should perform proﬁtability
calculations simply setting s(0) to the observed value of the spread on that date.
We do not consider transaction costs in our analysis.
We do not consider any discount factors.
S.4.) Fitting to probability density functions
As indicated in the main article, one of the main goals of this research work
is to analyse the probability distributions of the residuals. To this end we ﬁt them
(i.e. σ ε from eq. (7)) to selected representative distributions. Our Monte Carlo
simulations to analyse the performance of trading rules assume independent values of the residuals for different times. This way to proceed is supported by the
analysis of the autocorrelations of the residuals, which are displayed in Fig. 3 for
six example cases.
In Tabs. 1 , 2 we present the ﬁtting parameters of the two sets of spreads that
we have analysed: those corresponding to oil companies and to US banks. The
bold font indicates lowest (i.e. optimal) loss function. From the data of Tab. 1 we
notice that the non-centered t-student distribution (nct) provides the best ﬁtting in
16 out of 38 study cases (42%), the generalized hyperbolic (g. hyp.) distribution
provides the best ﬁtting in 21 cases (55%), and the stable is the best suited in only
one case (3%). This supports the conclusions that we presented in the main article
(based on 6 examples): the best-suited distributions are nct and g. hyp, depending

S.5.) Results of pairs trading
S.5.1.) Convergence
In Fig. 13 we present the value of three of the analysed quantities as a function of the number of Monte Carlo paths considered in the calculations. These
quantities are the average of the proﬁt (measured in units of spread), the semideviation of the proﬁt, and the rescaled Sharpe ratio from semideviation (i.e. SR′,
the quotient between the other two quantities divided by
√
2). Each curve corresponds to a chosen set of enter value and proﬁt-taking parameters. The example
case corresponds to the spread of BP vs Shell ﬁtted to a non-centered t-student
distribution (this is, its conclusions might not be perfectly suitable for e.g. stable
distributions). The displayed curves indicate that the differences between the values obtained with 20,000 paths are very similar to those obtained with 3 millions
of paths (the average proﬁts differ in 0.2% and the Sharpe ratios differ in 0.05%).
Moreover, they also indicate that the optimal input –enter value and proﬁt-taking
parameters– remain the same for different values of the number of paths. Therefore, the calculations performed using 20,000 Monte Carlo paths can be considered reliable.
