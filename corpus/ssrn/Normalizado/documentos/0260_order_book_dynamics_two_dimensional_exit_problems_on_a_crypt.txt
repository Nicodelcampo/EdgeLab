Abstract
This thesis reports on the applicability of drift-diﬀusion and diﬀusion-only models to estimate future
price directions from Level-1 limit order book data. We ﬁt and evaluate the probability models on a
data set of 78M order book updates (1 month) of the Bitcoin −U.S. Dollar market on BitMEX, a
large cryptocurrency exchange.
We show that diﬀusion-only models outperform drift-diﬀusion models, which is likely a result of the
limited Level-1 description of the order book. Both types of models oﬀer signiﬁcant improvements
over baseline models that do not incorporate order book information. On selected evaluation points
leading up to price changes, the best-performing model obtains an RMS direction prediction error of
9.18 −30.52%. A model-based price direction classiﬁer obtains an average classiﬁcation precision on
the same points in the range of 93.83 −99.64%.
We ﬁnally demonstrate eﬀectiveness of the classiﬁer in a simple trading strategy, resulting in an average
return of 0.062% per trade and win rate of 69.12% over 38K trades. A realistic implementation of the
strategy will require further analysis in price move sizes and/or limit order execution.
Keywords: Drift-diﬀusion, cryptocurrency, exit problems, high-frequency trading, limit order books,
market microstructure, option valuation.

Chapter 1
Introduction
At the heart of modern trading exchanges is an electronic matching system to track and execute
orders given by market participants. This system continuously updates a list of open orders, which
express buying or selling interest, with their associated price level and order volume. This double
list is generally called the Limit Order Book (LOB), and the process of executing these orders the
Continuous Double Auction (CDA). As the market participants send their instructions, liquidity is
either created by accumulation of open interest (limit order), or removed through a trade (market
order) or a cancellation of a limit order.
This interplay of existing and arriving orders results in the price discovery of the asset trading on the
exchange. The arrivals of orders and cancellations can therefore be viewed as the microscopic process
driving the macroscopic price development and is hence of major interest to a multitude of parties.
Market participants wish to inform themselves of the behaviour of other participants and the potential
impact of a large-volume trade. Market regulators want to monitor trading behaviour and evaluate
the performance of the order matching system. Scientiﬁc and quantitative researchers may use the
LOB to develop new dynamical models or to back-test trading strategies.
Arguing that the main order ﬂow is directed at the best bid and ask prices, Cont and De Larrard
(2013) proposed a reduced-form model, which takes into account only the volume at these two levels.
Inspired by Cont, Stoikov, and Talreja (2010), Avellaneda, Reed, and Stoikov (2011) deﬁne a similarly
reduced, but symmetrical model which includes the negative correlation of the best bid and ask queue
sizes, and derive the price increase probability.

Introduction
It is known from Biais, Hillion, and Spatt (1995) that limit order arrivals show clustering behaviour.
It is therefore a serious limitation of both the Cont-Stoikov-Talreja (CST) model and the AvellanedaReed-Stoikov (ARS) model that the arrival rates are modeled as constants.
In this thesis, we investigate whether the analytical predictions of the price process, provided by the
aforementioned CST and ARS models, can be improved by allowing the Poisson process intensities to
vary between arrival events. Additionally, we introduce and compare two new models for predictions of
the price increase probability, based on convection-diﬀusion results of López and Sinusía (2004). These
correspond to heavy-traﬃc approximations of more general imbalanced (drifting) arrival processes with
and without correlation.
The application of these reduced-form models on cryptocurrency markets is motivated by ﬁndings of
Schnaubelt, Rende, and Krauss (2019), who investigated the Bitcoin (XBT) −United States Dollar
(USD) market. At the time of writing, Bitcoin is the world’s largest cryptocurrency by market capacity.
The authors point out that average order book volume has a global maximum at the best bid and
ask levels, indicating a high information content at the ﬁrst LOB level. Secondly, real-time LOB data
is readily available for such markets for free, facilitating the ready application of our models. The
LOB under investigation in this thesis is a three-month-long data set of the XBT −USD market on
BitMEX, the largest cryptocurrency exchange by reported trading volume (CoinMarketCap, 2019).
In a rolling window approach, we estimate the order ﬂow parameters on the bid and ask sides of
the LOB using exponentially ﬁltered cumulative order ﬂow processes.
We assume that the ﬁtted
parameters at a speciﬁc point in time remain constant until the next price move. This way, the available
analytical results for computing transition probabilities still apply. We investigate the eﬀectiveness of
the rolling window method by varying the weighting parameter of both the exponential and rolling
window ﬁlters.
For the chosen set of evaluation points, all order ﬂow models show signiﬁcant improvement over baseline
models that do not take the order ﬂow into account. This indicates that the incorporation of Level-1
order volume information can provide a superior estimate of future price direction. The inclusion of
drift eﬀects does not provide better direction estimates, which is likely a result of the limited Level-1
data set. Inclusion of correlation, and parameter estimation from short rolling windows do not improve
prediction performance either.
When the models are used as binary threshold classiﬁers, we observe that the time-averaged models
generate (nearly) identical classiﬁcations. Classiﬁer precision on the evaluation points ranges between
87.69 −99.00%. If we exclude certain order volume combinations for prediction, classiﬁer precision of
the best-performing model can be increased to 93.83 −99.64%, at the cost of reduced recall.
Finally, we show the eﬀectiveness of the classiﬁer for generating trading signals. Over the course of 1

Chapter 2
Literature Review
Limit Order Book (LOB) research has been inﬂuenced by many ﬁelds of study, and there is no general
agreement on the best approach (Gould et al., 2013). Overall, there are two signiﬁcantly diﬀerent
main modeling paradigms, which both rest on strong assumptions on trader behaviour.
From classical economic literature and psychology, traders are assumed to act strategically, participating in the market according to personal utility functions and optimization procedures. Assumptions
on the rationality of traders have however been contested by Gode and Sunder (1993), who show in
a Continuous Double Auction (CDA) simulation experiment that even highly uninformed traders can
perform well and generate a high market eﬃciency. This indicates that aggregated high-level properties
of markets can be reproduced with only a minimal set of assumptions on trader behaviour.
From physics models, order ﬂows are treated as stochastic counting processes, drawing many analogies
with processes encountered in statistical mechanics such as particle motion and heat diﬀusion. These
so-called zero-intelligence models further seek to describe the order ﬂows in terms of transition rate
parameters and order size distribution. The advantage of these models is that they are based on directly
observable signals and often lead to quantiﬁable predictions without resorting to computationally
expensive simulation methods (Gould et al., 2013). Especially for traders, preference may be given to
zero-intelligence models to obtain predictions in real-time.
Early research in LOB dynamics focused on empirical analyses of data, reporting on qualitative properties of the observed order ﬂow. Biais et al. (1995) cite properties such as serial cross-correlation
of arrival intensities, undercutting behaviour and clustering in time on the Paris Bourse. Another

empirical study on the Paris Bourse by Bouchaud, Mézard, and Potters (2002) reports that prices of
arriving limit orders follow a power-law distribution around the current price.
In an attempt to reproduce key features of the LOB in an analytically tractable model, Smith, Farmer,
Gillemot, and Krishnamurthy (2003) propose a parsimonious zero-intelligence model based on only
ﬁve measurable scalar parameters. Limit orders, market orders and cancellations all arrive as Poisson
processes with constant rates on a price grid with ﬁxed tick size. They comment on the apparent longterm balance of market orders and limit orders, and draw an analogy with evaporation-deposition
processes.
Cont et al. (2010) move the problem of LOB modeling into the domain of Markov chains −stochastic
models of event sequences and transition probabilities that only depend on the current state of the
system. Rate parameters that describe independent Poissonian order ﬂows are obtained directly from
LOB data and are modeled according to the power-law described in Bouchaud et al. (2002). Applying
results from queuing theory, they show that several conditional probabilities of special interest to
traders, such as the predicted probability of an upward price move, the probability of executing an
order before the mid-price moves and the probability of ‘making’ the spread can be computed using
Laplace transform methods.
They also show that the mid-price process converges to a Brownian
motion with volatility depending on the tick size and mean queue size. The prediction of price moves
from order book queues is closely related to the study of exit problems, which also arise in the ﬁelds
of barrier option pricing and population dynamics (Grasman & Van Herwaarden, 1999).
In Cont and De Larrard (2013), the model is reduced to the evolution of the volume at the best bid and
best ask queues. The authors explain that the motivation is twofold: better availability of data, and
the fact that the main order ﬂow is often directed at the best bid and ask prices. In this reduced form,
they obtain conditional price increase probabilities that can be computed directly, both in a balanced
order book, where the arrival intensities of the market orders and cancellations are equal to the arrival
intensity of limit orders, and in an unbalanced order book. However, as Zhang, Christensen, Li, and
Godsill (2016) point out, this last result has been derived incorrectly, owing to a non-convergent term
in Van Leeuwaarden and Raschel (2013). Using characteristic functions, they present a new integral
for the probability of a mid-price increase in an imbalance case.
The Cont-Stoikov-Talreja (CST) model has inspired many more studies into LOB modeling. Andoh
(2017) applies the model on the order book of AstraZeneca PLC and shows that the Laplace transform methods agree with simulation results obtained from parameters ﬁtted to the data set. Abergel
and Jedidi (2013) further investigate stability of the order book with constant arrival intensities, and
prove, under certain assumptions, that it is ergodic. Avellaneda et al. (2011) consider a symmetrical order book model but add extra transition probabilities corresponding to correlation in the bid

and ask volumes. Furthermore, they shift the domain of the probability function to match with the
empirical probabilities. For this, they introduce an implied volume parameter, which accounts for
hidden liquidity. The so-called hidden liquidity represents the existence of (partially) hidden orders or
patient traders who choose to only reveal their preferences once the queue almost depletes. The suggestion of a potential liquidity supply outside the observable order book data has also been suggested
by Biais et al. (1995). Drawing on results from diﬀusion theory, the stochastic Partial Diﬀerential
Equation (PDE) of the Avellaneda-Reed-Stoikov (ARS) model has an explicit solution which allows
for quick computation of the transition probabilities.
The stochastic PDE of the ARS model can be extended to a stationary version of the two-asset BlackScholes equation (In ’t Hout & Valkov, 2015). In this more general form, bid and ask queue sizes
also exhibit constant drift. The solution to this equation, with suitable boundary conditions, has been
approximated by López and Sinusía (2005) in the context of convection-diﬀusion problems. Although
the authors derive full asymptotic expansions, an intermediate integral result can still be computed
quickly.
Another extension is a generalization by Huang and Kercheval (2012), allowing limit orders to arrive in multiple sizes, but keeping market orders and cancellations at unit size.
Their result is a
computationally costly integral, which is not very suitable for high-frequency prediction.
The empirically observed clustering of order arrivals is problematic for the CST and ARS models, which
assume constant arrival rates. Chávez-Casillas, Elliott, Rémillard, and Swishchuk (2017) introduce a
time-changed arrival process, but this does not inﬂuence the associated (and incorrectly derived) price
increase probability from Cont and De Larrard (2013). Swishchuk and Vadori (2017) generalize the
CST model to arbitrary interarrival time distributions and add extra state parameters to account for
serial correlation (so-called semi-Markovian modelling). The resulting calculation of a price increase
probability requires computation of a double improper integral.
Gorshenin et al. (2013) propose a multiplicative model employing doubly stochastic Poisson arrival
processes (Cox processes), so that the intensities of the Poisson processes themselves become stochastic
as well. This multiplicative property, which can be likened to the time changed process of ChávezCasillas et al. (2017), gives rise to an analysis of the imbalance process −the instantaneous ratio
between buying and selling pressure. We further investigate this process in the so-called Order Flow
Imbalance (OFI) process in Chertok, Korolev, and Korchagin (2016). As price changes are a relatively
rare occasion in the microscopic time intervals of an order book, the authors argue that the OFI
process can provide more information for high-frequency traders and show that it closely tracks price
changes over short time intervals.

Data collection and description
The BitMEX platform allows subscribing to real-time trading data through a Websocket Application
Programming Interface (API). Full Level 2 order book data, which describes limit order volume at all
price levels, is available for free without the need for authentication. The messaging protocol is not
rate-limited and ensures that, once subscribed to a data channel, no messages are lost. Messages are
time stamped according to server time.
Over the course of 81 days, a dedicated data logger registered over 245M order book updates concerning
the ﬁrst three levels of the book. During this period, downtime totaled 67 minutes, of which 42 can
be attributed to intermediate data export. Unscheduled downtime therefore amounts to 0.0216% of
the total time span. These statistics are summarized in Table 3-1.

Chapter 4
Methodology
In this chapter, we ﬁrst place the Limit Order Book (LOB) dynamics in a mathematical framework,
in which we identify the relevant parameters for describing the order arrivals (Section 4-1). From the
bid and ask queue data, we deﬁne cumulative order ﬂow processes in Section 4-2. The methods used
to obtain model parameters from the order ﬂow processes are described in Section 4-3. Finally, the
performance evaluation methods are treated in Section 4-4.
4-1
Mathematical framework
As motivated by the literature research and the initial data analysis, we study the evolution of the
LOB through the volume at its best bid and ask levels. We follow the notation of Avellaneda et al.
(2011) to describe the volume at the bid (ask) price at time t by the stochastic process Xt (Yt). The
combined process (Xt, Yt) forms a continuous-time Markov chain, which eﬀectively moves through the
ﬁrst quadrant with a step size h −the minimum order size −on hN × hN.

diagonal transitions incorporate correlation between order arrivals, but can in reality only occur after
two consecutive orders. The authors argue that such distinctions are not essential on a macroscopic
level, when ﬁtting the parameters to real-world data.

In an attempt to remove the positive drift, we propose a simple ﬁlter. From every point of the process
(Bt, At) the Exponential Moving Average (EMA), calculated with weighting factor α, is subtracted.
This corresponds to the investigation of a local (relative) evolution of the process, and exhibits the
desired mean-reversing behaviour. Furthermore, the impact of large price jumps is slowly ‘forgotten’.
The ﬁlter for calculating the exponentially weighted average is given by
st(xt; α) = αxt + (1 −α)st−1,
where the ﬁlter is initialized using s−1 = 0. We select exponential weighting parameters from α =
{1, 2, 5}·10NE where NE ∈{−2, −3, −4}. Consequently, we calculate model parameters µX, µY , σX, σY
and ρ using simple moving windows, where we select window sizes from WS = {1, 2, 5}·10NW messages
where NW ∈{3, 4, 5, 6}.

4-4
Performance Evaluation
To calculate the prediction performance of the models, we determine a range of evaluation points.
Determination of evaluation points is non-trivial, as we do not know in advance when the price will
change.
A simple selection would be prediction in regular time or message intervals.
Such naive
selection may however result in multiple predictions for the same price change, or none at all, making
it hard to compare model performance per price change. Here, we make an ex-post selection of price
changes and investigate the evolution of the prediction up until a price change.
Given that the models are only valid when the spread is minimal, we collect all points with minimal
spread and calculate the time t until the price changes. We select evaluation points up until price
changes that took at least 5000 ms to change. For all times t = {1, 2, 5} · 10NT ms with NT ∈{1, 2, 3}
up until the next price change, we evaluate the probability model. The selection may introduce a
slight directional bias in price direction, but the bias is equal among prediction points2.
For all evaluation points, we calculate the model probabilities using the time-averaged and rolling
window parameters µX, µY , σX, σY , ρ. We then compare the probabilities with the ‘observed probability’ −100% in case the next price move is positive, and 0% for a negative move. We calculate
performance according to the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) statistic. We compare RMSE with
three baseline models that are independent of the order ﬂow: ‘Always Up/Always Down’ predictors
and the ‘Previous Direction’ predictor. The latter predicts up if the previous price direction was up,
and predicts down if the previous price direction was down.
We evaluate performance diﬀerences assuming that the error distribution can be approximated by an
unbiased normal distribution. Technically, the error distribution has ﬁnite support, and results by
Marchand (1996) on truncated and non-central chi-squared distributions may be more appropriate.
These results however rely on recursive computation over the degrees of freedom and no convergence
proofs are given for large n. Use of a regular, non-truncated normal distribution likely overestimates
the conﬁdence bounds. This decreases the power of the test, but avoids false rejection of the null
hypothesis. For n = 74746 observations, we consider relative RMSE diﬀerences of larger than 0.51%
signiﬁcant on a 95% conﬁdence level (see Appendix A-1).

6-3-2
Rolling Parameters
For all evaluation points, we calculate the model probabilities from the windowed parameters µX, µY ,
σX, σY , ρ using rolling windows of sizes WS = {1, 2, 5}·10{3,4,5,6}. We evaluate performance using the
same evaluation points and signiﬁcance thresholds of the averaged-parameter models of the previous
section.
The results are included in Tables B-2 and B-3 of Appendix B. We see that Models 1 and 2 do not
beneﬁt from a rolling window approach −we observe strictly increasing prediction performances for
the three sets of evaluation points (t = 100, 10, 1 ms) as window sizes are increased to a maximum of
5M messages. It does however prove that the use of past order ﬂow provides good parameters, as the
‘full window’ parameters, i.e. the averaged parameters of the previous section, technically incorporate
future information.
Performance diﬀerences within models per horizon t are already statistically
indistinguishable for WS ≥106 messages.
We observe similar performance convergence towards the full-window versions for Models 3 and 4,
which indicates that rolling windows do not improve the predictions when drift parameters are included.
Summarizing, we see that a local analysis of the order ﬂow through windowed parameter estimation
does not improve prediction performance. Possible explanations are that the windowed parameter
estimates are too noisy and lead to aggressive prediction behaviour. Alternatively, the prediction may
suﬀer from estimation delays. For example, assuming there exist ‘true’ instantaneous parameters,
estimation using past windows is inevitably lagged.

Trading Strategy
We propose a simple additive trading strategy using predictions of the ‘Up-Down-No Prediction’
classiﬁer to enter and exit the market. We use the most selective version of the classiﬁer (largest
region S, 20th percentile). We initiate a long (short) position of +1 XBT (−1 XBT) at 5000 ms after
a price change if the spread is minimal and the predicted direction is up (down). This delay gives the
bid and ask volumes a chance to stabilize and reﬂect market beliefs. We close a long (short) position
once the best bid (ask) price moves down (up).
The procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1 in Appendix C. Here, tL represents the last time the bid
or ask price changed, ti the current time, P the current market position (1 is long, −1 is short), and
all other symbols are deﬁned as before. From September 9th to October 13th, the algorithm opens
and closes 19188 positions. Average position duration is a little over 2 minutes. These statistics are
summarized in Table 6-4.

If we ignore transaction costs, the average return per trade is 5.547 USD, with a standard deviation of

continues, the return distribution is heavily skewed to the right. The distribution is also leptokurtic: it
produces more outliers than the normal distribution. Both correctly followed long trends and wrongly
estimated large price changes in the opposite direction may have caused this result.
In reality, transaction costs however not negligible. The BitMEX platform charges a commission of
0.075% per market order (MO), and pays a rebate of 0.025% per limit order (LO). This structure is
referred to as maker-taker, and is designed to reward market making.
If we would execute both the opening and closing of the trading position as market orders, the paid
commission more than oﬀsets the net proﬁt, resulting in a net loss. On average, every trade loses 7.911
USD (0.088%). If we can carry out the position close using a limit order, the average return is still
positive: 1.061 USD (0.012%). Execution of limit orders is however not guaranteed, as the price might
move away before the order is ﬁlled. Placement of limit orders to optimize the trading strategy, or an
investigation into market making strategies on the exchange, are interesting points of future research.

Chapter 7
Conclusion and Discussion
For the chosen set of evaluation points, all order ﬂow models show signiﬁcant improvement in price
direction prediction over baseline models that do not take order ﬂow into account.
Whereas the
best performing baseline model (‘Previous Direction’) obtains an RMSE of 53.80%, the best order
ﬂow model generates RMSEs in the range of 9.18 −30.52%, depending on the time until a given price
change. This indicates that the incorporation of Level 1 order volume information can provide superior
estimates of price direction on the BitMEX exchange.
The extension of time-averaged diﬀusion-only Models 1 and 2 to include drift leads to a deterioration
of the RMSE, which may be attributed to positive bias in the order ﬂow data. The ﬁltered order
ﬂow namely still exhibits a positive bias, which has an averaging eﬀect on the probability surface.
This averaged positive drift is a result of the choice to incorporate only Level 1 information. From
the one-level order book description, loss of ﬂow information during large trades that deplete multiple
price levels is virtually guaranteed. The associated non-monitored order book queues likely had a
non-zero queue size and we underestimate the exact trade size. Possible solutions include tracking of
multiple order book levels, or data fusion using reported trades.
Realistically, order ﬂows are in equilibrium over long time scales, and only temporarily exhibit drift.
On average, µX and µY should therefore equal 0. For bid and ask queue volumes that have stabilized
after a price change, we can also ﬁx µX and µY as equal and negative. This is motivated by the
observation that bid and ask queue dynamics exhibit multiple phases: they often ﬁll rapidly after
a price change, by agents trying to gain time priority, then stabilize, and eventually always deplete

approach does appear to improve the performances of Models 3 and 4 over the diﬀusion-only models,

Another possible improvement with respect to the data is the execution of a second, identical data

The ﬁltered order ﬂow process clearly exhibits negative correlation. However, inclusion of negative

conservative, which increases the RMSE.
An attempt to make the models more dynamic, by estimating the order ﬂow parameters through

suﬀers from noisy estimates or estimation delays.
When the models are used as binary threshold classiﬁers, we observe that the time-averaged models
generate (nearly) identical classiﬁcations. This is a result of the symmetry of the averaged parameters
and selection of the decision boundary. Classiﬁer precision on the evaluation points ranges between
87.69−99.00%. If we exclude certain order volume combinations from prediction, classiﬁer precision of
the best-performing model can be increased to 93.83−99.64%, at the cost of reduced recall. Alternative
classiﬁer thresholds may be employed, such as limits on the predicted probability instead of volume.
Finally, we showed the eﬀectiveness of the classiﬁer for generating trading signals. Over the course of
1 month, a trading algorithm executed 38376 trades. In the absence of transaction costs, an average
return of 5.547 USD (approximately 0.062%) per market entry and exit was obtained, trading only
ﬁxed sizes of ±1 XBT. If transaction costs for market orders are included, the paid commission is
shown to more than oﬀset the obtained proﬁts. When we assume the trade exits can be carried out
using limit orders, a trader can earn commission rebates and still make proﬁts (1.061 USD, 0.012%,
per entry and exit). Execution of limit orders is however not guaranteed, as the price might move
away before the order is ﬁlled.
Future research may be directed at the execution risk of limit orders, or prediction probability of
larger price moves by including deeper order book levels. Accurate prediction of larger price moves
may compensate the transaction costs associated with market orders.
Mathematically, there is still some work related to the limiting probability functions of Models 3 and 4
in the extreme case that correlations are perfectly positive or negative. Also, more results are desired
on the convergence of the (non-central) truncated chi-squared distribution to improve on conﬁdence
bounds of RMS errors for predictions of a binary variable.

The obtained probability functions can also ﬁnd application in the pricing of rainbow options, which
are options that are exposed to multiple (correlated) sources of uncertainty. Speciﬁcally, two-asset
cash-or-nothing options without expiry date, which are knocked in according to a barrier on the ﬁrst
asset price, and knocked out according to another barrier on the second, appear to be directly priceable
by the probability models collected in this thesis. To the best knowledge of the author, these options
have not been priced analytically before.
Analytical extension of the exit problem to include a third asset or dimension (such as time) is possible
through results of López, Sinusía, and Temme (2007). This way, option expiry may be introduced via
‘exit through a maturity date boundary’. In the case of assets, this requires further mathematical
transformations to remove correlation-associated cross-terms and will likely result in a 3D sector
problem domain.
