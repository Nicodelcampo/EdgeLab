Hydrodynamic limit of order book dynamics∗
Xuefeng Gao ∗∗, J. G. Dai †, A. B. Dieker ‡, and S. J. Deng §
November 20, 2014
Abstract
Motivated by optimal trade execution, this paper studies the temporal evolution of
the shape of a limit order book over a time horizon that is large compared with the
length of time between order book events, with the aim of approximating the transient distribution of the shape. Relying on the stochastic order book model in Cont
et al. (2010), we show that when the tick size approaches zero, a pair of measure-valued
processes representing the “sell-side shape” and “buy-side shape” of an order book converges to a pair of deterministic measure-valued processes in a certain sense. Moreover,
we describe the density proﬁle of the limiting processes through ordinary diﬀerential
equations which can be solved explicitly. We also perform experiments to test our
limiting model against data. The empirical results suggest that the approximation is
eﬀective.

Introduction
As a trading mechanism, limit order books have gained growing popularity in equity and
derivative markets in the past two decades. Nowadays, the majority of the world’s ﬁnancial
markets, such as Electronic Communication Networks in the United States, the Hong Kong
Stock Exchange and the Toronto Stock Exchange, are organized as electronic limit-order
books to match buyers and sellers. This has inspired intense research activities on limit
order books. See, e.g., Parlour and Seppi (2008); Gould et al. (2013) for reviews.
Our work is motivated by a body of literature on optimal trade execution. The task of
optimal trade execution is to slice a large institutional size market order into a sequence of
smaller trades so as to improve execution quality. The typical time horizon to fully execute
such a large market order ranges from seconds to hours for liquidly traded assets and hours
to days for illiquid ones. Such an execution horizon is relatively large compared with the
time length between order book events, which is around milliseconds in high-frequency
trading. The key factor to model in the optimal trade execution problem is price impact,
i.e., how large trades aﬀect prices. Closely related with price impact is the shape of the
order book that describes the number of awaiting limit orders at each price level. On the
one hand, the shape of an order book can be used to derive the so-called virtual price
impact (Weber and Rosenow (2005)). On the other hand, a body of literature on optimal
trade execution takes the shape of the order book as input to determine price impact and
derive optimal trading strategies, see for instance Obizhaeva and Wang (2012); Alfonsi
et al. (2010); Predoiu et al. (2011); Fruth et al. (2013); Chen et al. (2013); Tsoukalas et al.
(2013). Therefore, it is desirable to have an endogenous description of the evolution of
the shape of a limit order book on “large” time scales where an institutional size order is
executed. This is the goal of our paper.
In order to characterize the evolution of the shape of the order book on a large time
scale (low-frequency), we rely on microscopic order book models which describe the shortterm behavior of order book events (high-frequency). In doing so, we connect order book
dynamics on diﬀerent time scales. There have been quite a few microscopic dynamical
models of limit order books, see, e.g., Bak et al. (1997); Smith et al. (2003); Cont et al.
(2010); Abergel and Jedidi (2011). Our work is based on Cont et al. (2010), where order
book dynamics are described by a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC). We pick this
model mainly because the price as well as the shape in the model are both determined
within the limit order book system based on order ﬂows. This is how a limit order book
operates and how price moves, so such endogenous nature of price and shape dynamics is
critical in order book modelling (see, e.g., Cont et al. (2013), Domowitz (1996)). In this
model, there are a ﬁnite number of security price levels and the state of the order book is
described by an order quantity at each price. Using Laplace transform analysis, Cont et al.
(2010) eﬃciently compute conditional probabilities of various events of interest. However,
it is computationally challenging to perform transient analysis (compute the transient
distribution) of this multi-dimensional CTMC, in part due to its large state space and the
2

coupling between the order ﬂows of the buy-side and sell-side. While simulation is one
viable way (potentially also costly) for understanding the transient behavior of the shape
or the CTMC, we rely on the theory of hydrodynamic limits (see, e.g., Kipnis and Landim
(1999)) to derive analytical approximations of the shape which could serve as input for
optimal trade execution problems.
1.1
Contributions
In this paper, we develop a deterministic dynamical model to approximate the highdimensional CTMC model of order book dynamics in Cont et al. (2010) by establishing a
type of law of large numbers limit theorem. We do it by considering a time scale that is
large compared to the length of time between order book events and a regime where the
price tick size is very small. This regime is relevant, for example, for U.S. stock markets
where the stock price tick size is only one penny. Such a regime has also been adopted in
studies of order book models by researchers such as Smith et al. (2003). In this scaling
regime, we develop a ﬂuid approximation for the transient behavior of the shape of a limit
order book based on the interaction of order ﬂows.
Mathematically, we prove in Theorem 3.1 that as the tick size 1/n approaches zero, a
sequence of pairs of measure-valued processes {(ζn,+, ζn,−) : n ≥1}, representing the “sellside shape” and “buy-side shape” of the order book, converges to a pair of deterministic
measure-valued processes in a certain sense. Moreover, the density proﬁle of the limiting
processes can be described by Ordinary Diﬀerential Equations (ODEs) with coeﬃcients
determined by order ﬂow intensities. Such a ﬂuid approximation is possible mainly because
(a) we assume that there are large volumes of limit orders sitting at or near the best
quotes and waiting for market transactions, hence the impact of an individual order is very
small; (b) we assume that the magnitude of market order arrival rate (e.g., volume per
second) is not very high compared to the volumes of limit orders in the vicinity of market
price, speciﬁcally during the time periods of low price volatility. Both assumptions appear
reasonable for liquidly traded stocks in our data; (c) the duration of order book events is
short, so on the large time scale that we consider, the randomness in the cumulative order
volumes on each price level is averaged out as a manifestation of the law of large numbers.
Our result has several implications for optimal execution of orders. The limiting ODEs
describing the “sell-side shape” and “buy-side shape” can be solved explicitly in terms of
order arrival and cancellation rates (see (3.10) and (3.11)). The limiting best bid and best
ask prices converge to the same constant, which leads to a zero-spread model (Proposition 3.1). Such models of the shape and the price are endogenous in the sense that they
are derived from the interaction of order ﬂows. Thus, our models of the shape and price
can be taken as input in optimal trade execution problems and our theory could provide
theoretical guidance for ﬁnding optimal trading strategies.
Besides the implication for
trade execution using market orders, our result may be potentially useful for other trading
problems as well. For example, one may integrate the limiting shape density and obtain the
3

virtual price impact as in Weber and Rosenow (2005). Such a price impact model could be
used and provide insight into problems such as optimal execution using limit orders (see,
e.g., Bayraktar and Ludkovski (2012); Gu´eant et al. (2012); Gu´eant and Lehalle (2013)).
We also test our limiting deterministic model against historical order book data from
the U.S. exchange NYSE Arca. Our empirical analysis demonstrates that our theory can
be used to eﬀectively approximate the shape of an order book for stocks and time periods
which satisfy our model assumptions (Section 4). To the best of our knowledge, this is the
ﬁrst empirical result of this kind. The main goal of our empirical analysis is to illustrate the
explanatory power of the endogenous modeling of the order-book shape evolution on large
time scale rather than to make prediction about the future order-book shape, so no outof-sample prediction test is conducted. A predictive model of the order-book shape would
require additional realistic features such as non-stationarity, time-clustering and mutual
dependence of order ﬂows (see, e.g., Toke (2011), Abergel and Jedidi (2013)), which is left
to future research.
We now compare our work with some closely related alternatives which also study the
scaling limits of the shape of limit order books in certain asymptotic regimes. These include Kruk (2012), Lakner et al. (2013), Lakner et al. (2014) and Horst and Paulsen (2013).
Among them, only Horst and Paulsen (2013) incorporate order cancellations (modelling
order cancellations is important: for example, more than 95% of limit orders at NASDAQ are cancelled without execution, see Hautsch and Huang (2011)). Horst and Paulsen
(2013) prove a law of large numbers result for the dynamics of two-sided shape of an order
book. In their limit, the best bid and best ask price dynamics can be described by two
coupled ODEs and the relative buy and sell shape functions can be described by two linear
ﬁrst-order hyperbolic partial diﬀerential equations. Our work diﬀers from theirs in three
distinct aspects. First, unlike Horst and Paulsen (2013), which relies on a model assuming
that market orders and placements of limit orders in the spread both cause one-tick price
change, our limiting price and shape are endogenous in the sense that they are completely
determined by order ﬂow interactions. This property is inherited from the framework of
Cont et al. (2010). Second, we empirically test our limit theorem with order book data
and illustrate the eﬀectiveness of our approximation. Finally, our approach is based on a
martingale method while they use a certain averaging principle for Banach space-valued
processes.
The major mathematical challenges in establishing our limit theorem include (a) the
state space of the CTMC model in Cont et al. (2010) is the n−dimensional integer lattice and it becomes inﬁnitely dimensional as n →∞(or the tick size 1/n →0). This
is one of the key obstacles to applying standard limit theorems on the convergence of
sequences of Markov chains (see, e.g. Kurtz (1971)); (b) one may instead study the occupation measure of the CTMC (see (3.3) in Section 3), but its state space is the space
of signed measures. This space is ill-suited for studying weak convergence since the weak
topology is not metrizable (see, e.g., Spiliopoulos et al. (2014)); (c) the total number of
limit orders on the order book is stochastic and not ﬁxed, so there is no conservation law.
4

This contrasts with standard hydrodynamic limits of interacting particle systems which
also derive macroscopic behavior from microscopic interaction of particles (see, e.g., Kipnis
and Landim (1999)); (d) the price dynamics are determined by coupled order ﬂows, the
intensity of which depends on the opposite best quotes. Therefore it is not clear what
the limiting price dynamics would be. We overcome these challenges by (1) considering a
pair of non-negative measure-valued processes with ﬁxed state space which is equivalent
to the original CTMC and it still allows us to use a martingale approach; (2) establishing
stochastic bounds on the total number of limit orders (Lemma B.1); (3) exploiting Massey’s
stochastic comparison result (Massey (1987)) to show that the best bid and best ask prices
converge to the same point in the limit, thus “decoupling” the dynamics of buy-side and
sell-side shapes (Proposition 3.1).
1.2
Other Related Literature
The research literature contains various studies on diﬀerent aspects of the shape of a limit
order book besides the microscopic stochastic models of order books mentioned earlier.
Empirical works (e.g., Bouchaud et al. (2002), Potters and Bouchaud (2003), Gu et al.
(2008) and Biais et al. (2012)) typically focus on the steady-state properties of the shape
of limit order books, rather than the transient distribution we study here. Equilibrium
models (e.g., Glosten (1994), Ro¸su (2009)) have been proposed for analyzing the role of
economic variables such as trade size and price volatility in determining the shape of the
limit order book. These models provide insights into the determinants of the shape, but
as pointed out in Cont et al. (2010), they have unobservable parameters so it is diﬃcult
to estimate them or use them in practice. While our work focuses on the whole shape of
an order book, there are also other scaling limits for the limit order volumes at only two
price levels (best bid and best ask prices) (see, e.g., Cont and De Larrard (2011), Kirilenko
et al. (2013) and Blanchet and Chen (2013)).
1.3
Organization
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews a variant of the CTMC
model in Cont et al. (2010) for limit order book dynamics and states the assumptions on
order ﬂow rates and initial conditions. Section 3 summarizes our main result. Section 4
discusses empirical analysis. Section 5 presents preliminaries for proving the main result.
Section 6 is devoted to the proof of the result. Auxiliary results, their proofs and summary
statistics of data are provided in the appendices.

Empirical Analysis
In this section we perform empirical analysis to illustrate the eﬀectiveness of Theorem 3.1
in approximating the evolution of the shape of the order book on “large” time scale on the
magnitude of a few minutes.
4.1
Data
Our empirical analysis is based on one-month message-level order book data from NYSE
Arca (in short, Arca) in August 2010.
As of 2009, NYSE Arca is the second largest
electronic communication network in U.S. in terms of shares traded (around 20% of market
share for NASDAQ-listed securities and 10% of NYSE-listed securities).
Two data sets are used. The ﬁrst data set consists of all limit order activities on Arca,
including limit order submission, modiﬁcation and deletion. For each message in the data,
it contains a time stamp down to millisecond, the price and order size, the buy or sell
indicator, stock symbol, exchange, and an ID (identiﬁer).
This data set enables us to
recreate the limit order book at any give time for stocks traded on Arca. Our second data
set records all the trades. Each message contains a time stamp down to second, the traded
price and order size, the buy or sell indicator, the best bid/ask prices and standing limit
order quantities on these two prices when trades occur, stock symbol, and an ID (identiﬁer).
In conjunction with the ﬁrst data set, we can analyze the detailed order ﬂow properties
such as limit order arrival rate, market order arrival rate and limit order cancellation rate.
