Abstract 
 
This paper explores what a quantitative trading algorithm is, and how one can be developed and 
evaluated through statistical models and simulations. This independent research paper is exploratory 
and aims to create a basic understanding of how different models perform in practice. It is not to 
present a finished trading strategy.  
 
The historical price data for which the developed algorithms are based on is S&P 500 index fund 
(SPY) from 2021-2026. The five versions of the trading algorithms were developed and evaluated in 
Python. Each version introduced new tools, from basic market trends to an AI-driven sentiment 
analysis. The results show that model performance varies significantly through different market 
conditions, highlighting the importance of flexibility in an algorithm. 
 
The final model achieved a Sharpe ratio of 3.32 and a maximum drawdown of 5.43%. These results 
are promising but should be interpreted with caution. These models have only been tested on 
historical data, and historical performance can never be used as a guarantee for future outcomes.

1. Introduction 
If you wanted to buy stocks 30 years ago, you would call your stockbroker. Today, the majority of 
trading is conducted by advanced computer programs. Algorithms that analyze and execute millions of 
trades in a single split second, which also means that pricing and edge constantly change. Algorithmic 
trading has revolutionized trading. 
 
Quantitative trading is a combination of math, statistics, computer science, and economics. The core 
assumption is that markets are not fully stochastic, and that there actually are identifiable tendencies 
and correlations. This in turn means that models that systematically search for patterns and can 
execute their observations, can outperform almost every human trader. 
 
This research paper is about just that: Investigating how a quantitative trading algorithm can be built, 
tested, and improved step by step. Important to remember is that this paper is exploratory. The goal is 
not to present a finished solution, but instead to get an understanding of which mathematical and 
statistical tools are relevant for the development of a quantitative model. Furthermore, how these tools 
interact with each other and their limitations. 
 
This topic possesses significant relevance as it has been proven vital for normal societal function. 
Quantitative trading affects market liquidity, stability, and accessibility. The most common negative 
consequence of quantitative trading are flash crashes, which are quick automated crashes. Flash 
crashes are commonly triggered by badly adjusted risk in trading algorithms, which makes the 
algorithm amplify the market's volatility by an instant mass sell-off. Therefore, understanding how 
these systems work is not only crucial for investors, but also for everyone who seeks an understanding 
of today's economic system. 
 
Moreover, it is the topic’s combination of three disciplines that makes this an interesting topic. Math, 
programming and economy, all combined can prove an intellectual challenge for even the smartest of 
people. 
 
 
 
 
1.1 Purpose and questions 
This research paper aims to develop and evaluate a quantitative trading algorithm with historical 
market data, statistical methods, and programming. The work is exploratory, and the goal is not to 
create the perfect strategy, but rather to explore how different models perform and what factors prove 
important.  
The three main questions are: 
• 
Which mathematical and statistical principles are central for the development of a quantitative 
trading algorithm? 
• 
How can historical market data be analyzed and used to identify key trading signals? 
• 
How does the algorithm perform in backtesting, and which factors affect the result? 
 
 
 
1.2 Terms and definitions  
These are key concepts for the research paper. 

2 
 
Algorithmic trading 
Trading where computer programs based on predetermined rules, automatically perform buy and sell 
orders. 
Quantitative trading 
A form of algorithmic trading that builds on specific mathematical models and statistical analysis of 
historical data. 
Moving Average 
The average price under a specific period of time. Used to identify trends and filter out uncertainties 
Backtesting 
To try a trading strategy on old market data, to simulate its performance. The results should be lightly 
interpreted and only used as an indication. 
Overfitting 
When a model is negatively tailored for a specific dataset. 
Volatility 
A measurement of how much the price has moved, often under a certain time frame. 
Sharpe-quote 
A risk adjusted measurement that indicates how big an investments return is in proportion to how big 
the risk taken is. 
Maximal drawdown 
The biggest percentual loss after a high in a certain timeframe. It can be used to determine the worst 
period. 
Relative Strength Index (RSI) 
An indicator that measure based on the price development, if a product is oversold. 
Leverage 
Tool used to increase one’s position on the market. Leverage is achieved through borrowed capital or 
other financial tools. It increases both potential wins and losses. 
Sentiment 
A general assessment of the market, often based on news and events. 
Monte Carlo-simulation 
A statistical tool that performs hundreds of randomized scenarios to distribute outcome probability. 
Markov chain 
A probability model where the previous outcome affects the next outcomes probability. 
Look-ahead bias 
An analytical error where future information is used in back testing, which leads to unrealistic 
outcomes. 
 
 
2. Material and methods 

3 
 
This research paper is based on experimental trials and errors. It aims to iteratively build and test a 
trading algorithm on historical market data. Where every new iteration utilizes new models, this makes 
it possible to isolate and analyze the effect of every separate addition. 
 
The chosen trading commodity for which to base the algorithm is the SPDR S&P 500. The SPDR S&P 
500 is an index fund that consists of the 500 biggest publicly traded companies in the USA. SPY was 
chosen due to a plethora of reasons. Firstly, it is the world's oldest and most traded ETF, which 
guarantees high liquidity and reliable price data. Secondly, it reflects the price of 500 individual 
companies, which means that its price is more stable than a single company. A stable price makes it 
easier to develop a trading algorithm, whereas the algorithm does not need to analyze a specific 
company in addition to its market analysis. 
 
The timeframe is January 2021 to January 2026, a 5-year period. This timeframe allows for a recent 
market analysis with relatively few unforeseen market events. If the timeframe were any longer, the 
algorithm would need to take events such as 911, the 2008 financial crisis, and the 2020 corona 
pandemic into account. Including more unforeseen market events such as the listed ones makes it 
harder to calibrate the algorithm and increases the risk for misleading results. A five-year timeframe 
allows for enough recent market data while minimizing its complexity. 
 
The market data is taken from Yahoo! Finance, a news and financial platform with over 150 million 
monthly users. The extracted data were also cross-examined with other reliable sources; this ensures 
that the market data is reliable.

2.2 Sources and criticism 
Baver (2020), published at the New York University, lays much of this paper’s theoretical 
groundwork, technical analysis and algorithmic strategy development. The source is pedagogical and 
relatively neutral in its display, which improves reliability. However, the source is rather limited in its 
description of a few of its models. A more empirical and deeper description would have been more 
favorable. 
 
Automated Quantitative Trading Systems and Performance Evaluation (Phan Tien Dung & Giudici, 
2024), published through SSRN, is a crucial source. It highlights the paper’s most important 
limitation, that studies based on backtesting tend to blow the results out of proportion. The paper 
utilizes a clear scientific structure, while motivating its choice of methods. 
 
Guo et al. (2024), published on arXiv, discusses AI in quantitative trading and its relevance.  
This source requires a little skepticism as it is a preprint, it has not been fully peer reviewed. 
 
Investopedia is used for definitions and technical indicators. The website is constantly being reviewed 
for errors by experts. Therefore, the website can be seen as a reliable source for fact-based definitions. 

4 
 
 
An important limitation of this research paper is that back testing never fully can emulate real time 
trading. This is because there are always so many random factors, and a computer program can never 
create true randomization. Factors such as transaction cost, slippage, liquidity, and human actions. 
Therefore, the results should be used as an indicator of potential, not as a given. 
 
 
 
3. Previous research                                                           
Research in algorithmic and quantitative trading has been on an exponential increase during recent 
decades. It has grown together with computer availability and the strength of its processors. In this 
chapter, three relevant studies for this paper are presented, followed by an explanation of the 
theoretical principles behind this paper's algorithms. 
In a 2011 empirical study conducted by Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld, it was found through the 
New York Stock exchange that algorithmic trading affected the market's quality. The study concluded 
with establishing a correlation between automated trading and quicker price adjustments, which 
boosted liquidity and led to fewer price errors. Algorithms made the stock exchange more effective. 
The study was also able to identify that when many algorithms reacted to the same data at the same 
time, its actions were amplified which increased short term volatility. This means that there is a 
systematic dynamic relationship between algorithms. This can be summarized as algorithms never 
moving alone, other algorithms become a market factor to consider. 
 
3.1 Backtesting 
Phan Tien Dung and Giudici (2024) analyzed a broad spectrum of quantitative trading strategies and 
their performance in backtesting. They found that the strategies that performed well in backtesting 
often performed worse when used on new market data. This is one of the most common fallacies when 
developing a trading strategy, it is called overfitting. Overfitting occurs when the model has learned to 
describe the past rather than identifying new patterns.  
 
To combat this, the study recommends making the algorithm more realistic, and realism is achieved by 
including transaction costs, splitting the training data from the test data, and stress testing the strategy 
under different market conditions. They conclude by writing that simpler algorithms should focus 
more on the methods of reliability rather than maximum historical yield. 
3.2 AI                                                                                                                                                    
Guo, Wang, Ni and Shum investigated how AI models and machine learning (ML) could be used in 
automated trading (2023). Their study showed that AI and ML models outperformed traditional 
algorithms. The biggest gain was found in the AI and ML models prognostic capacity, they excelled at 
finding non-linear patterns in big datasets. They discussed the potential large language models (a type 
of ML) had at identifying market regimes by analyzing the news. 

5 
 
The study simultaneously identified the risks. AI models tend to overfit easily, while also being hard 
interpreted and rigid. These limitations prove extremely relevant for version 5, where a language 
model is integrated. 
3.3 Theoretical principles                                                                                                                       
The core principles of quantitative trading are mostly theoretical. Although the market is complex and 
partially unforeseeable, statistics have shown that it can be modeled. Baver (2020) highlights how the 
market should be viewed as a stochastic system where the outcome can be calculated through 
analyzing the past.  
This can be achieved with time-series analysis. It analyzes historical price data to identify trends, 
patterns, and changes in market measurements such as volatility. By considering relative price 
changes, a more stable and comparable representation of the market is achieved. Volatility 
measurements and momentum indicators provide new information about the current market 
conditions. 
Monte Carlo simulation with Markov chains enables a better overview of the probability of future 
outcomes. This enables the algorithm to cancel some of the randomness in a market, allowing for a 
better and more realistic depiction of its performance. The Markov chains provide another dimension 
of realism as probability is constantly changing and dependent on previous outcomes. Calibrating the 
probability in Markov chains is the hard part. A realistic Markov chain requires a huge number of 
datasets and is paradoxically, also dependent on integrated randomness. 
 
4. Experiments and results 
In this chapter, five versions of an algorithm were developed and tested. The versions are 
successively built upon the previous iteration. This allows for the isolation of new models, 
which creates a more accurate understanding of what every new model actually does. The 
performance is measured by two key measurements, sharpe quote and maximal drawdown. 
This is because the yield in relation to the risk and the biggest loss after a high, allows for a 
fair comparison. Factors such as trade frequency and trading volume are not allowed to 
impact the algorithms' total reliability. 
 
4.1 Common methods 
All versions feature the same core algorithm. The core algorithm utilizes logarithmic returns 
to measure daily price changes, which is more realistic as it is additive over time and not just 
a simple percentual change. 
Another core principle is the moving average. It has a 20- and 50-day timeframe where  
long- and short-term trends are identified. When the shorter moving average crosses the 
longer one from above, a buy signal is generated. Vice versa for the long moving average but 
instead of a buy signal, a sell signal is created. This is a commonly used and well tested 
principle in technical analysis.

4.3 Version 2 
The second iteration utilizes three additions. The first addition being a volatility filter. The 
filter worked by generating trade signals when the volatility was really high, high, low and

conditions. 
 
The second addition was stop-loss and take-profit function. The daily loss was capped at a 
maximum of 2% meanwhile win was capped at 4%. These caps further limited the algorithms 
exposure to risk in a single day. The third addition were more strict signals. A buy signal can 
now only be generated when three requirements are met, positive trend, low volatility and 
high positive momentum.

5. Analysis and discussion 
 
This chapter analyzes the results in relation to the papers main questions and the previous research 
represented in chapter 3. This aims to build an understanding of what happened, why, and what the 
results actually mean. 
 
5.1 Mathematical and statistical principles 
The results show that a combination of mathematical and statistical principles directly affects the 
algorithm's performance. The performance of the first version, that only utilized moving average, 

9 
 
gradually became better with each new version's implementation. A single tool such as moving 
average is not bad just because it performs bad, but rather one of many good tools that need to be 
composed to generate a good result.  
 
From version two to four, a combination of principles is implemented, Volatility detectors that inform 
the algorithm of the current market conditions pared with a 200-day moving average window that 
gives it a longer timeframe, takes a more well-grounded decision together. Baver (2020) and 
Hendershott et al. (2011) both highlight that statistical models work best when combined which 
confirms the result. 
 
Monte Carlo simulation with Markov chain enables the algorithm to acceptably quantify uncertainty, 
which minimizes the risk of statistical errors. Furthermore, this also communicates the algorithms' 
risks and limitations better. This conclusion supports the findings in Automated Quantitative Trading 
Systems (2024) that being able to robustly evaluate an algorithm is just as important as its results.  
 
 
5.2 Historical data and signals 
The developed algorithms show that historical market data can be transformed into useful trading 
signals, but that the processes require filtration and management. Price data first need to be 
transformed into different series and filtered before it can be used. 
 
The integration of sentiment analysis in version five highlights how market data is intertwined with 
real world actions. How expectations, news and market atmosphere reflect in the market. The language 
model complemented the technical indicators, which improved the algorithms' signal handling. 
 
This confirms what Guo et al. (2023) showed that AI models can improve prognostication meanwhile 
demanding carefulness. The sentimental analysis is plagued with a heightened risk of lookahead bias. 
It is uncertain how the model would have performed if the news were fresh. 
 
5.3 The algorithms performance and previous research 
The algorithm’s results followed a linear improvement with every new iteration. From a negative 
Sharpe quote in version one, to a 3,32 in version 5. Every new version introduced something that was 
previously missed. The improvements were consistent and measurable. 
 
Hendershott et al. (2011) confirm this in their study. Automated and rule-based systems are more 
likely to make disciplined choices than humans. This can be explained to a system's lack of fear and 
greed. It has a structural advantage that becomes more prominent with every new version. 
 
The results are also confirmed by Automated Quantitative Trading Systems (2024) that back testing is 
optimistic by nature. This is clear in version five, where there was a high risk that the sentimental 
analysis was interpreted retroactively. An improvement would be to paper trade the strategy, to fully 
eradicate the possibility of lookahead bias. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with 
carefulness. 
 
5.4 Leverage and risk 
The leverage that was introduced in version five proved to be a great addition. The results show that 
sentimental-based leverage improved yield, but also risk taken. The potential loss is always equal to 
the potential gain of a trade when leverage is used. That means that leverage should mostly be used 
when the uncertainty is low. This is taken into account in version five with its dynamic leveraging and 
need for independent signals. High leverage when the positive signals were many and defensive 
positions when they were few or negative. How effective the leverage would be outside of the back 
testing would need to be verified with actual trading.

5.5 Answers to main questions 
The three main questions are answered: 
 
Which mathematical and statistical principles are central for the development of a quantitative 
trading algorithm? 
Timeseries analysis, volatility, RSI indicator, and risk adjusted positioning proved vital for an 
algorithm's performance. Monte Carlo simulation with Markov chains is a useful tool for modelling 
uncertainty and indicating an algorithm's reliability. It is important to combine principles and models 
tastefully; they do not perform well single or as a random composition. 
 
How can historical market data be analyzed and used to identify key trading signals? 
Raw price data need to be sorted, interpreted, and then they can become the base for an algorithm. If 
not, then the data is unreliable and can create biases such as the look ahead bias.  Sentiment analysis 
can provide new information that is not reflected in the price data.  
 
How does the algorithm perform in back testing, and which factors affect the result? 
The algorithms results improved gradually in parity with the gradual increase of complexity. Risk 
adjustment, indicators, data quality, and leverage all proportionally improved the outcome. The 
method of reliability is as important as its results when back testing. 
 
5.6 Final note 
The results as a whole indicate that a fine-tuned combination of statistical methods and AI-driven 
analysis can improve a trading algorithm's reliability and profitability. But historical data should 
always be carefully interpreted. This is a basis for reflection and further research, not a promise of 
future yield.

Moving average crossover signal logic 
def create_features(df):  
 
df['return'] = np.log(df['Close'] / df['Close'].shift(1))  
  
df['ma_20'] = df['Close'].rolling(20).mean()  
df['ma_50'] = df['Close'].rolling(50).mean()  
  

16 
 
df['volatility'] = df['return'].rolling(20).std()  
  
return df  
   
def generate_signals(df):  
df['signal'] = 0  
  
df.loc[df['ma_20'] > df['ma_50'], 'signal'] = 1  
df.loc[df['ma_20'] < df['ma_50'], 'signal'] = -1  
  
df['position'] = df['signal'].shift(1)  
  
return df  
   
def backtest(df):  
df['strategy_return'] = df['position'] * df['return']  
  
df['cum_market'] = df['return'].cumsum().apply(np.exp)  
df['cum_strategy'] = df['strategy_return'].cumsum().apply(np.exp)  
df['strategy_return'] = np.clip(df['strategy_return'], -0.03, 0.05)  
stop_loss = -0.02   
  
df['strategy_return'] = np.where(  
df['strategy_return'] < stop_loss,  
stop_loss,  
df['strategy_return']  
)  
take_profit = 0.04   
df['strategy_return'] = np.where(  
df['strategy_return'] > take_profit,  
take_profit,  
df['strategy_return']  
)  
  
return df
