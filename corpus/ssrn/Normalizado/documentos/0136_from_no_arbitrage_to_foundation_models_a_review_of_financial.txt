Abstract
Artificial intelligence increasingly enters quantitative finance through option pricing, volatility calibration, hedging, asset pricing, risk measurement, market simulation, financial text analysis, and time-series forecasting. Yet finance differs from many
prediction domains because a statistically accurate model can still be economically
invalid.
Prices must respect no-arbitrage restrictions, discounted asset prices must
be consistent with martingale arguments under suitable measures, volatility surfaces
must satisfy static and dynamic admissibility conditions, hedging rules must account
for transaction costs and risk preferences, and scenario generators must preserve tail
behavior rather than merely match average dependence. This semi-systematic review
studies modern financial AI through this constraint-centered perspective. It organizes
the literature from arbitrage pricing and stochastic volatility to neural option pricing,
deep calibration, deep hedging, no-arbitrage asset pricing, generative market models,
financial large language models, and time-series foundation models. The review makes
three contributions. First, it separates financial constraints into structural admissibility, task-level economic objectives, and governance-validity requirements. Second, it
identifies recurring design patterns for constraint injection, including hard parameterization, soft penalties, projection or repair, adversarial testing, and temporal validation. Third, it proposes layered and task-specific evaluation criteria that distinguish
statistical fit from mathematical admissibility, economic usefulness, robustness, and
deployability. The main argument is that financial mathematics is not a legacy layer
that deep learning replaces. It provides the inductive biases, loss functions, diagnostic
tests, and deployment gates that make AI models usable in quantitative finance.
Keywords: no-arbitrage; financial mathematics; generative AI; deep learning; quantitative
finance; option pricing; asset pricing; market generators; foundation models.

1
Introduction
The rapid diffusion of generative AI and deep learning into finance creates a productive
tension. Neural networks, transformers, neural stochastic differential equations, generative
adversarial networks, diffusion models, and large language models provide flexible tools for
learning nonlinear relations from high-dimensional data.
They can approximate pricing
functions, infer latent factors, generate market paths, summarize financial documents, and
transfer across related time-series tasks. At the same time, financial data are noisy, nonstationary, strategically adaptive, and shaped by equilibrium restrictions. A model can fit
historical returns, option prices, or financial news while violating basic economic conditions.
In quantitative finance, this is not a minor modeling imperfection. It can produce arbitrage
opportunities that cannot exist in a competitive market, unstable hedges, misleading risk
estimates, and backtests that disappear once realistic trading frictions and temporal leakage
are controlled.
This review therefore takes financial-mathematical constraints as its organizing principle.
The central question is how no-arbitrage, martingale pricing, stochastic volatility, calibration, hedging error, and tail-risk constraints enter modern AI models. This framing differs
from general surveys of AI in finance, which often classify papers by task, such as forecasting, sentiment analysis, portfolio allocation, or risk management. It also differs from
surveys of neural option pricing or financial large language models because those literatures
usually focus on one model family or one application area. The proposed perspective treats
mathematical constraints as a common language across derivatives, asset pricing, market
generation, and foundation models.
The intellectual starting point is the arbitrage-pricing tradition. Black and Scholes (1973)
and Merton (1973) show that derivative prices are restricted by dynamic replication and
rational pricing arguments. Harrison and Kreps (1979) and Harrison and Pliska (1981) formulate the martingale foundation of asset pricing. Heath et al. (1992) develop a no-arbitrage
framework for the term structure. Heston (1993), Dupire (1994), Comte and Renault (1998),
and Gatheral et al. (2018) enrich the modeling of volatility through stochastic, local, longmemory, and rough-volatility structures. These works do more than provide classical baselines. They define admissibility conditions that modern learning systems must respect if
they are to produce financially meaningful outputs.
The central thesis of this review is that financial mathematics should be read as a design
language for AI in quantitative finance. In this view, no-arbitrage is not merely a theorem
that explains why a classical price is valid. It becomes an architectural constraint, a regularization principle, a diagnostic test, and a rejection criterion for learned outputs. Martingale
pricing is not simply a historical foundation of derivatives. It becomes a way to distinguish
statistical fit from economic consistency.
Calibration is not only a numerical procedure.
It becomes a test of whether a learned map respects economically meaningful parameters
and extrapolates safely. Tail risk is not a secondary reporting statistic. It becomes a core
objective for generators and risk engines.
This review makes three contributions. First, it separates financial constraints into structural admissibility, task-level economic objectives, and governance-validity requirements.
This resolves a common ambiguity in AI-finance surveys, where no-arbitrage restrictions,
hedging losses, tail-risk metrics, and leakage controls are often discussed as if they operated
2

at the same level. Second, it identifies recurring design patterns for constraint injection,
including hard parameterization, soft penalties, projection or repair, adversarial testing, and
temporal validation. Third, it proposes a task-specific evaluation matrix that distinguishes
statistical accuracy from mathematical admissibility, economic usefulness, robustness, and
deployability. The resulting criterion is deliberately stronger than generic predictive performance: an AI model is useful in quantitative finance only when it is statistically competent,
financially admissible, economically meaningful, and valid at the time of decision.
These contributions can be stated as three propositions. Proposition 1 is that financial
constraints should be classified by level of operation: structural admissibility defines what
outputs are financially possible, task-level objectives define what outputs are useful, and
governance-validity requirements define whether the evidence supporting the model is valid.
Proposition 2 is that statistical fit is only the first layer of evaluation; quantitative-finance
models must also pass mathematical admissibility, economic performance, robustness, and
governance tests. Proposition 3 is that constraint injection is not a single technique but a
design choice among hard parameterization, soft penalization, projection, adversarial testing,
and temporal validation. In short, this review does not merely summarize AI in finance. It
defines the conditions under which AI can be considered financially acceptable.

Review Methodology: Search and Screening
This review uses a semi-systematic design that combines structured search with narrative
synthesis. It is not a bibliometric meta-analysis and does not report prevalence estimates
for the entire AI-in-finance literature. The objective is narrower: to identify work in which
financial-mathematical constraints shape model architecture, learning objectives, diagnostics, or deployment validation.
The search used five source channels: Google Scholar for broad cross-disciplinary discovery; arXiv and SSRN for recent preprints in machine learning, quantitative finance, and
financial economics; Crossref and publisher databases, including ScienceDirect, SpringerLink,
Wiley Online Library, Taylor & Francis Online, SIAM, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library where relevant, for journal and conference versions; reference lists from major surveys;
and forward/backward citation chaining from foundational papers. The search window emphasized work from 2017 to 2026, while classical financial-mathematics foundations were
included outside this window when they define constraints used by later AI methods.
The search strings combined finance and AI keyword families. The finance family included “no-arbitrage,” “martingale pricing,” “option pricing,” “implied volatility,” “volatility surface,” “deep hedging,” “asset pricing,” “stochastic discount factor,” “market generator,” “financial time series,” “financial LLM,” and “financial foundation model.” The
AI family included “deep learning,” “neural network,” “neural SDE,” “BSDE,” “generative model,” “GAN,” “diffusion,” “transformer,” “foundation model,” and “large language
model.” Searches were run as paired combinations, for example “no-arbitrage neural network,” “volatility surface generative model,” “deep hedging transaction costs,” “asset pricing machine learning no-arbitrage,” “financial time series foundation model,” and “financial
LLM temporal leakage.”
Records were included when they satisfied at least one of four criteria: (i) they introduced
3

or formalized a financial constraint that later constrains AI models; (ii) they embedded such
a constraint into a learning architecture, loss function, calibration procedure, or simulation
mechanism; (iii) they evaluated AI outputs through financial admissibility, economic performance, or leakage-aware diagnostics; or (iv) they proposed benchmarks or foundation-model
protocols relevant to quantitative finance. Records were excluded when they used AI for
finance without an identifiable financial-mathematical constraint, focused only on generic
NLP or forecasting without temporal or economic validation, lacked sufficient methodological detail, or duplicated earlier versions of the same work.
Deduplication and screening proceeded in three stages.
First, duplicate records were
removed by matching title, author list, year, DOI, arXiv identifier, and published/preprint
versions of the same paper. When both a preprint and a peer-reviewed version were available, the peer-reviewed version was preferred unless the preprint contained materially newer
content. Second, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to at least one application
domain: derivatives, asset pricing, market generation, financial LLMs, or time-series foundation models. Third, full texts were assessed for whether the financial constraint affected
the model, objective, evaluation protocol, or deployment logic rather than appearing only
as background motivation.
The final included set contains 51 records: 31 published journal or practitioner-journal
articles, five conference papers, one book or book chapter, and 14 arXiv or other preprints.
Preprints are retained because the topics of financial LLMs, time-series foundation models, and market generators are moving faster than journal publication cycles.
They are
used mainly to characterize recent methods and open benchmarks, while stronger inferential
weight is placed on peer-reviewed journal articles, conference papers, and classical financialmathematics foundations.
Each included record was coded along four dimensions: application domain, financial
constraint, constraint-injection mechanism, and evaluation protocol. Evidence was weighted
more heavily when a study combined methodological novelty with out-of-sample, leakagecontrolled, arbitrage-aware, tail-aware, or cost-aware evaluation.
This design supports a
constraint-centered synthesis rather than a frequency count of topics.
Table 1 positions this review against closely related surveys. The contribution is not
breadth alone. The review contributes a constraint-centered evaluation logic: AI models in
finance should be classified not only by algorithm or task, but by the financial restrictions
they encode, violate, or test.
3
Three-Layer Constraint Taxonomy
No-arbitrage is the most basic constraint in financial modeling. In its static form, it rules out
price systems that permit a nonnegative payoff with positive probability of gain and no initial
cost. In its dynamic form, it connects admissible trading strategies, equivalent martingale
measures, and discounted asset-price processes. The fundamental theorem of asset pricing
gives this connection its canonical form. Under suitable technical conditions, absence of
arbitrage corresponds to the existence of a pricing measure under which discounted prices
behave as martingales. This insight matters for AI because it turns a qualitative economic
principle into testable restrictions on model outputs.

Option markets provide a clear example. A learned option surface must preserve monotonicity and convexity in strike, suitable calendar-spread relations across maturities, and
consistency between call and put prices. A model that minimizes root mean squared pricing
error but violates these restrictions can generate prices that cannot coexist in a frictionless market.
Similarly, implied volatility models must respect shape restrictions implied
by static arbitrage and must remain stable under extrapolation because risk systems and
hedging desks often require prices outside the dense region of observed quotes.
Calibration is another central constraint. In classical models, calibration maps market
prices to model parameters, such as volatility, mean reversion, correlation, and jump intensity. In deep learning, calibration often becomes an inverse problem solved by neural
surrogates or amortized maps. The speed advantage is important, but speed alone is insufficient. The learned map must preserve economically meaningful parameter regions, maintain
stability across maturities and strikes, and avoid overfitting microstructure noise.
Deep
calibration therefore sits between numerical approximation and financial identification.
Hedging adds a decision-theoretic layer. A price model can be judged by pricing error,
but a hedging model must also account for trading frequency, transaction costs, liquidity, risk
aversion, and the distribution of terminal hedging loss. Deep hedging is important because
it formulates hedging as an optimization problem under frictions and convex risk measures
(Buehler et al., 2019). This shifts attention from replicating a frictionless delta hedge to
learning policies that minimize economically relevant loss functions.
Tail risk imposes a further constraint on both predictive and generative models. Value-atRisk and Expected Shortfall are imperfect but widely used summaries of downside exposure.
Financial generators that reproduce mean, variance, and autocorrelation while underestimating extremes are dangerous. Conversely, a model that explicitly optimizes tail-sensitive
metrics may be more useful for stress testing even if it does not dominate on standard forecast errors. This is one reason why recent work on generative financial time series places
increasing emphasis on VaR, Expected Shortfall, drawdowns, and distributional backtesting
(Ericson et al., 2024; Cont et al., 2025).
The constraints reviewed here are not homogeneous. They operate at three levels. Structural constraints define the admissible financial object, task-level constraints define what a
model must optimize for a specific use case, and governance constraints define whether the
empirical evidence supporting the model is valid at decision time. Table 2 summarizes this
three-layer taxonomy. It is organized by the level at which the restriction enters the modeling
pipeline rather than by algorithm, because the same algorithm can be economically useful
or invalid depending on how the restriction is imposed and tested.

The modern literature moves from generic approximation toward constraint-aware learning. For high-dimensional derivatives, deep PDE and BSDE methods address the curse of
dimensionality by learning solution representations or control processes (Han et al., 2018;
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2018; Beck et al., 2019). These methods are relevant to finance
because many derivative-pricing problems are naturally expressed through parabolic partial
differential equations or backward stochastic differential equations. Their financial value,
however, depends on boundary conditions, stability, and consistency with the underlying
pricing measure.
Deep calibration provides another bridge between classical models and neural approximation. Hernandez (2017) shows how neural networks can accelerate model calibration. Bayer
et al. (2019) extend this direction to rough stochastic-volatility models, where standard calibration is computationally demanding. Biagini et al. (2024) provide theoretical support by
studying approximation rates for deep calibration. The common lesson is that neural networks are most convincing when they accelerate a financially meaningful map rather than
replace the pricing theory that defines the map.
Rough volatility illustrates why this distinction matters. Gatheral et al. (2018) show
that volatility is empirically rough, which motivates models with fractional or long-memory
structure. These models capture important features of implied volatility but create difficult
calibration problems. Deep calibration can make them operational, yet the learned system
must still preserve the model-implied relation between paths, option prices, and volatility
surfaces. Otherwise, the output is fast but not financially interpretable.
Deep hedging makes constraints even more explicit.
Buehler et al. (2019) formulate
hedging under transaction costs and risk preferences as a learning problem. The objective is
not to recover a classical hedge in an idealized market but to learn a strategy that manages
the distribution of losses in a realistic environment. This changes the role of deep learning.
It becomes a policy-learning tool governed by a financial objective rather than a black-box
price interpolator.
Recent neural SDE market models further tighten the link between learning and noarbitrage. Cohen et al. (2023) construct neural SDE market models on an arbitrage-free
state space of option prices. This is directly aligned with the thesis of the present review.
Instead of training a flexible dynamics model and testing for arbitrage afterward, the model
architecture and state representation incorporate admissibility from the outset. Such work
suggests a general design principle. When the financial constraint is known, it should be
built into the model space, the loss, or the simulation mechanism before deployment.

Constraint-Driven Asset Pricing
Asset pricing raises a different but related problem. The object is not a derivative surface but
the cross section and time series of expected returns. Traditional factor models, including
Fama and French (1992) and Carhart (1997), impose low-dimensional economic structure.
Later work on latent factors and characteristics, such as Kelly et al. (2019), Kozak et al.
(2018), and Freyberger et al. (2020), enriches this structure while retaining the central role
8

of economic restrictions.
Machine learning changes empirical asset pricing by modeling nonlinear interactions
among firm characteristics and macro states. Gu et al. (2020) show that machine learning methods improve return prediction relative to many traditional approaches, especially
when the feature space is large and nonlinear. Yet pure prediction is not the final objective
in asset pricing. Expected returns are equilibrium objects, and a predictive signal must be
evaluated through portfolio performance, risk adjustment, and no-arbitrage restrictions.
Autoencoder asset pricing models address this issue directly. Gu et al. (2021) use an
autoencoder structure to learn latent factors and conditional exposures while maintaining
an asset-pricing interpretation. The model compresses information into factors that explain
expected returns through an economic pricing relation. This makes the autoencoder more
than a dimensionality-reduction device. It becomes a mechanism for imposing structure on
high-dimensional return data.
Chen et al. (2024) further develop this line by using deep learning together with noarbitrage criteria and adversarial test assets. The adversarial component searches for portfolios that expose pricing errors, which makes the learning process closer to an economic stress
test than a standard supervised-learning exercise. This approach is important because it
treats no-arbitrage not as a theorem cited in the introduction but as an operational training
and evaluation principle.
The asset-pricing literature also warns against naive use of AI. Financial returns have
low signal-to-noise ratios, unstable regimes, and severe multiple-testing risks. A model can
appear successful because of data snooping, survivor bias, look-ahead bias, or an evaluation period that favors a particular style. Constraint-aware asset-pricing AI must therefore
combine statistical validation with economic validation. Out-of-sample prediction, portfolio
turnover, transaction costs, capacity, risk exposures, and crash performance all belong to
the evaluation set.
6
Generative Market Models and Risk Validation
Generative models are attractive in finance because real market data are scarce, proprietary,
and regime-dependent. Synthetic paths can support stress testing, model validation, reinforcement learning, and risk measurement. However, financial generation is harder than
visual or textual generation in one crucial respect. The generated samples must be tradable, risk-relevant, and economically admissible. A market generator that looks realistic by
standard distributional metrics can still fail if it creates arbitrage, suppresses tail events, or
breaks cross-asset dependence.
Time-series GANs provide a general starting point. Yoon et al. (2019) propose TimeGAN
for sequential data, while Ni et al. (2020) use signature-based metrics for time-series generation. Neural controlled differential equations (Kidger et al., 2020) and neural SDEs (Gierjatowicz et al., 2020) also offer continuous-time representations that are natural for irregular
financial data. These methods improve the expressive power of generative finance, but financial validity still requires domain-specific tests.
Option markets make this requirement especially visible. Wiese et al. (2019) study learning to simulate equity option markets, while Cuchiero et al. (2020) connect generative ad9

versarial methods with calibration of local stochastic-volatility models. Cont and Vuletic
(2023) focus on arbitrage-free implied volatility surfaces, and Vuletic and Cont (2024) propose VolGAN for arbitrage-free implied-volatility surface generation. This line of work is
central because it treats arbitrage constraints as part of the generative target.
Tail-GAN provides a complementary example for risk management. Cont et al. (2025)
develop nonparametric scenario generation for tail-risk estimation. The key insight is that
a generator for risk management should be evaluated by the quality of tail-risk estimates,
not only by its ability to imitate average sample moments. This aligns with the broader
principle that the evaluation metric must reflect the financial use case.
Recent surveys of deep generative financial time series and market generators synthesize
this emerging field (Ericson et al., 2024; Caulfield and Gleeson, 2024; Horvath et al., 2026).
They show that model comparison remains difficult because datasets, preprocessing choices,
stylized-fact tests, and downstream tasks vary widely. For a constraint-centered review, the
most important open issue is the absence of a unified benchmark that combines statistical
similarity, no-arbitrage diagnostics, tail-risk accuracy, and trading relevance.
7
Financial Large Language Models and Time-Series
Foundation Models
This review uses the term “foundation model” operationally rather than as a marketing
label. A model is treated as a financial foundation-model candidate only if it is pretrained at
scale on broad financial or time-series corpora, supports transfer across multiple downstream
tasks, and can be evaluated under timestamped, leakage-controlled protocols. Under this
definition, BloombergGPT and FinGPT are financial language-model candidates; Chronos,
Lag-Llama, TimesFM, and MOIRAI are time-series foundation-model candidates; FinBERT
and PatchTST are important baselines or precursor architectures rather than equivalent
foundation models unless broad transfer and task generality are demonstrated.
7.1
Financial Language Models
Financial large language models extend AI in finance from numerical prediction to text-rich
decision environments. BloombergGPT (Wu et al., 2023) shows the value of domain-specific
pretraining for financial NLP tasks. FinGPT (Yang et al., 2023) emphasizes open-source
adaptation and instruction tuning for finance. FinBERT (Araci, 2019) remains an important pre-LLM baseline for financial sentiment analysis. Surveys of large language models in
finance and FinLLMs map applications in sentiment analysis, information extraction, question answering, report generation, and decision support (Li et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025).
These models are useful because financial markets digest text from news, filings, earnings
calls, analyst reports, and social media.
Yet financial LLMs introduce special risks. Textual signals are time-stamped, revised,
leaked, and unevenly disseminated. Evaluation must ensure that the model does not access
future information through pretraining data, revised databases, or benchmark construction.
For a financial LLM, a benchmark built from revised filings or post-event news labels can
overstate signal quality; valid evaluation must restrict each prompt, retrieval corpus, and
10

label to information available at the forecast timestamp. Lopez-Lira and Tang (2023) connect
LLM-based news interpretation with stock return predictability, but such evidence must be
read together with market efficiency, transaction costs, and decay of public signals.
In
quantitative finance, a language model is not validated by linguistic fluency. It is validated
by its incremental information content after controlling for timing, tradability, risk, and
implementation costs.
7.2
Time-Series Foundation Models
Time-series foundation models create a parallel opportunity. Chronos (Ansari et al., 2024),
Lag-Llama (Rasul et al., 2023), TimesFM (Das et al., 2024), and MOIRAI (Woo et al.,
2024) show that large pretrained models can transfer across forecasting tasks. PatchTST
(Nie et al., 2023) and the critique by Zeng et al. (2023) provide important context for
understanding when transformer-based forecasting helps and when simpler models remain
competitive. These models are relevant to finance because markets generate many related
time series across assets, maturities, frequencies, and macro variables.
However, general time-series pretraining is not the same as financial modeling. Financial series are shaped by trading calendars, microstructure noise, corporate actions, regime
shifts, leverage effects, volatility clustering, and policy events. They also respond to model
deployment itself. A foundation model for quantitative finance must therefore be evaluated through rolling-origin backtests, leakage audits, distributional forecasts, tail calibration, transaction-cost-aware trading simulations, and stress-period performance. Without
these tests, foundation-model accuracy can be a statistical artifact rather than an economic
advantage.
7.3
Toward Quantitative-Finance Foundation Models
A promising direction is to combine language, time series, and financial constraints in a
single architecture.
Such a model could process news, filings, prices, volatility surfaces,
order-book states, and macro data while enforcing no-arbitrage, temporal causality, and risk
constraints. This is closer to a financial foundation model than a generic LLM applied to
finance. The challenge is not only scale. It is the design of objectives and benchmarks that
reward economically valid reasoning.
8
Unified Evaluation Framework and Benchmark Gaps
The constraint-centered view makes it possible to compare areas that are often reviewed
separately. Option pricing, empirical asset pricing, market generation, and financial foundation models differ in data structure and objective, but they face a common problem. Pure
statistical learning is too weak because the object being learned is embedded in an economic
system. The relevant question is not only whether the model fits data. It is whether the
model respects the restrictions that make the data financially meaningful.
In derivatives, the mathematical constraints are explicit. Static arbitrage, put-call parity,
calendar restrictions, and martingale pricing provide clear tests.
This makes derivatives
11

a natural laboratory for constraint-aware AI. The weakness is that many neural pricing
studies still evaluate models on interpolation accuracy rather than on the harder tasks of
extrapolation, stress behavior, and live hedging. A model that prices options accurately
within the observed strike-maturity grid may fail exactly where risk managers need it most,
which is outside dense quote regions and during volatility shocks.
In asset pricing, the constraint is less local but more economically ambitious. No-arbitrage
does not produce a single option surface condition. It restricts the relation between expected
returns, factors, stochastic discount factors, and test assets. The strength of recent deep
asset-pricing work is that it uses no-arbitrage as an objective or adversarial criterion rather
than as background theory. The weakness is that return predictability remains fragile. A
model may find nonlinear signals that are statistically real in one sample but economically
weak after turnover, costs, short-sale constraints, and capacity are considered.
In generative market modeling, the central problem is validation. A generator can pass
distributional similarity tests while failing financial diagnostics.
Conversely, a generator
that is imperfect in generic time-series metrics may be valuable if it produces realistic stress
scenarios and tail losses. The strength of recent work such as VolGAN and Tail-GAN is that
it aligns the generative objective with financial use cases. The weakness is that there is still
no standard evaluation protocol that jointly covers arbitrage, stylized facts, risk metrics, and
downstream decision quality.
In financial LLMs and time-series foundation models, the main constraint is temporal
causality. Pretraining scale can hide future information, revised data, and benchmark contamination. For this reason, the validation of financial foundation models must be stricter
than the validation of generic language or forecasting models. Their promise is real because finance is a multimodal domain where text, prices, volatility, macro variables, and
microstructure signals interact. Their risk is also real because a model can seem intelligent
while using information that would not be available at decision time.
This comparison supports a direct evaluation proposition: the minimum admissibility test
depends on the financial object being generated or acted upon. Derivatives require arbitragefree surfaces and hedge-aware stress tests; asset pricing requires no-arbitrage pricing errors
and cost-adjusted performance; market generation requires tail and downstream risk validation; financial LLMs and time-series foundation models require timestamped, leakage-free
evaluation.
A recurring weakness in AI finance is the overuse of generic predictive metrics. Mean
squared error, classification accuracy, and likelihood are useful but incomplete.
A pricing model needs pricing accuracy, calibration stability, and arbitrage checks.
A hedging
model needs terminal loss distributions, turnover, transaction costs, and sensitivity to market
regimes. An asset-pricing model needs risk-adjusted performance, factor exposures, capacity, and robustness across samples. A market generator needs stylized facts, cross-sectional
dependence, tail-risk accuracy, and no-arbitrage diagnostics. A financial LLM needs temporal leakage control, factual reliability, source attribution, and economically meaningful
downstream tests.
This review therefore proposes a layered evaluation framework. The first layer is statistical fit, including forecast error, likelihood, distributional distance, and calibration. The
second layer is mathematical admissibility, including no-arbitrage, martingale consistency,
shape restrictions, and valid volatility dynamics. The third layer is economic performance,
12

including hedging loss, risk-adjusted returns, drawdowns, turnover, capacity, and transaction
costs. The fourth layer is robustness, including regime changes, stress periods, data revisions,
adversarial perturbations, and out-of-market extrapolation. The fifth layer is governance,
including interpretability, auditability, reproducibility, and model-risk documentation.
The layered framework becomes more useful when translated into task-specific minimum
reporting standards. Table 5 gives such a matrix. The entries are not meant to be exhaustive;
they define the minimum evidence needed before a model can be interpreted as financially
meaningful rather than merely predictive.
For quantitative deployment, statistical fit is never sufficient on its own. A model that
fails the relevant structural admissibility test should be treated as financially invalid even if
it improves generic predictive accuracy. Economic performance and governance tests then
determine whether an admissible model is useful and deployable.
This layered framework also clarifies the relation between old and new methods. Classical
models often have strong admissibility and interpretability but limited flexibility.
Deep
models often have high flexibility but weak guarantees.
The goal is not to declare one
side superior. The goal is to design models that inherit the economic discipline of financial
mathematics and the representational power of modern AI.
The benchmark implication is equally direct. Quantitative finance lacks shared benchmarks that combine prices, returns, volatility surfaces, text, macro variables, and trading
frictions under strict timestamping. A useful benchmark should prevent leakage, record data
revisions, include realistic transaction costs, evaluate stress periods, and report both statistical and economic metrics. Without such benchmarks, claims about foundation models in
finance remain difficult to compare.
9
Design Patterns for Constraint Injection
The literature suggests several design patterns for injecting financial constraints into AI
models. The first is hard parameterization. In this approach, the model output is restricted
by construction to an admissible set. For example, an implied-volatility surface generator can
be parameterized so that it satisfies convexity and monotonicity conditions, or a neural SDE
can be defined on a state space that excludes arbitrage. The advantage is strong validity.
The disadvantage is that the parameterization may be difficult to design and may reduce
flexibility.
The second pattern is soft penalization. The training loss combines statistical fit with
penalties for arbitrage, calibration error, hedging loss, tail-risk underestimation, or temporal
leakage. This approach is easy to implement and compatible with many model classes. Its
weakness is that penalty weights are often unstable and may not guarantee validity under
extrapolation. A small average penalty can still allow severe violations in rare but important
states.
The third pattern is projection or repair. A flexible model first generates a price, surface,
or scenario, and a second step projects the output onto an admissible set. This approach
separates learning from validity enforcement. It can be useful when exact constraints are
known but hard to integrate into the model architecture. Its weakness is that repair can
distort the learned distribution and may hide systematic model failures.

The fourth pattern is adversarial testing. Instead of manually specifying all failure modes,
an adversary searches for portfolios, states, or scenarios that expose pricing errors, arbitrage,
or tail-risk weakness. This is especially natural in asset pricing, where adversarial test assets
can reveal violations of no-arbitrage conditions. It is also promising for market generators
because adversarial diagnostics can search for unrealistic stress scenarios or dependence
structures.
The fifth pattern is causal and temporal validation. This pattern is essential for LLMs
and foundation models. The model may be architecturally powerful, but its evaluation is
invalid if future information enters through pretraining, revised financial statements, data
vendors, or benchmark construction. Constraint injection here means designing the data
pipeline and evaluation protocol so that all inputs are available at the decision time. This
is less glamorous than model architecture, but it is often more important.
The design-pattern conclusion is practical: hard constraints should be preferred when
the admissible set is known and violations are unacceptable; soft penalties and projection
are useful when exact constraints are difficult to encode; adversarial testing is strongest
when failure modes are high-dimensional; and temporal validation is mandatory when text
or pretrained time-series models are used for market decisions. Strong systems are likely to
combine these patterns. An option market generator may use hard no-arbitrage parameterization, tail-risk penalties, and stress-period validation. A financial LLM for trading may
use timestamped retrieval, source attribution, leakage audits, and cost-aware backtests. A
deep asset-pricing model may use no-arbitrage losses, adversarial test assets, and portfolio
capacity constraints.
10
Research Agenda and Deployment Issues
The first priority is constraint injection. Researchers need systematic comparisons among
hard architectural constraints, soft penalties, projection methods, constrained decoding, synthetic data filtering, and post-hoc arbitrage repair. The right choice may depend on the
task. Option-surface generation may benefit from hard no-arbitrage parameterizations, while
asset-pricing models may benefit from adversarial test assets and economic loss functions. A
mature literature should report not only whether constraints improve average accuracy, but
also whether they reduce catastrophic violations under extrapolation and stress.
The second priority is benchmark construction. The benchmark gap identified above
should be addressed through public, timestamped datasets that combine market data, text,
macro variables, trading frictions, and stress-period labels. Benchmark design is especially
important because foundation models are trained on broad corpora where future information
can enter through subtle channels.
The third priority is generative validation. Market generators should be judged by more
than visual similarity or marginal distributions. They should pass tests for stylized facts,
dependence, volatility clustering, drawdowns, liquidity stress, option-surface arbitrage, and
tail-risk estimation. Risk departments and trading desks need generators that fail visibly
when they leave the domain of validity. A useful generator should communicate uncertainty
about its own validity domain rather than produce plausible but unsupported scenarios.
The fourth priority is interpretability. Constraint-aware AI should expose which eco16

nomic restrictions bind, which features drive pricing errors, which scenarios produce hedging
losses, and which textual sources influence decisions. Interpretability is not only a regulatory
preference. It is a practical requirement for diagnosing unstable strategies. In derivatives,
interpretability may mean sensitivity to strikes, maturities, and state variables. In asset pricing, it may mean factor exposures and characteristic interactions. In LLM-driven finance, it
may mean source traceability and temporal provenance.
The fifth priority is deployment research. Many models look promising in offline backtests
but degrade under latency, costs, crowding, changing regimes, and feedback from other market participants. Future studies should report live or paper-trading evidence when possible,
separate confirmatory from exploratory analysis, and document capacity constraints. In finance, deployment is part of the scientific test. A model that cannot survive implementation
frictions has not yet solved the quantitative problem it claims to solve.
11
Conclusion
Financial AI is entering a new phase.
The field is moving beyond isolated applications
of neural networks to pricing, forecasting, or sentiment analysis and toward models that
combine flexible learning with financial-mathematical structure. The central lesson of the
literature is clear. In quantitative finance, constraints are not optional decorations. Noarbitrage, martingale consistency, calibration, hedging objectives, and tail-risk diagnostics
define what it means for a model to be financially valid.
This review argues that the most promising path runs from no-arbitrage to foundation
models rather than from generic AI to finance. Deep learning can accelerate calibration, solve
high-dimensional pricing problems, learn hedging policies, discover nonlinear asset-pricing
relations, and generate market scenarios. Foundation models can integrate text and time
series across markets. But these systems become scientifically and practically credible only
when financial mathematics guides their architecture, training objectives, evaluation, and
deployment. The paper’s central conclusion can be stated in one sentence: the issue is not
whether AI can be applied to finance, but what conditions make AI financially admissible.
The next generation of quantitative finance research should therefore treat mathematical
constraints as the core design language for generative AI and deep learning.
