Architecture Over Asset Class: Market Microstructure
Across Centralised and Decentralised Trading Venues
Shozaib Khan
Independent Researcher
khan.shozaib006@gmail.com
Abstract
Much of the debate around decentralised finance has centred on whether it can replace the institutions of
traditional finance — a framing that, while intuitive, obscures the more interesting question of how markets
function once the infrastructure beneath them changes. This paper takes a different starting point, asking how
changing the underlying architecture of a market alters the behaviour of prices, the structure of liquidity, and
the validity of the analytical tools we use to study both.
The study compares four trading venue archetypes across three market layers. In spot markets, effective spread
analysis across NYSE, Binance, Uniswap v3, and Hyperliquid finds that CLOB venues — both centralised
and on-chain — produce lower all-in execution costs than Uniswap’s AMM at every measured trade size, with
the AMM’s 0.05% fee floor acting as a cost ceiling that CLOB market makers consistently undercut. NYSE
quoted spreads are competitive at standard trade sizes as a consequence of mandatory liquidity provision
under Reg NMS. In derivatives markets, perpetual funding rates on Binance and Hyperliquid converge
closely over the sample period but diverge materially from CME futures basis during stress events, reflecting
structural differences in participant composition rather than arbitrage inefficiency. A focused case study traces
Hyperliquid’s HIP framework through HIP-3’s synthetic real-world asset listings and the oracle-dependent
risks they introduce, including persistent price deviations during NYSE non-trading hours.
The final thread concerns the portability of quantitative methods. GARCH(1,1) volatility clustering, return
autocorrelation structure, and options implied volatility skew all exhibit structurally different behaviour across
TradFi and crypto asset classes. The BTC skew oscillates around zero with no structural anchor, in contrast to
the persistently negative SPX skew driven by institutional hedging demand. The central argument is that these
breakdowns are not modelling failures but structural signals, revealing genuine differences in how prices are
formed and information is incorporated across market types. Quantitative strategies require venue-specific
recalibration rather than direct cross-market transfer.
Keywords: Market microstructure, automated market makers, central limit order books,
perpetual swaps, implied volatility, impermanent loss, Hyperliquid, decentralised finance,
quantitative finance, loss-versus-rebalancing.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background
of
the
Study
Finance has always been a story about intermediaries. For most of modern history, buying a
share, taking a loan, or hedging a position required going through a broker, a bank, or a clearing
house – institutions that absorbed settlement risk, verified identities, and enforced contracts, but
also imposed fees, restricted access by geography or wealth, and operated on schedules that
suited them more than their clients.
Blockchain-based financial protocols have spent several years challenging that arrangement, and
by 2025 the scale of the challenge had become difficult to dismiss. Total value locked across
DeFi protocols reached a record $237 billion during the third quarter before settling at
approximately $116.7 billion by year-end, reflecting broader market conditions rather than any
structural reversal (DappRadar, 2025). More telling was the derivative layer: the top ten
decentralised perpetual exchanges processed $6.7 trillion in trading volume over 2025, a 346%
increase from the prior year, with the ratio of decentralised-to-centralised perpetual futures
volume tripling from 6.3% to 18.7% in a single year (NFTPlazas, 2025). Hyperliquid alone
accounted for $2.9 trillion of that volume. Alongside this, tokenised real-world assets grew
from roughly $6 billion in 2022 to over $30 billion by late 2025, with protocols such as
Hyperliquid listing synthetic equities through their HIP-3 framework – making it possible to
trade representations of NVIDIA or Tesla on-chain, 24 hours a day, without a brokerage account.
Yet the academic literature on DeFi has remained largely preoccupied with institutional
questions – whether it can replace banks, whether it poses systemic risk, whether it should be
regulated. These matter, but they treat DeFi as an institutional phenomenon rather than a market
one. What receives far less attention is how these markets actually work at the level of prices,
liquidity, and execution – and whether the analytical tools developed for traditional markets
translate meaningfully once the infrastructure beneath them changes. This paper is built around
that question.
1.2 Research
Problem
and
Objectives
The central problem here is not whether decentralised markets are better or worse than
traditional ones. It is whether changing a market’s architecture – from regulated order books
with designated market makers to permissionless automated market makers on public
blockchains – produces markets that behave differently in ways that matter analytically. A
market where prices update only at block confirmation, where liquidity providers withdraw
under stress rather than being obligated to stay, and where execution follows a deterministic
1

formula rather than a matching engine, is structurally unlike anything that preceded it. The
question is whether those differences show up in spreads, price impact, options pricing, and the
reliability of standard quantitative tools.
The paper pursues four objectives. First, it builds a comparative microstructure framework
across four venue archetypes – traditional exchanges, centralised crypto exchanges,
AMM-based DEXs, and on-chain CLOB exchanges – evaluated on execution cost, market
depth, and price impact across spot and derivatives markets. Second, it examines perpetual swap
cost-of-carry against CME futures and compares implied volatility surfaces between equity and
crypto options markets. Third, it presents Hyperliquid’s HIP-2 through HIP-4 framework as a
case study in bridging DEX transparency with CEX-grade execution, with attention to the
structural risks of HIP-3 synthetic equity listings. Fourth, it evaluates the portability of standard
quantitative measures – the Amihud ratio, GARCH volatility modelling, and autocorrelation
signals – arguing that where they degrade or fail, the breakdown reveals something genuine
about the market generating the data.
1.3 Scope
and
Significance
The study is bounded by the comparative framework described above. It does not assess the
investment merits of any protocol or take a position on DeFi regulation. The asset universe is
limited to Bitcoin and Ethereum on the crypto side and SPY and SPX options on the TradFi
side, chosen for their liquidity depth and data availability across venues. The empirical horizon
covers 2022 to 2025, capturing the FTX collapse and structural rupture of late 2022, the
recovery through 2023–2024, and the volume expansion of 2025.
The study contributes academically by applying a systematic cross-venue microstructure
comparison across all three market layers – spot, perpetuals, and options – within a single
framework. Practically, it addresses the question of strategy portability across market types,
which has received almost no systematic treatment despite its obvious relevance to quantitative
practitioners. And from a policy standpoint, understanding how on-chain derivatives markets
function, particularly as they begin listing synthetic regulated securities, matters for any
regulatory assessment of whether existing frameworks are adequate.
1.4 Paper
Roadmap
Chapter 2 reviews the microstructure, DeFi exchange comparison, and quantitative methods
literature, identifying the gap this paper addresses. Chapter 3 describes the research design, data
sources, and translation problems across structurally different venues. Chapter 4 presents the
core empirical analysis across spot markets, perpetuals, options, the Hyperliquid case study, and
quantitative measure transferability. Chapter 5 synthesises findings against the research
objectives and literature. Chapter 6 concludes with practical recommendations and directions
for future research, including the HIP-4 framework in active development at the time of writing.

CHAPTER 2
LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Market
Microstructure:
TradFi
Foundations
The academic study of how markets function at the level of individual transactions traces its
modern origins to two models that remain foundational. Kyle (1985) showed that a single
informed trader causes prices to move not because their order overwhelms supply, but because it
signals private information to the market maker reading the order flow. Glosten and Milgrom
(1985) formalised the consequence: the bid-ask spread exists because market makers face the
possibility that the counterparty knows more than they do. Together these models established
that the spread is not merely a transaction cost but an encoding of information asymmetry — a
property that, as this paper will argue, breaks down in meaningful ways once the market maker
is replaced by a deterministic pricing formula.
Amihud (2002) extended the microstructure framework into the empirical domain, proposing
the ratio of absolute daily return to dollar trading volume as a tractable proxy for price impact,
validated extensively across equity markets and now a standard input in cross-sectional asset
pricing. Regulation NMS, introduced in 2005, institutionalised many of the market quality
principles that this literature had identified, mandating best-execution routing and designated
market maker obligations. The result was a TradFi market structure in which spreads, depth,
and order routing behaviour were well understood, empirically validated, and legally governed.
2.2 DeFi
Market
Structure
and
Exchange
Comparison
Schär (2021) provided the first rigorous architectural framework for DeFi, arguing that its
defining properties — non-custodial settlement, permissionless access, and open auditability —
constituted a genuinely novel financial infrastructure. Werner et al. (2022) mapped this
ecosystem systematically, documenting protocol types, failure modes, and open research
questions.
The empirical comparison between DEX and CEX market quality was pursued most rigorously
by Barbon and Ranaldo (2022), who found that centralised exchanges provide superior liquidity
and price efficiency overall, with the primary DEX friction attributable to gas fees from
proof-of-work blockchains rather than any inherent AMM limitation. Aspris, Foley, Svec, and
Wang (2021) reached similar conclusions from a price discovery angle, treating the differences
not as temporary inefficiencies to be arbitraged away but as structural properties of the AMM
mechanism. Park (2025) situated these findings within an institutional economics framework,
contrasting the custody, authentication, and compliance functions of TradFi intermediaries with
the smart-contract equivalents in DeFi, and identifying new risk categories — oracle
3

dependency, governance capture, smart contract failure — that have no direct TradFi parallel.
What this body of work largely predates is the emergence of on-chain central limit order book
exchanges as a structurally distinct third category. Early CLOB attempts on Ethereum were
constrained by gas costs and block latency, making genuine order book matching economically
unviable. The development of purpose-built high-throughput chains — most visibly
Hyperliquid’s HyperBFT consensus layer, which achieves sub-second finality at throughput
sufficient for competitive market making — changed this constraint materially. Capponi and Jia
(2021) modelled the conditions under which AMM-based liquidity provision converges toward
order book efficiency as trading volume grows, but their framework did not anticipate a venue
architecture that combines on-chain settlement with the order book matching model of a
centralised exchange. The microstructural implications of this hybrid — particularly for spread
dynamics, market maker incentives, and the absence of designated market maker obligations —
remain largely unexamined. The trust dynamics literature provides a partial lens: Wu, Deng,
Quan, and Zhang (2024) documented a measurable shift of trading activity from CEXs to DEXs
following the FTX collapse in November 2022, suggesting that the self-custody property of
on-chain CLOBs carries genuine demand-side value beyond technical efficiency considerations.
2.3 AMMs,
Impermanent
Loss,
and
Derivative
Connections
The constant-product automated market maker, where two token reserves x and y satisfy
x·y = k, prices trades deterministically along a curve rather than through order matching. The
cost that liquidity providers bear for this simplicity is impermanent loss — the shortfall between
holding tokens in a pool versus holding them outright, which worsens as prices diverge from
their deposit-time values (Aigner & Dhaliwal, 2021). Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden, and
Zhang (2022) sharpened this concept considerably with loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR),
showing that the true cost to LPs is better captured by comparing the pool position against a
continuously rebalanced benchmark, since arbitrageurs systematically extract value in every
block where the pool lags the external market price — a dynamic that Heimbach, Wang, and
Wattenhofer (2022) confirmed empirically is priced into LP return outcomes on Uniswap v3.
Milionis et al. (2022) also showed analytically that concentrated liquidity positions replicate
short straddle payoffs, and Alexander, Deng, Feng, and Wan (2023) demonstrated that the
resulting option-like exposure can be hedged using vanilla instruments on Deribit — a result
that connects the AMM literature directly to the options market comparison this paper
undertakes in Chapter 4. On the lending side, Xu and Vadgama (2022) traced the structural
difference between TradFi credit — where collateral requirements reflect creditworthiness
assessment — and DeFi’s over-collateralised lending model, where the absence of identity and
credit scoring produces a capital-inefficient but censorship-resistant alternative.

2.4 Quantitative
Methods
and
the
Research
Gap
The application of standard quantitative tools to DeFi data has begun to produce a literature of
useful anomalies. Del Monte, de Lucio, and Urban (2025) finds that statistical regularities in
DeFi price and liquidity data diverge systematically from TradFi equivalents, identifying MEV
activity as the primary structural driver in Uniswap v3 pools.
A particularly underexplored source of these anomalies is the discrete price process imposed by
block-based execution. In TradFi, price formation is effectively continuous — orders execute as
they arrive, and standard volatility estimators such as the Garman-Klass range-based estimator
assume this continuity implicitly. In DeFi, prices only change when a transaction is confirmed
in a new block, meaning that all trades within a single block are economically simultaneous
regardless of the order in which they arrived. This introduces a structural autocorrelation into
DeFi price series that has no equivalent in equity microstructure data, and which standard
estimators are not designed to accommodate. The same block-time structure enables maximal
extractable value (MEV) — the ability of block producers to reorder, insert, or censor
transactions within a block for profit. Economically, MEV functions as a structural analog to
HFT latency arbitrage in TradFi: in both cases, a technically sophisticated participant extracts
systematic rents from regular traders by exploiting an execution advantage. The critical
difference is visibility. HFT profits in equity markets are estimated indirectly through academic
modelling; MEV extraction is recorded on-chain and publicly quantifiable, making it a rare case
where a market friction is both theoretically significant and empirically transparent.
Taken together, these contributions point to a consistent gap. The literature tends to compare
two venue types at a time, focus on a single product layer, or examine DeFi in isolation rather
than in systematic contrast with TradFi. The on-chain CLOB category — exemplified by
Hyperliquid, which by 2025 had captured roughly 80% of decentralised perpetuals volume and
begun listing synthetic equities through HIP-3 — is almost entirely absent from academic
treatment, as is any cross-market evaluation of whether standard quantitative tools retain their
validity as venue architecture changes, and what the breakdown of those tools implies about the
markets generating the data. This paper addresses both gaps within a single comparative
microstructure framework applied consistently across spot, perpetuals, and options markets.

CHAPTER 3
RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
3.1 Research
Design
and
Approach
This study adopts a comparative, quantitative research design grounded in market
microstructure theory. Rather than testing a directional hypothesis about which venue type
performs better, the analysis is structured around a consistent set of empirical metrics applied
across four venue archetypes, allowing structural differences to emerge from the data rather than
being assumed in advance. Where primary data collection from live markets is used —
specifically for Hyperliquid’s on-chain order book and HIP-3 synthetic asset prices — it is
supplemented by secondary data drawn from public APIs, academic datasets, and
exchange-published historical records. The study is non-experimental: no trading strategies are
deployed, and no interventions are made in the markets under observation. Where standard
metrics produce anomalous readings when applied to DeFi data, the paper treats those
anomalies as findings rather than data quality problems.
3.2 Research
Hypotheses
Three substantive hypotheses are advanced, each corresponding to a distinct dimension of the
analysis in Chapter 4.
H1 (Execution Cost): On-chain and centralised CLOB venues produce lower all-in execution
costs than AMM-based DEX venues across all measured trade sizes, while regulated TradFi
venues produce competitive quoted spreads at standard trade sizes due to mandatory liquidity
provision under Reg NMS.
H2 (Derivatives Integration): Perpetual funding rates across CEX and on-chain CLOB venues
converge over the sample period and track each other closely under normal conditions, but
diverge materially from CME futures basis during stress events, reflecting structural differences
in participant composition.
H3 (Metric Transferability): Standard quantitative measures — GARCH(1,1) volatility
clustering, return autocorrelation structure, and implied volatility skew — exhibit structurally
different behaviour across TradFi and crypto asset classes, such that models calibrated on
equity data cannot be applied to crypto venues without material mis-specification.
3.3 Venue
and
Asset
Selection
Four venue archetypes are selected to span the full spectrum from regulated TradFi to
permissionless on-chain markets. NYSE and NASDAQ represent the TradFi baseline, operating
6

under Regulation NMS with designated market makers and centralised clearing through the
DTCC. Binance is selected as the CEX representative on the basis of liquidity depth and data
availability. Uniswap v3 represents the AMM-DEX category, chosen for its concentrated
liquidity mechanism and the depth of its academic coverage. Hyperliquid represents the
on-chain CLOB category, selected for its dominant market share in decentralised perpetuals and
the analytical interest of its HIP framework.
Bitcoin and Ethereum are used for cross-venue crypto comparisons, as they are the only assets
with meaningful liquidity across all four venue types simultaneously. SPY and SPX options are
used on the TradFi side, chosen for liquidity depth and CBOE implied volatility data availability.
The empirical window covers January 2022 through December 2025, spanning the FTX
collapse, the 2023–2024 recovery, and the volume expansion of 2025.
3.4 Data
Sources
Equity market spread and depth data are sourced from IEX Cloud. CME futures basis data is
constructed from daily settlement prices on the CME Group public data portal, with Bitcoin
spot as the reference price from Binance’s historical OHLCV records via the Binance REST
API, from which perpetual funding rate history is also retrieved. Hyperliquid order book
snapshots, perpetual funding rates, and HIP-3 synthetic asset candle data are obtained through
Hyperliquid’s public API. Implied volatility data for CBOE equity options is sourced from the
CBOE public data portal, and Deribit BTC and ETH options volatility surface data via Deribit’s
public REST API. Uniswap v3 pool liquidity and volume data is obtained through The Graph’s
Uniswap v3 subgraph. NYSE reference prices for HIP-3 deviation analysis are sourced via the
yfinance Python library.
3.5 Analytical
Metrics
and
Translation
Across
Venues
Effective spread is the primary execution cost metric. In equity and CEX order book markets it
is calculated as twice the absolute difference between the transaction price and the prevailing
mid-quote, expressed as a percentage of the mid-quote. In Uniswap v3, there is no mid-quote:
execution cost is the sum of the pool’s LP fee tier and the price impact along the bonding curve
for a given trade size, both analytically derivable from pool state. In Hyperliquid’s CLOB the
standard order book definition applies.
The Amihud illiquidity ratio is calculated as the daily average of |rt|/Vt, where rt is the daily
return and Vt is the dollar trading volume. In AMM pools, volume is partly endogenous: the
bonding curve means a given price change implies a minimum arbitrage volume regardless of
organic demand, which is flagged as a limitation in the analysis.
GARCH(1,1) volatility estimation and autocorrelation function analysis are applied identically
across all asset series using daily close-to-close returns, at which frequency the block-time
caveat from Chapter 2 is less material than at intraday granularity. Implied volatility surface
7

comparison uses the 25-delta risk reversal as the primary skew metric and the at-the-money
term structure as the primary shape metric at standardised tenors of one week, one month, and
three months across both CBOE and Deribit datasets.
3.6 Limitations
and
Caveats
Several limitations bear noting. First, data availability for Hyperliquid is constrained to the
period following the protocol’s launch in late 2023. Second, the HIP-3 oracle deviation analysis
is based on a selected observation window given the recency of the feature. Third, causality is
not claimed in any cross-venue comparison: interpretations are grounded in theory rather than
formal causal identification. Fourth, the asset universe is deliberately narrow; findings apply to
liquid, large-capitalisation assets and do not generalise to long-tail tokens or illiquid markets.

CHAPTER 4
DATA ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION
4.1 Spot
Market
Microstructure:
A
Four-Venue
Comparison
The most direct way to observe how venue architecture affects market quality is to measure
execution cost as a function of trade size across all four venue types simultaneously. Figure ??
presents effective spread curves for NYSE (SPY), Binance BTC/USDT, Uniswap v3
USDC/ETH (0.05% fee tier), and Hyperliquid BTC across trade sizes ranging from $1,000 to
$1,000,000.
Note on NYSE spread measurement: NYSE quoted spread is computed from a live bid-ask
snapshot rather than historical tick-level data, as intraday bid-ask records are not freely available
at the required granularity; the estimate is consistent with time-weighted average quoted spreads
for SPY reported in the microstructure literature.
Three structural patterns emerge from the data, two of which contradict the naive expectation
that regulated markets produce the tightest execution costs. First, NYSE spreads are the highest
of the four venues at every trade size measured, ranging from approximately 1.1% at $1,000 to
1.5% at $1,000,000. This reflects the nature of the SPY spread measurement: the NYSE quoted
spread captures the full bid-ask cost of a single exchange transaction and excludes the tighter
effective spreads achievable through smart order routing across fragmented venues under Reg
NMS (Regulation National Market System, the US rule requiring brokers to seek the best
available price across all trading venues). Taken at face value, the figure demonstrates that
regulated market structure does not automatically confer superior execution cost for a
retail-sized single-venue transaction.
Second, the Uniswap v3 spread is entirely flat across all trade sizes at exactly its LP (liquidity
provider) fee tier of 0.05% – the price impact component along the bonding curve is negligible
at the trade sizes tested, meaning the pool’s concentrated liquidity in the USDC/ETH pair is
deep enough to absorb up to $1,000,000 without material slippage. This holds only for the most
liquid pools; long-tail token pools would show steeply rising spread curves at much smaller
trade sizes.
Third, Binance and Hyperliquid both sit well below Uniswap at every trade size measured –
starting below 0.001% at $1,000 and rising to approximately 0.03% at $1,000,000, never
crossing above Uniswap’s flat 0.05% floor. The implication is that for this pool and these trade
sizes, the CLOB architecture produces lower all-in execution cost than the AMM at every point
on the curve. Uniswap’s fee floor sets an effective cost ceiling that CLOB market makers
undercut across the full range. Hyperliquid tracks Binance closely throughout, confirming that
the on-chain CLOB matches CEX execution quality without introducing meaningful additional
9

spread friction under normal market conditions.
The Amihud illiquidity ratio computed across venues reinforces this picture but with a notable
anomaly in the AMM case. For NYSE SPY, Binance BTC, and Hyperliquid BTC, the ratio
behaves as expected – lower for more liquid assets and higher during stress periods. For the
Uniswap v3 pool, the ratio produces systematically distorted readings. This is the endogeneity
problem identified in Chapter 3: because arbitrageurs are mechanically required to trade against
the pool whenever its price deviates from the external market, a large price return always
co-occurs with a minimum volume of arbitrage trades, compressing the ratio relative to what
organic price impact alone would produce. The Amihud measure, in this context, is not
measuring illiquidity – it is measuring the efficiency of the pool’s arbitrage mechanism, which
is a structurally different quantity.
4.2 Perpetuals
and
Futures:
Cost
of
Carry
Across
Regimes
The cost-of-carry structure of a derivatives market encodes how participants price forward
exposure over time. In CME (Chicago Mercantile Exchange) Bitcoin futures, this is the
annualised basis – the premium of the futures price over spot, determined primarily by interest
rate parity and institutional demand for leveraged long exposure. In perpetual swap markets,
cost-of-carry is replaced by the funding rate: a periodic transfer between long and short
positions calibrated to keep the contract price anchored to spot. The two mechanisms serve the
same economic function through structurally different means.

4.3 Options
Markets:
Where
Black-Scholes
Begins
to
Break
The implied volatility skew – the difference in implied volatility between out-of-the-money puts
and calls at equivalent strikes – is one of the most information-rich quantities in options markets.
In TradFi equity options, the skew is persistently negative: put implied volatility exceeds call
implied volatility, reflecting the structural demand from long-only equity investors who buy
downside protection. This pattern is stable enough that it has been formalised into dedicated
indices, most notably the CBOE (Chicago Board Options Exchange) Skew Index.
Figure 4.3 presents the two series as separate panels. The SPX proxy remains consistently
negative throughout – persistently below zero and trending more negative from mid-2023
through 2025, indicating deepening put skew as equity markets priced in increasing tail risk
even while prices rose. The structural nature of this skew is clear: it does not reverse materially
even during the 2023–2024 equity bull market, which is consistent with the persistent
institutional hedging demand that anchors it.
The BTC proxy oscillates around zero with no persistent direction, which is the analytically
significant finding. Unlike the SPX series, the BTC skew does not anchor around a structural
baseline: it cycles between positive and negative on a monthly timescale, reflecting the
speculative and two-sided nature of the crypto options participant base. There is a tendency
toward positive readings around the 2024 ETF approval and subsequent all-time high, indicating
that market participants were temporarily paying more for upside exposure than downside
protection during that period. However, this is not persistent enough to constitute a structural
pattern comparable to the SPX put skew – it is a sentiment-driven oscillation rather than a
structural bid. The two series are therefore structurally different, not merely quantitatively
different: one reflects portfolio insurance demand from a long-only institutional base, the other

On-chain options protocols – Lyra and Premia – add a further layer of structural constraint
beyond the skew problem. Both rely on oracle-sourced prices for settlement, and both use
AMM-based liquidity rather than competitive market making, meaning that the implied
volatility surface is not emergent from informed order flow but is set by a pricing algorithm
updated at discrete intervals. This creates windows during which on-chain option prices can
diverge materially from Deribit, and during which the standard framework of no-arbitrage
pricing fails to hold – not because participants are irrational, but because the infrastructure
preventing arbitrage from closing the gap does not operate at sufficient speed.
The failure of Black-Scholes in this context is qualitatively different from its well-documented
failure in TradFi equity markets. In equity options, the model fails primarily because it assumes
a flat volatility surface and log-normal returns – both empirically violated, but violated in ways
that are stable and well-characterised enough to be corrected through standard adjustments such
as the volatility smile and jump-diffusion extensions. In on-chain options, the model fails for a
more fundamental reason: the underlying price itself is oracle-defined rather than emergent.
When the price an option settles against is not the price discovered by the market but the price
reported by an external feed at a scheduled update interval, the continuous-time no-arbitrage
logic that underpins Black-Scholes is structurally inapplicable. This distinction matters because
it means the gap between on-chain and off-chain options pricing cannot be closed by better
calibration alone – it requires a different pricing architecture.

4.4 Hyperliquid
and
the
HIP
Framework
Hyperliquid’s rise from launch in late 2023 to $2.9 trillion in annual perpetual trading volume
by the end of 2025 represents the most rapid accumulation of market share in the history of
decentralised derivatives. The HIP (Hyperliquid Improvement Proposal) framework is the
protocol’s mechanism for introducing new financial primitives directly on-chain. Understanding
Hyperliquid’s growth requires examining the architectural decisions that distinguish it from
prior on-chain attempts, and understanding the risks requires examining what those decisions
introduce.
HIP-1 established a native token standard on Hyperliquid’s L1 (Layer 1, meaning the base
blockchain itself rather than a secondary protocol built on top of it), enabling permissionless
deployment of financial instruments without bridging to an external chain. HIP-2 extended this
with a protocol-native liquidity seeding mechanism, allowing new spot markets to bootstrap
liquidity from the protocol itself rather than relying on external market makers. Together, these
two standards created the infrastructure for HIP-3 – the most consequential of the three – which
enables permissionless listing of synthetic spot tokens representing real-world assets, including
equities such as TSLA, AAPL, and SPY.

of the arbitrage mechanism that would normally close the gap. When NYSE is open,
arbitrageurs can trade both legs simultaneously, keeping the synthetic anchored. When NYSE is
closed, that anchor is removed and the synthetic price is set entirely by Hyperliquid participants
who may be reacting to crypto-specific news flows rather than TSLA fundamentals.
The risks compound across layers. HIP-3 tokens have no direct claim on the underlying equity –
they are purely synthetic, with value contingent on the oracle feed and the protocol’s continued
operation. In many jurisdictions they would constitute unregistered securities under existing
regulatory frameworks, a legal ambiguity that HIP-3 inherits by design. The SEC (Securities
and Exchange Commission, the primary US financial markets regulator) broad definition of a
security under the Howey Test – an investment of money in a common enterprise with an
expectation of profits from the efforts of others – is plausibly satisfied by HIP-3 equity tokens,
since holders have economic exposure to the performance of regulated companies without any
of the investor protections that equity ownership ordinarily entails. No prospectus, no voting
rights, no recourse in insolvency. For users in jurisdictions where access to foreign equity
markets is restricted by capital controls or brokerage licensing requirements, HIP-3 offers
genuine financial inclusion. For regulators in those same jurisdictions, it represents a direct
circumvention of frameworks designed to protect retail investors. Both readings are
simultaneously correct, and the tension between them is unlikely to resolve without either
regulatory intervention or protocol-level compliance mechanisms that do not yet exist. HIP-4,
currently in testing, proposes to allow arbitrary EVM (Ethereum Virtual Machine, the standard
environment for running smart contracts) contracts on HyperEVM to interact with the core L1
order book. If implemented, this would enable options and structured products written on HIP-3
equity tokens – a derivatives layer on top of a synthetic underlying, with oracle risk at every
level. The risk-compounding across layers is not additive but multiplicative, and the distance
from regulated underlying to on-chain derivative grows with each additional layer of abstraction.

not merely academic. Practitioners who deploy systematic strategies across asset classes make
implicit assumptions about the stability of the statistical properties they model. This section
examines three such properties and evaluates where those assumptions hold.

encounter a different signal environment in BTC, and a different one again in ETH, because the
autocorrelation structure of each market reflects a different underlying price formation process.
4.6 MEV
and
Fragmentation:
Structural
Externalities
Both TradFi and DeFi markets contain mechanisms through which technically sophisticated
participants systematically extract value from ordinary traders. In TradFi equity markets, this
takes the form of HFT (High-Frequency Trading) latency arbitrage: co-located servers detect
and respond to large incoming orders before they are fully executed across fragmented venues,
capturing small but consistent profits at the expense of institutional order flow. The scale of this
extraction has been estimated in the range of $5–8 billion annually across US equity markets
(Menkveld, 2013). In DeFi, the equivalent mechanism is MEV (Maximal Extractable Value) –
the ability of block producers and searchers to reorder, insert, or censor transactions within a
block for profit, effectively jumping ahead of other users to capture favourable prices. Sandwich
attacks, in which a searcher inserts buy and sell orders around a large pending swap to profit
from the price impact it creates, are the most visible form. EigenPhi data for 2024 documents
average daily MEV extraction on Ethereum exceeding $3 million, with sandwich attacks
accounting for the largest share.
The structural comparison illuminates a difference that is easy to overlook. Both mechanisms
are economically equivalent – a tax on regular participants paid to those with an execution
advantage. But in TradFi the mechanism is largely invisible, estimated through academic
modelling and largely unaddressed by regulation despite being well understood. In DeFi, every
MEV transaction is recorded on-chain, publicly attributable, and measurable in real time. The
transparency that DeFi’s architecture enforces as a general principle applies equally to its
dysfunctions. Whether that visibility translates into better outcomes for regular participants
depends on governance responses that remain, at the time of writing, unresolved.
Liquidity fragmentation presents a parallel contrast. In US equity markets, Reg NMS mandates
that brokers route orders to the venue offering the best available price, creating a legally
enforced integration of liquidity across fragmented venues. DeFi has no such mandate: the
same asset routinely trades at different prices on Uniswap, Curve, and Hyperliquid
16

simultaneously, with no obligation on any party to route to the best price. DEX aggregators
such as 1inch perform a functionally similar role – routing across venues to minimise execution
cost – but as a profit-motivated service rather than a regulatory obligation. The party who bears
the fragmentation cost in each system is different: in TradFi it is the broker who bears the
routing obligation; in DeFi it is the trader who must know to use an aggregator.

CHAPTER 5
FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION
5.1 Principal
Findings
The analysis in Chapter 4 produces five findings that collectively answer the research question:
how do market microstructure properties differ across TradFi, CEX, AMM-DEX, and on-chain
CLOB venues, and do standard quantitative tools remain valid across them?
Finding 1: Regulation does not automatically confer execution quality advantage. NYSE
spreads for SPY are the highest of the four venues at every trade size, exceeding both CEX and
on-chain CLOB venues by two orders of magnitude at retail trade sizes. This is not a failure of
the regulatory framework – Reg NMS is designed to ensure best execution across fragmented
venues, not to minimise single-venue spread – but it does challenge the assumption that TradFi
infrastructure is uniformly superior on execution cost grounds. For a retail investor executing a
single transaction on a single venue, a Binance or Hyperliquid account offers materially lower
transaction costs than the NYSE equivalent.
Finding 2: AMM and CLOB architectures occupy different cost regimes that do not simply
converge. Uniswap v3’s concentrated liquidity mechanism produces a flat spread profile
determined entirely by the LP fee tier, while CLOB venues produce near-zero spreads at small
sizes that rise with trade size. Neither architecture dominates unconditionally: the AMM’s fee
floor is a ceiling that CLOBs undercut at retail sizes but approach at institutional sizes. The
practical implication is that optimal venue selection depends on trade size and cannot be
determined by architecture alone.
Finding 3: The on-chain CLOB achieves functional parity with CEX across all measured
dimensions. Hyperliquid’s spread profile, funding rate behaviour, and volatility properties track
Binance closely throughout the sample period. This is a significant finding because prior
literature treated on-chain execution as structurally inferior to CEX execution on latency and
depth grounds. The data suggests that, at least for liquid assets and standard trade sizes, the
infrastructure gap has closed.
Finding 4: Standard quantitative tools require structural adjustment before cross-venue
application. The Amihud ratio is not a valid illiquidity measure for AMM pools due to the
endogeneity of arbitrage volume. GARCH-based volatility targeting calibrated on equity regime
persistence will systematically misprice crypto risk due to the higher frequency and different
distribution of volatility regimes. ACF-based mean-reversion signals validated on SPY will
encounter a structurally different autocorrelation environment in BTC and ETH. In each case,
the issue is not that the tool is wrong – it is that the tool embeds assumptions about market
structure that do not hold uniformly across venue types.
18

Finding 5: HIP-3 synthetic equity tokens introduce a qualitatively new category of oracle risk
with regulatory implications that existing frameworks are not designed to handle. The TSLA
deviation analysis demonstrates that the synthetic token price is structurally unanchored during
NYSE-closed hours, with observed deviations of 5–10% over multi-day windows. This is not a
temporary inefficiency that arbitrage will eliminate – it is a permanent structural feature of any
instrument that trades continuously against an underlying that does not. The regulatory question
is similarly structural: HIP-3 tokens are neither securities nor derivatives in any existing legal
category, and the Howey Test analysis in Chapter 4 suggests that this gap is a design choice
rather than an oversight.
5.2 Discussion
These findings have implications that extend beyond the specific venues and assets examined.
The most significant is that the traditional mapping between regulation and market quality is
breaking down at the execution level. Regulated venues remain superior on investor protection,
systemic risk management, and legal recourse – dimensions this paper does not measure. But on
the dimensions that directly affect trading cost and price discovery efficiency, the gap between
regulated and unregulated venues has narrowed to the point where it can no longer be assumed
without measurement.
The convergence of Hyperliquid and Binance suggests that the DeFi-CeFi (decentralised
finance–centralised finance) distinction, while architecturally real, may be less meaningful to a
trader than the CLOB-AMM distinction. Two venues can share an order book architecture and
produce near-identical execution statistics while differing fundamentally on custody model and
failure mode – and the analytical tools appropriate for each are different.
The HIP-3 finding raises a broader question about the direction of DeFi development. The
original promise of decentralised finance was disintermediation: removing rent-extracting
middlemen between savers and borrowers, buyers and sellers. HIP-3 does something different –
it recreates exposure to regulated assets inside an unregulated wrapper, preserving returns while
stripping the protections regulation attaches to them. Whether this constitutes genuine financial
innovation or regulatory arbitrage depends on the observer’s prior, but the data makes the
mechanism precise: the synthetic’s price is anchored to the underlying only when arbitrage is
mechanically possible, and free to diverge whenever it is not.
The quantitative tools finding points toward a practical agenda. The problem is not that
researchers and practitioners use the wrong models – it is that they use correct models with
incorrect domain assumptions. GARCH is a valid volatility model; the issue is calibrating its
persistence parameter on equity data and applying it to crypto. The Amihud ratio is a valid
illiquidity measure; the issue is applying it to a market where volume is partly mechanically
generated rather than organic. Developing domain-adjusted versions of standard tools – rather
than replacing them entirely – is a more tractable research programme.

CHAPTER 6
CONCLUSIONS
6.1 Summary
This paper set out to examine whether DeFi markets have matured to the point where they can
be evaluated on the same microstructure terms as their TradFi counterparts, and whether the
quantitative tools developed for traditional markets transfer cleanly across the divide. The
answer to both questions is qualified: yes, with important structural caveats that have practical
consequences for traders, researchers, and regulators alike.
Across four venue types – NYSE, Binance CEX, Uniswap v3 AMM, and Hyperliquid on-chain
CLOB – the data reveals that execution cost is not correlated with regulatory status in the
direction most observers would expect. The most regulated venue is the most expensive at every
trade size. The least regulated – an on-chain order book with no central operator – matches the
CEX on spread, funding rate behaviour, and volatility properties. This does not mean regulation
is irrelevant; it means that the costs regulation imposes on execution quality are real and
measurable, and that the protections it provides operate on dimensions this paper does not
capture: legal recourse, custody safety, and systemic risk containment.
The AMM-CLOB comparison produces a more nuanced finding. Neither architecture
dominates unconditionally. AMMs provide a predictable, size-invariant fee structure that traders
can model precisely; CLOBs provide near-zero retail spreads that rise with size. The choice
between them is a function of trade size and whether the trader values cost predictability over
cost minimisation.
The HIP framework analysis identifies a new category of financial instrument that sits outside
existing regulatory definitions and introduces structural risks – oracle dependency, after-hours
price dislocation, and layered synthetic exposure – that are not adequately captured by
frameworks developed for either traditional derivatives or earlier DeFi primitives.
6.2 Limitations
Several limitations constrain the generalisability of these findings. First, the microstructure data
is a snapshot: order book depth and spread measurements reflect a single point in time and may
not represent average conditions across the full sample period. Second, the HIP-3 analysis is
limited to a single asset (TSLA) over a 45-day window, which is insufficient to characterise the
full distribution of oracle deviations across different market conditions. Third, the paper does
not measure the investor protection dimensions of regulation – recourse mechanisms, disclosure
requirements, and systemic oversight – which may justify execution cost differences that spread
data alone cannot explain.
20

6.3 Future
Research
Three directions follow directly from the findings. First, a longitudinal spread study across
multiple assets and time periods would determine whether Hyperliquid’s execution parity with
Binance is persistent or a feature of current market structure. Second, a systematic analysis of
HIP-3 oracle deviations across multiple assets and conditions – particularly around corporate
events such as earnings releases, which occur outside NYSE hours – would quantify the tail risk
that the TSLA window analysis suggests is material. Third, developing domain-adjusted
versions of the Amihud ratio and GARCH models that account for structural properties of
AMM and on-chain CLOB markets would produce more reliable tools for the growing
community of researchers and practitioners working across venue types.
The broader implication is that the boundary between TradFi and DeFi is becoming a productive
object of study rather than a dividing line between two separate literatures. The markets are
converging faster than the analytical frameworks used to study them, and closing that gap is
both tractable and overdue.
