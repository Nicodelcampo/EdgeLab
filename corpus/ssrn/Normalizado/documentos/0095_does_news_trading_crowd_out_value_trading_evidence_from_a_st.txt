Abstract
Technology produces data streams at lower cost, which intensifies “news” trading.
Does this trading on value changes crowd out trading on value levels, i.e., value
trading? And, what is the net effect on liquidity and market efficiency? We propose a
Kyle (1985)-type model to study these questions, both theoretically and empirically.
The latter is possible, because closed-form solutions admit structural estimation.
Implementing it on a 2013-2023 proprietary sample covering trades in the EURO
STOXX 50 Index Futures reveals how liquidity, market efficiency, and the profit of
both types of informed trading changed over time, and why.

Does News Trading Crowd Out Value Trading?
Evidence from a Structural Estimation
Abstract
Technology produces data streams at lower cost, which intensifies “news” trading.
Does this trading on value changes crowd out trading on value levels, i.e., value
trading? And, what is the net effect on liquidity and market efficiency? We propose a
Kyle (1985)-type model to study these questions, both theoretically and empirically.
The latter is possible, because closed-form solutions admit structural estimation.
Implementing it on a 2013-2023 proprietary sample covering trades in the EURO
STOXX 50 Index Futures reveals how liquidity, market efficiency, and the profit of
both types of informed trading changed over time, and why.

Introduction
The information environment of modern financial markets is characterized by a fundamental
dichotomy in how agents process signals. While traditional models of information asymmetry
focus on value level information – private knowledge regarding the fundamental value of
an asset – the rise of high-frequency trading has shifted focus toward “news traders” who
specialize in value changes. These agents profit from a speed advantage that allows them
to trade on news before the market can adjust. While the existing literature has analyzed
these types in isolation, the equilibrium dynamics that emerges when both types coexist and
compete remain largely unexplored. This paper proposes a model that incorporates both
traditional value traders and news traders. The model is solved analytically and structurally
estimated using proprietary trade data on EURO STOXX 50 Index Futures trading.
The literature on value trading.
The theoretical foundation for trading on value levels is
rooted in the seminal work of Kyle (1985), where a value trader privately observes the value of
an asset at the end of a trading period consisting of multiple rounds. He strategically trades
on this information over time, fully cognizant of the information leaking to the rest of the
market in each trade he engages in. The value trader could be thought of as privately knowing
the full-information end-of-day price of an asset, and stealthily trades towards it. This type
of model typically assumes that the private information relates to a static fundamental value,
focusing on how prices gradually converge to this level. It abstracts from the rapid price
adjustments triggered by the continuous arrival of public news that may be intercepted by
faster participants.
The literature on news trading.
In contrast, a more recent literature focuses on traders
with a speed advantage: high-frequency traders (HFTs).1 These traders should be thought of
1Survey articles include Jones (2013), O’Hara (2015), and Menkveld (2016).
1

as machines that are extremely fast in processing information flows and acting on it. They,
therefore, are modern news traders in the sense that they can quickly identify value changes,
mostly ignorant of value levels. This literature is typically exclusively focused on news trading
and ignores value trading. One exception is Foucault, Hombert and Roşu (2016) (FHR16)
who focus on both value trading and news trading. They compare a model where a value
trader cannot engage in news trading (slow model) to one where he can (fast model). The
value trader is, therefore, either fast or slow relative to the dealer. When fast, the speculator
observes a signal correlated with incoming news and trades on the anticipated price reaction,
before the dealer can update his quotes.
Synthesis and contribution.
While FHR16 provides a powerful comparison of how a
single agent’s behavior changes when he is given speed, it does not address the interaction
between a (slow) value trader and a (fast) news trader operating in the same market. We
believe such distinction makes sense if the objective is to model real-world markets. Some
agents specialize in fundamental research to identify value levels, such as, for example,
financial analysts or asset managers. Others specialize in quickly analyzing value changes,
proprietary traders parsing the news, which is broadly defined as the continuous flow of
value-relevant information (e.g., company news, order-book events, or social media posts).
This paper bridges the two frameworks by developing and estimating a model where a
level-informed value trader and a change-informed news trader coexist in equilibrium. The
proposed model builds on FHR16, with the distinction that value trading and news trading
are assigned to two distinct types. By explicitly modeling these two types separately, we move
beyond fast vs. slow comparisons and, instead, analyze the strategic interaction between the
two types of informed agents. More specifically, the model features a finite trading period
where trading is continuous. Similar to Kyle (1985), at the start, the value trader has private
information on the value level. During the period, this value changes continuously, with a
2

news trader observing these changes fast, but imperfectly. The value trader observes these
changes slow, but perfectly. This setting is a straightforward extrapolation of FHR16 to our
setting with the two different types of informed traders.
The model turns out to be tractable and delivers a unique linear equilibrium that is
used to both generate theoretical insights and structurally estimate the model parameters.
Starting with the theoretical insights, we ask the following questions. First, to what extent
does news trading crowd out value trading? Second, how does the presence of both types
of informed traders affect the informational efficiency of the market? Finally, structural
estimation enables us to study how the model parameters evolve for ten years of trading
EURO STOXX 50 Index Futures. It, therefore, yields insight in the strength of these effects
for an empirically relevant part of the parameter space.
Results.
The equilibrium expressions show that news trading does crowd out value trading.
While this might not be all that surprising, the effect it has on market liquidity is nontrivial.
One might say that information being moved from one type of informed trader to another
should not affect liquidity, because the dealer continues to be experience adverse-selection
cost on this information. This, however, is not true, because who trades on this information
matters. News traders are unable to trade patiently on information across multiple rounds,
simply because dealers soon observe the same news. Value traders, however, are under no such
pressure and thus can create up to twice as high profits on the same information. Therefore,
if news becomes more precise, then the information moving from the value trader to the news
trader generates less profit. This makes the zero-profit dealer charge a lower price impact,
because his adverse-selection cost is reduced. Market liquidity improves as a result.
In striking contrast to the market liquidity result, news trading crowding out value trading
does not affect market efficiency. More precisely, define informational inefficiency as the
variance of the wedge between the dealer quote and the true value, which we will refer to
3

as “pricing error.” In equilibrium, the variance of this pricing error declines linearly towards
zero at the end of the trading session. This result does not depend on how precise the news
is throughout the day and, therefore, is invariant to the relative presence of both types of
informed trading. In particular, for high levels of precision, the crowding out of value traders
by news traders trading on more precise news does not affect informational efficiency.
The equilibrium dynamics throughout the trading session can be represented by a Gaussian
state space model and can, therefore, be estimated with maximum likelihood. The likelihood
is computed using the Kalman filter. To take the model to the data, one needs multiple
trading sessions with for each session, trade prices and aggregate net volumes througout the
trading session, and net volume of the news trader. The identification of news trading makes
standard off-the-shelf datasets, such as US TAQ data, unfit for estimation.
The model is estimated based on a proprietary dataset made available by Deutsche
Börse. It contains all trades in EURO STOXX 50 Index Futures from 2013 through 2023. A
particularly attractive feature of this sample is that, for each trade, both sides to the trade are
flagged as either agency or principal. Using this information, we interpret principal trading
as news trading. The idea is that exchange members are close to the market and likely are
the first to observe news to trade on it. Agency trades, on the other hand, are assumed to
capture both value trading and noise trading. Each day in the sample is considered a trading
session for which intraday trades prices and net volumes are observed. If one considers these
interpretations reasonable, then the structural estimation yields the following insights. The
estimation was done year by year to study the evolution of trading over the sample period.
The model estimates yield the following results. First, in the 2010s, news became steadily
more precise from revealing 35.3% of the value change in 2013, the start of the sample, to
revealing 46.8% of it in 2018. Then it declined to 29.9% at the end of the sample in 2023.2

day. The standard deviation of this part is about 23.26 basis points (bps). The standard
deviation of the information value traders have at the start of the day is 1.29 bps.
Third, the gross profit of value traders far exceeds the profit of news traders. This might
be surprising at first, because value traders start the day with relatively little information.
But, they learn the value changes throughout the day perfectly, whereas news traders only
observe these changes with noise, i.e., the news, and can trade on them only for a brief period,
because the news gets learned by the dealer immediately after the news trader traded on
it. The average gross profit for news traders is =C670,000 per day, whereas it is =C1,700,000
per day for value traders. Although the model does not feature trading fees, these fees are
substantial, but do not turn gross profits into losses. They are about 18% of gross profit for
news traders and about 4% for value traders.
Related literature.
In their seminal paper on stock return variance, French and Roll
(1986) study why asset prices are more volatile during trading hours relative to nontrading
hours. They argue that it could be public information that tends to arrive during trading
hours, private information of investors who trade on it during these hours, or pricing errors.
Using exchange holidays as an instrument in their analysis, they conclude that “private
information is the principle factor behind high trading-time variances.” This spurred an
enormous literature on informed trading in financial markets.
Follow-up analysis reveals another striking stylized fact: The variance of open-to-open
5

(O-O) returns is significantly larger than the variance of close-to-close (C-C) returns. Amihud
and Mendelson (1987) find a difference of 20% for Dow Jones stocks. Further analysis leads
them to conclude that at the opening prices exhibit higher residual noise. Stoll and Whaley
(1990) confirm these findings for a larger sample of NYSE stocks. The index returns in
our sample exhibit a 2% higher variance in O-O vs. C-C returns. This suggests that while
the effect is still there, it is weaker for index returns. Lu, Malliaris and Qin (2023) explain
these findings by the cream-skimming risk of that fast (informed) arbitrageurs impose on
liquidity suppliers in the morning, thus making liquidity demanders pay large (transitory)
price pressure early in the trading session. Our model is, in the tradition of Kyle (1985),
focused on endogenous information revelation. The information structure thus does not
exogenously assume informed fast traders to be active in the morning. Importantly, the
stylized facts of noisy opening prices and efficient closing prices are consistent with our
equilibrium result that some information slowly enters prices by value traders slowly trading
on it throughout the day.3
Another literature that our study relates focuses on asset pricing. Recent evidence suggests
that value trading is waning, which explains the general unease informationational efficiency.
Barras, Scaillet and Wermers (2010, Fig. 4) show that the average alpha of US mutual funds
declined from 1989 through 2006. Using more recent data, Antoniewicz et al. (2025, Fig. 2.5)
show that for mutual funds and exchange traded funds (ETFs), the share of actively managed
funds steadily declined from 81% in 2010 to 49% in 2024. Even the actively managed funds
seems to become more “algorithmic.” Birru et al. (2024) show that recommendations by
analysts who use Quants are more aware of well-known anomalies. In 2017, Thomas Jr (2017)

of informed trading. We speculate that this the case because the model does not have a
fixed (rental) cost of operating as an value trader or a news trader. If it feature such cost,
then trivially, the value trader would disappear from the market if his profitability becomes
negative. This would make prices less efficient as value trader information no longer enters
market prices.

Empirics
Let us convert the equilibrium dynamics to a state space form that approximates it. To
discretize the dynamics, we will estimate the model where the time increments are small.
16

application, we approximate the dynamics implied by the model by picking “dt” to be one
minute. This trades off a short enough periodicity to capture news trading, but not too short
to keep the likelihood maximization numerically feasible.

3.4
Data
The model will be estimated for daily samples of trading in EURO STOXX 50 Index Futures
(FESX). Each sample consists of trades, where each trade record contains:
• The match price, which is the locked-in index value. It is multiplied by 10 to get to
contract value, because each point change in the index corresponds to =C10.
• Whether the buyer or the seller initiated the transaction and, therefore, was the liquidity
demander in the trade. We refer to buyer as the agent who enters the long side of the
futures contract. The seller enters the short side.
21

• Whether the buyer is an exchange member trading engages in a principal or an agency
trade. A principal trade refers to a trade for his own account whereas an agency trade
is a trade for a client.
• Whether the seller engages in a principal or agency trade.
We construct the sample to estimate the model as follows. For each day, we select the contract
with the highest volume, often the nearest-to-maturity contract. Then, for each interval, we
sign each trade size according to the initiating side. Buyer-initiated trades get a positive sign
and seller initiated trades get a negative sign. We sum these signed trade sizes to obtain dyt
for the interval. We assume that intermediaries are the news traders and, therefore, define xN
t
as the sum of all signed trade sizes where the initiator was an exchange member trading for
his own account. Finally, we set pt+dt to be the last trade price in the interval. All variables
are set to non-available if there is no trade in the interval. The Kalman filter used in the
estimation allows for non-availables, which is one of the benefits of the approach.
A trading day spans the time when most European equity markets open and close: 9:00
until 17:30 Central European Time (CET). Figure IA.1 in the Internet Appendix illustrates
that this period captures most trading volume in EURO STOXX 50 Index Futures. The figure
further shows that volume in this period is U-shaped. This is the result of the information
flow following a transaction clock instead of the wall clock (Ané and Geman, 2000). To run
the analysis on a transaction clock, we create new timestamps as follows. We use the existing
timestamps to sort all transactions on a particular a day, then replace these timestamps by
new timestamps that are evenly spaced in the trading day. The aggregation to one-minute
intervals thus aggregates across a constant number of transactions each day. Finally, to avoid
idiosyncrasies associated with the opening and closing of the market, the first and last five
minutes are removed before creating the new timestamps.

Continuations or reversals in net volume.
The analysis on the relative strength
of continuations and reversals in net volume is based on variance ratios. The 15-minute
periodicity value trader variance ratio, for example, measures the variance of value trader net
volume at two periodicities. The numerator consists of the variance of flow at a 15-minute
periodicity, divided by 15. The denominator consists of the variance of one-minute flow. If
net volume is white noise, the variance ratio is expected to be one. If, however, net-volume
continuations (i.e., buy after buy, sell after sell) dominate reversals (i.e., buy after sell, sell
after buy), then the variance ratio exceeds one. This is most easily seen by developing the
variance of 15-minute flow in terms of one-minute increments as follows:

positive autocovariances, and reversals, i.e., negative autocovariances. If the second sum is
positive, then continuations dominate reversals. If it is negative, reversals dominate. It is
zero for serially uncorrelated net volume, i.e., white noise. It is for this reason that variance
ratios provide a useful characterization of trading.

Figure 6 plots the variance ratios for both types of net volume for various periodicities of
the numerator. The one-minute periodicity trivially yields a variance ratio of one, because the
periodicities of the numerator and denominator coincide. For the news trader, the variance
ratio drops by about 10 percent for the five-minute periodicity, which suggests that there is
reversion for news traders within five minutes. One explanation could be that news traders
quickly offloads his inventory within this time frame if these traders are sufficiently risk
averse (Roşu, 2019). For higher periodicities, the variance ratio continues to drop but only

very mildly. Overall, the pattern for news trading is consistent with the conjecture that the
information of news traders is short-lived.
The variance ratio of (noisy) value trader net volume increases strongly in numerator
periodicity, from the baseline value of one for the one-minute periodicity to more than two
for the 15-minute periodicity. This is consistent with strategic trading by value traders
who cautiously trade on their long-lived signal. This is consistent with continuations being
a dominant feature of their net volume dynamics. The variance ratios continuing to rise
with numerator periodicity is consistent with their trading being very strategically slow and
stretching across tens of minutes.10
4
Conclusion
The analysis of a model along with an estimation of its parameters shed light on the overarching
question: Does news trading crowd out value trading? The theoretical results show that more
precise news makes news traders trade more intensively on their signal, which, in turn, makes
value traders trade less intensively on their signal. Thus, news trading does crowd out value
trading. The results further show that this does not affect the speed at which information is
revealed in the market, but it does affect market liquidity, which improves.
Estimation of the model based on proprietary EURO STOXX 50 Index Futures trade
data, yields several insights. First, both value trading and news trading are profitable, also
net of trading fees. Value trading, however, is almost three times as profitable as news
trading. Second, the level of precision in news fluctuates substantially in the sample. News
reveals 35.3% of the value change at the start of the sample, which increases to 46.8% in the
middle of it, and declines to 29.9% at the end of it. This decline along with a decline in noise
10These variance ratios are hard to extend beyond the 15-minute numerator, because the number of
observations becomes too low. In our sample, there are 8 × 4 = 32 complete 15-minute in a trading day,
which starts at 9:05 and ends at 17:25.
29

trading explains the substantial decline in liquidity by more than 50% in the second half of
the sample.
One additional take-away from our analysis is information-share analysis is critically
contingent on the periodicity of the analysis. The information that value traders trade is
simply more visible at longer periodicities, whereas news-trader information is more visible
at shorter periodicities. This should inform future empirical analysis of information shares.
By design, our analysis focuses on the intensive margin of value trading. If there were
a fixed (rental) cost to an value trader running his operation, then the results are likely to
change. If value trader rents drop below this level, then he might leave the market and the
private information he generated will no longer enter market prices. We leave this for future
research.
