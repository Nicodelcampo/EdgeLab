Abstract
We consider in detail how liquidity in a limit order book (LOB) is replenished
immediately after the spread widens — the first stage of a price change. To this
end, we use a highly granular dataset which we acquire directly from the blockchain
of the blockchain-based exchange Hyperliquid. We analyze the entire message flow
(approximately 4.5 billion messages over one month) relating to the Bitcoin perpetual
contract, which has an average daily turnover of approximately $3 billion.
We reveal the “neutrinos” of the order book: a massive, pervasive, yet weakly
interacting flow of post-only limit orders—most of which are mechanically rejected
because they would cross the spread—accounting for approximately 67% of all
messages sent to the exchange. Such orders, when rejected (which is the vast majority
of cases) do not appear in any standard market data feed and are effectively invisible
to all traders except the submitter. Nevertheless, they are submitted in vast quantities
and almost continuously, by a small group of traders.
We argue that these traders are implementing a strategy to capture favourable
queue priority (as part of market-making). They bombard the exchange with orders,
most of which are rejected, but a small fraction arrive immediately after a spreadwidening event, and secure the desired queue position. We present detailed evidence
that the traders are submitting in an extraordinarily accurate directional manner,
presumably in response to private and common signals, and we show how the strategy
has an embedded risk management which exploits the exchange’s own operational
controls. We also show that the strategy accounts for a large proportion of “flickering

may help detect whether the strategy is being used in markets where data on rejected

1. Introduction
The study of market microstructure has long adhered to a principle we might
call ‘Sola Scriptura’ (“by the book alone"). The focus, from foundational models
[21, 15] to contemporary empirical studies [18, 8], has been almost exclusively on
the observable events recorded in the Limit Order Book (LOB): accepted orders,
cancellations, and trades. This perspective is natural, and it has been enormously
productive, providing evidential underpinnings for many aspects of our understanding
of the roles played by private and public information in price formation and discovery.
The perspective has the great virtue of minimising reliance on suppositions about
unobservable elements such as trader intentions or the precise nature of signals.
However, perspectives should evolve with the evolution of available data. Although
they were a huge step forward when they first became widely accessible, standard
market data feeds—even Level 3 feeds—do not include rejected orders. They report
what enters the book, not what bounces off the matching engine and vanishes. The
phenomena we document in this paper were therefore structurally unobservable until
the advent of blockchain-based exchanges like Hyperliquid, where the full message
stream can be reconstructed from on-chain records. When richer data become available,
two things follow. First, phenomena invisible to earlier studies can be documented
directly. Second—and perhaps more consequentially—established findings can be
revisited. Some will be confirmed; others may require qualification. Explanations
that seemed plausible given the old data may now appear incomplete.
In this paper, we exploit a dataset that is, at present, uniquely granular: it
captures every message submitted to the exchange, including those rejected by the
matching engine. This level of detail has not been available for standard exchanges;
it becomes possible here because Hyperliquid operates a blockchain-based matching
engine whose full message stream can be reconstructed from on-chain records (see
Appendix A for details on the exchange architecture).
What this data reveals is striking. We identify the ‘neutrinos’ of the order book:
a massive, pervasive, yet weakly interacting flow of rejected ‘post-only’ orders. Like
their namesakes in physics, these messages are ubiquitous and vastly numerous in
2

aggregate, yet almost all of them are invisible in standard data feeds—they pass
through the matching engine without altering the observable state of the book in any
way.
The scale of this “neutrino” flow is staggering. In our sample of about 4.5 billion
messages, rejected orders account for approximately 89% of all liquidity-providing
submissions and dwarf the visible liquidity resting in the LOB by more than two
orders of magnitude. This is not random noise or a technical artifact; it is the
dominant form of interaction with the exchange.
We present strong evidence to support the hypothesis that
this hidden flow stems almost entirely from a single, pervasive strategy executed by
a specialized cohort of high-frequency traders (HFTs) whose goal is to optimise the
queue position of their orders; we term this the Queue Priority Strategy Hypothesis
(QPSH).
The QPS is driven by the immense value of queue priority, namely securing the
first-in-line (FIL) position at a new price [23, 25]. We show that HFTs engage in
relentless, anticipatory “spamming”, flooding the market with a constant rain of orders
structured as what we term submission bursts—volleys of simultaneous post-only
submissions targeting multiple aggressive price levels. This flow is directional: the
net direction of rejected orders in the 100 ms before each price change correctly
predicts the direction of that change 91.3% of the time. This evidence suggests that
traders are not firing blindly; they are acting on signals that forecast short-term price
movements with remarkable accuracy. The objective is to exploit latency: traders
aim for their orders to be “in-flight” precisely when the price changes, usually via
liquidity removal through a taker order and/or maker cancellation. If the QPS orders
arrive even milliseconds too early, they are mechanically rejected (generating the
invisible component of the “neutrino flow”). The small proportion of QPS orders that
arrive at the optimal time often secure the coveted first-in-line (FIL) queue priority.
We proceed to examine the implications of the strategy.
First, it challenges our understanding of how the spread typically refills after it is
temporarily widened (by taker orders, cancellations, or both). The conventional view
is reactive: the spread widens, traders observe this, and then react to competitively
establish the new bid and ask prices[21, 14, 22]. We show instead that spread refilling
is primarily anticipatory. The liquidity that establishes the new best price is typically
already “in-flight” before the price change even occurs, implying that the effective
latency of replenishment is close to zero.
Second, the QPSH provides a parsimonious explanation for “flickering orders”
(orders cancelled almost immediately after submission; we operationalize this as
cancellation within 500 ms). Flickering has been documented as a distinct, puzzling
3

phenomenon [18]. We show it is, to a large extent, a risk-management byproduct
of the QPSH. When a submission burst succeeds, the trader instantly cancels the
unwanted, off-price orders to avoid multi-fill risk. This rapid “cleanup” accounts for
roughly 30% of the flickering activity in our dataset. We return to flickering orders in
Section 3.6.
Returning to our opening point, that previously unavailable data allows us to
extend the range of our interpretation, our findings allow us to infer more about
the hidden “private" world that underlies the observable universe of the LOB. In
particular, our evidence sheds light on the details of the liquidity-provision side of
price formation, and how market makers submit accurate anticipatory orders well
before liquidity removal changes the price. Our findings suggest that FIL makers
are primarily signal-driven in their submissions, whether those signals be private or
public, and that QPS traders are remarkably effective in replenishing the LOB as
soon as a taker order (perhaps driven by a stronger signal) has been filled.
1.1. Related Literature
This study connects to several strands of the market microstructure literature.
HFT Strategies and the Speed Race. A large literature examines what high-frequency
traders actually do. [20] show algorithmic trading improves liquidity; [8] document
HFTs’ role in price discovery. [10] argue that limit order books generate designinduced latency-arbitrage rents, spurring a socially wasteful “arms race” for speed; [3]
provide definitive empirical evidence of this race. Our findings complement this work
by documenting a strategy that operates largely outside the visible LOB, and that is
less about reacting faster to known events and more about positioning preemptively
for anticipated events. The massive flow of rejections we document is the byproduct
of this anticipatory mechanism.
The Value of Queue Priority. The economic value of queue position is well established.
[13] model the limit order book as a market for liquidity in which queue position
determines execution probability.
[23] show that queue position accounts for a
substantial fraction of limit order profitability, particularly in large-tick stocks. [25]
demonstrate that time-priority in limit order books allocates rents to faster traders
who exploit their speed advantage to secure superior queue positions—crowding out
slower participants. We take the value of queue priority as given and ask a different
question: how do traders attempt to capture it? The answer, we argue, is relentless
anticipatory spamming—a strategy that leaves behind a massive trail of rejected
orders.
4

Flickering Orders and Ephemeral Liquidity. The phenomenon of “flickering” or “fleeting” orders—rapid submissions and cancellations—has generated debate. [18] document that a large fraction of limit orders are cancelled within seconds. [17] shows
that quotes are more volatile at high frequencies than fundamentals would imply,
and frames this as a puzzle. [4] provide a theoretical model in which fleeting orders
arise from repeated mixed-strategy equilibria. Others view flickering as potentially
manipulative [12] or as legitimate risk management [5]; [6] show that flickering quotes
on European equities are positively correlated with subsequent price changes, are
concentrated among a handful of high-frequency firms, and—notably—are directional,
with buy-side flickering clustering after price rises and sell-side after declines; and [24]
provide an empirical characterization and situate flickering within an ongoing debate
over mechanism and intent, arguing that the evidence is more consistent with benign
trading activity rather than with manipulation. We offer a parsimonious mechanism:
flickering is predominantly the “cleanup” phase of an anticipatory strategy whose objective is to land orders with favourable queue position. It arises endogenously as part
of the strategy’s risk management. The concentration and directionality documented
by [6] are consistent with the empirical signatures implied by this mechanism.
[26] documents that immediately after a price change, displayed depth at the new
best quote in the direction of the move jumps up and then declines sharply within
a short time interval, reflecting submissions of new maker orders followed by rapid
cancellations at that same new best price. He proposes a queueing-based explanation:
after a spread-widening event, market makers submit multiple orders at the new best
price because uncertainty arising from latency means they do not know their queue
position ex ante; once orders are accepted and queue position is revealed (or can
be inferred), they cancel the orders with inferior priority. Our mechanism shares
the same economic force—queue position is valuable and uncertain—but differs in
timing and observability. We instead suggest that a substantial portion of the race
for priority occurs through anticipatory, in-flight submission attempts that precede
the observable price change.
Predictive Order Flow. Numerous studies document that order flow predicts subsequent price changes [19, 11, 9]. In a companion paper, we show this predictive
relationship extends even to rejected orders—messages that never touch the book.
The QPSH explains why: the predictive power does not reside in the order itself
(which, if rejected, has no causal effect on the book). It resides in the information that
motivated the trader to submit it. Rejected orders are informative precisely because
they are a byproduct of informed traders anticipating imminent price changes.
5

2. Data
Our data are drawn from the Hyperliquid exchange, a highly liquid cryptocurrency
perpetual futures marketplace. Our sample focuses on the BTC-USD perpetual
futures contract, the most actively traded instrument on the venue, with an average
daily trading volume of approximately $3 billion during our sample period. The
sample spans July 1st to July 29th, 2025, encompassing approximately one month of
continuous trading.
The dataset is uniquely granular.
Unlike standard Level 2 or Level 3 data
feeds which report only accepted orders and trades, our data include every message
submitted to the matching engine, including those that are rejected. The result is a
dataset of approximately 4.5 billion messages. The average message rate is roughly
112,000 per minute—about 1,860 per second, or one message every 0.54 ms.
Each message in our dataset is tagged with a unique trader identifier and, where
applicable, a client order ID (‘cloid’) that traders use to track their own submissions.
Each message carries a timestamp assigned by the matching engine at the moment
of processing. These timestamps reflect when the exchange internally recorded each
event—not when the trader submitted the message. Further technical details regarding
the construction of this dataset will be provided in a forthcoming companion paper,
which will also make the data publicly available to the academic community.
Crucially for our analysis, we observe the full stream of rejected “post-only"
orders. In this market, traders frequently use the “post-only" instruction to ensure
they provide liquidity (maker) rather than take it. If a “post-only" order is submitted
at a price that would cross the spread (e.g., a buy order priced at or above the
best ask), the matching engine rejects it with the error BadAloPxRejected. These
rejections are purely mechanical events: they leave no footprint in the order book,
do not execute, and are not disseminated via standard market data feeds used for
trading. However, our data captures this “invisible” flow, allowing us to analyze it
ex-post as a distinct class of market activity, although it is not observable to market
participants in real time.
2.1. Order Lifecycle and Event Taxonomy
To analyze our comprehensive message flow, we identify four distinct categories of
messages based on outcome and longevity.
Rejected Orders. Except for a small number of other rejections noted below, these
are “post-only” submissions that are mechanically rejected by the matching engine
because they would cross the spread if posted upon arrival. These events are central
to our study: they do not alter the LOB state, are not disseminated in standard
market data feeds, and are thus invisible to other participants.
6

Successful Placements (Accepted Orders). This category encompasses all limit order
submissions—whether flagged as ‘post-only’ or not—that successfully enter the limit
order book. This occurs when a ‘post-only’ order does not cross the spread, or when
a standard limit order is priced passively relative to the current market. These orders
add liquidity and are visible to all market participants via the standard data feeds.
Cancellations. A cancellation message attempts to remove an order from the LOB
prior to execution. Successful cancellations are visible events that reduce liquidity;
failed attempts (e.g., when the order was already filled or previously cancelled) leave
no trace in the book. Our message-level statistics include both. Because traders
frequently submit multiple cancellation requests for the same order—a redundancy
tactic to maximize the probability of successful cancellation in latency-sensitive
situations—cancellation messages outnumber accepted orders in our sample.
Other Order Outcomes. This residual category captures all remaining message types.
It includes: (i) taker orders—market orders, immediate-or-cancel (IOC) orders, and
marketable limit orders that cross the spread and execute immediately upon arrival;
(ii) other order types such as TWAP execution orders offered by the exchange; and
(iii) other rejections—submissions rejected for reasons unrelated to price, such as ratelimit violations, insufficient margin, self-trade prevention, or invalid order parameters.
Together, these account for approximately 4.3% of total message traffic and are
peripheral to our analysis.
The taxonomy above is purely mechanical. From it, we derive a subset of orders
that has attracted recent attention in the literature [6]:
Flickering Orders. We identify a specific subset of observed events characterized by
their transience. Following [18], we define a flickering order as an accepted order
that is subsequently cancelled within 500 ms of submission. 1 These events represent
liquidity that exists in the book only fleetingly.
Note that flickering is a derived category based on order longevity, not a fundamental message type. An order is classified as flickering only after observing its
subsequent cancellation; at the moment of submission, it is indistinguishable from
any other accepted order.
1In the literature, thresholds vary from sub-second to several seconds. We select 500 ms to
capture the high-frequency nature of the strategy we describe, though our results are robust to
thresholds of 100 ms or 1s.
7

2.4. Event Sequencing and Linkage
Next, we observe that rejections are rarely isolated and instead occur in structured,
sequential patterns. Orders that end up being rejected are typically not submitted
in isolation, but in high-frequency batches from individual traders. We define a
“Submission Burst” as any group of at least five “post-only" submissions from a
single trader that meet the following criteria:
• They are submitted in the same millisecond.
• They share the same 15-character ‘cloid’ prefix.3
• They have identical order sizes and target the same side of the book (though
potentially different price levels).
Note that the definition does not filter for the order’s outcome: a priori, orders
from a submission burst can experience any fate, whether it be a rejection, or
acceptance as an open order. We find that a staggering 97.8% of all rejected orders
are associated with a submission burst, as defined above.
We analyze the outcomes and lifecycles of all such submission bursts in Table 2.
The data in Table 2 reveal two distinct outcomes for this submission pattern.
Panel A confirms that the vast majority of bursts (86.5%) result in all orders being

2.5. The Concentration of Rejections: Identifying a Cohort
We next examine the distribution of rejected orders across traders. Rejection
activity is highly concentrated among a small number of participants.
Figure 1 illustrates this concentration. The bars show rejected orders per trader
per day, ranked in descending order; the line traces the cumulative share. The pattern
is unmistakable: rejection activity is not merely concentrated but dominated by a
handful of participants. One trader alone accounts for 39% of all rejections. By the
eighth trader, 95% of rejections are explained. By the tenth, 97%. By the twentieth,
over 99%. The remaining 53,000+ traders account for a negligible share of total
rejections.
We designate these eight traders as the QPS cohort.5
This small group is
responsible for nearly the entire flow of rejected orders. Moreover, their influence is
evident across two dimensions: they dominate rejected order flow and also account
for substantial trading volume. All eight rank among the top 50 traders by volume,
with two in the top 10. Collectively, they account for 13.5% of all maker volume on
the exchange—a striking concentration for just eight accounts.
The facts documented above call for explanation. In Section 3, we propose that
a single strategy—the pursuit of queue priority—provides a unified explanation for
these findings.

3. The Queue Priority Strategy (QPS)
The empirical facts in Section 2 leave us with a strange picture of the market.
Roughly nine out of ten post-only order submissions are bounced back as
BadAloPxRejected. This rejected flow is highly polarized—almost pure buy or pure
sell—and it forecasts the direction of the next price change with 73–91% accuracy. It
does not arrive as isolated probes, but rather in structured bursts that either vanish
as rejections or leave behind brief constellations of flickering orders. Orders posted at
the best price survive substantially longer than their off-price counterparts. Strikingly,
the entire phenomenon is generated by just eight traders.
We face three puzzles:
1. Why so much invisible effort? Why do traders submit such an enormous
volume of orders that ultimately end up being rejected?
2. Why are non-events apparently informed? How can mechanically rejected
orders—events that never touch the LOB—be so strongly directional and so
predictive of the next price change?
3. Why this particular choreography? Why do rejected orders almost always
arrive in bursts, and why does the survival time of accepted orders depend so
sharply on whether they are off-price or best-price?
These are not loose patterns that invite multiple interpretations.
They are
dominant features of the market, highly structured, and concentrated among just
eight traders. We propose a single hypothesis to account for all three: the QPS cohort
is executing a market-making strategy designed to capture queue priority immediately
following price changes.
3.1. The Value of Queue Priority
In price-time priority markets, queue position significantly influences both execution probability and expected profitability. The first order at the best price is
guaranteed to fill against the next incoming taker order of the opposite side. When
that best-price queue is large, these fills tend to be profitable, as shown in [2]. This
makes the FIL position extremely valuable—not just for getting filled, but for getting
filled profitably.
When are top-of-book queues large? Liquidity clusters at the top-of-book when
some combination of the following conditions holds: large tick sizes, meaningful maker
rebates, low volatility, or substantial “uninformed” taker flow that executes without
14

regard to short-term price signals.6 In such markets, the difference between first and
fiftieth in line can be the difference between a profitable fill and a losing one.
In the analysis that follows, we take as given the value of queue priority [23, 7].
Our focus is on the less-studied question of how HFTs attempt to secure favourable
queue positions—that is, what strategies do they use?
Competition for queue priority is especially fierce immediately after price changes.
When a taker order and/or cancellation widen the spread, the opportunity to establish
a new best quote arises. Consider a price rise triggered by a taker buy order that
clears the best ask. If that taker was informed, the fair price has likely risen—meaning
the old ask price is now attractive as a bid. Market makers recognize this: if someone
was willing to take at that price (particularly after accounting for the taker fee), I
should be willing to make at that price. The result is a race to post the first bid at the
vacated level, converting it into the new best bid. We now describe a strategy to win
(or, at least, place highly in) this race. We term it the Queue Priority Strategy (QPS),
and we show that it explains all the empirical evidence documented in Section 2.
3.2. The Queue Priority Strategy Hypothesis
The Queue Priority Strategy Hypothesis (QPSH) states that:
• A single strategy, the QPS, is the overwhelmingly dominant mechanism that
causes both the rejected order flow and the immediate replenishment of at-thetouch liquidity following a widening of the spread.
• The QPS has the goal of optimising same-side queue position for maker orders
at the touch immediately after the spread is refilled.
• The QPS is to maintain a high-frequency stream of submission bursts of postonly orders, targeting opposite-side price levels at and adjacent to the best
opposite-side price.

3.3. Strategy Mechanics and Evidence
We now present the details of the QPS, then test each of its components against
the data. Section 3.3.1 describes the strategy as an algorithm; Section 3.3.2 verifies
that the QPS cohort’s behavior matches each component.
3.3.1. The Strategy
The QPS operates as a continuous loop with three phases: signal evaluation, burst
submission, and cleanup.
Phase 1: Signal. The trader maintains a directional signal S(t) ∈{BUY, SELL, NEUTRAL},
updated continuously based on market information. Plausible inputs include order
book imbalance, recent trade flow, price movements on correlated venues, or proprietary combinations. We do not attempt to identify the inputs to the signal. Nor do we
have direct evidence of its existence; however, the data we present strongly suggests
that it exists and is remarkably accurate.
The NEUTRAL state corresponds to periods of inactivity: no bursts are submitted.
Any transition between states triggers cancellation of all outstanding orders before
the new state takes effect.
Phase 2: Submit Burst. (See Figure 4, panel B.) The trader submits a batch of
post-only orders targeting price levels on the opposite side of the book from the
current best quote. Let pa denote the current best ask, pb the current best bid, and δ
the tick size. Specifically:
• If S(t) = BUY: submit buy orders at prices {pa, pa + δ, pa + 2δ, . . . , pa + kδ},
where k is usually 8–10.
• If S(t) = SELL: submit sell orders at prices {pb, pb −δ, pb −2δ, . . . , pb −kδ}.
16

This “bracketing” across k + 1 consecutive price levels hedges uncertainty about how
far the price will move.7 The use of post-only orders ensures that if accepted, orders
are passive (earning maker rebates and avoiding the taker fee) and that orders which
would cross the spread are rejected rather than filled.
Orders are in flight for the network round-trip latency τ (typically 50–150 ms).
Upon arrival, the outcome depends on whether—and how far—the price has moved:
• Signal-aligned price move of m ticks: Orders at the first min(m, k) price levels
become passive and are accepted; orders at more aggressive levels still cross the
spread and are rejected. For example, if a buy burst targets 10 levels above pa
and the ask rises by 5 ticks, the first 5 orders are accepted while the remaining
5 are rejected.
• No movement (the typical case): All orders would cross the spread. They are
rejected with BadAloPxRejected and leave no trace on the order book.
• Signal-contrary movement: The price moves opposite to the signal—e.g., the
ask falls while a buy burst is in flight. The burst is rejected as above.
The bracketing structure ensures that some orders are accepted whenever the price
moves in the anticipated direction, regardless of the exact magnitude.
Phase 3: Cleanup. When orders are accepted, multiple orders from the same burst
may land and be accepted at different price levels. The trader retains only the order
at the new best price—if one exists—and immediately cancels the rest. If the price
move exceeds the burst’s bracketing range, no order lands at the best price; all are
cancelled. Either way, the cancelled orders become “flickering orders”—visible briefly
on the book before vanishing.
A second form of cleanup occurs when the directional signal reverses. If a trader
holds open buy orders and their signal flips to SELL, those orders face imminent
risk of negative-PFPR fills. Prudent risk management then requires cancelling the
open orders immediately. This signal-driven cancellation is not unique to the QPS;
it is standard practice for any high-frequency strategy involving passive orders; it is
arguably not part of the QPS at all.
The constant rain. Critically, the trader does not attempt to exactly time individual
burst submissions to coincide with price changes. Instead, bursts are submitted
continuously at sub-second intervals (ranging from tens to hundreds of milliseconds)
7On Hyperliquid, δ = $1 for BTC-perp, so a burst with k = 10 spans $10 of price space.
17

while the signal remains active, ensuring that orders are always in flight. The goal is
that when a price change occurs, at least one burst’s orders are processed immediately
after the price-moving LOB operations (cancellations and/or taker orders). Most
bursts are rejected. But the burst that happens to be submitted just before a price
change—and processed by the matching engine just after—succeeds: one or more of
its orders are accepted, likely among the first at the new price level.
The strategy thus produces three observable outputs: (i) rejected orders, when
bursts arrive before a price move; (ii) flickering orders, when bursts succeed but
require cleanup of off-price orders; and (iii) accepted orders at the new best price,
which are either left in place to be filled or later cancelled as part of routine order
management (e.g., if the price continues moving, or if the directional signal reverses).
All three are byproducts of the same underlying mechanism.
3.3.2. Evidence
We now test each component of the hypothesized strategy against the behavior
of the QPS cohort.
Throughout, we compare the cohort to All Other Traders
(AOT)—the remaining 52,821 traders in our sample. Table 3 summarizes the key
metrics.
Evidence of Directional Signals. Phase 1 posits that traders maintain a directional
signal and submit bursts only when that signal is active. If true, QPS rejected orders
should be directionally biased to align with the expected price move.
We test this by inferring the cohort’s aggregate signal before each price change.
Within the X ∈{100, 500, 1000, 2000} milliseconds preceding a price move, we count
QPS rejected buys and rejected sells. If buys exceed sells, the inferred signal is BUY;
if sells exceed buys, SELL.8
Three patterns in the data confirm that these rejected orders carry directional
information, consistent with the use of highly accurate short-term signals.
First, accuracy increases at shorter horizons. Across all qualifying price changes,
the inferred signal matches the subsequent move 76.8% of the time at 2 seconds,
rising to 81.7% at 1000 ms, 87% at 500 ms, and 91.3% at 100 ms (Figure 2a). Shorter
windows capture fresher signals, less diluted by old orders submitted when the trader’s
view may have been different.

6.07%. The QPS cohort is not passively posting liquidity; they are bracketing the
opposite side of the book.

3.4. Risk and Return Characteristics
The QPSH is a conjecture about behavior; we now ask whether it is economically
sensible. A strategy that generates billions of rejected orders must offer commensurate
rewards, unless the traders running it have discovered a spectacularly intensive way
to accomplish nothing. We examine the economics in three parts: first, the risks
inherent in signal-based market making and how QPS modifies them; second, the
rewards from queue priority; and third, the operational costs required to sustain the
strategy.

3.4.1. Risks
A benchmark for comparison. To understand the risk profile of QPS, consider a
hypothetical alternative: a market maker using the same (or a similar) short-term
directional signals, but implementing them conventionally—posting a passive limit
order at the top-of-book in the signal direction, waiting in the queue, and cancelling
when conditions change. Both approaches are signal-based market making in the
same economic sense: both aim to capture maker rebates and spread while receiving
fills with favourable post-fill price returns.
The difference lies in the execution:
QPS expresses the signal through crossing post-only bursts that are usually rejected,
whereas the conventional strategy expresses it through resting quotes that remain in
the book until cancelled.
Risks common to both strategies. Both strategies share the same core risks. Both
depend on accurate signals; both face fast takers who may hit resting quotes before
cancellation succeeds; both require sophisticated infrastructure. These are endemic to
short-horizon signal-based market making, not specific to QPS. The PFPR analysis
below quantifies this risk for the QPS cohort, but the underlying hazard applies
equally to any maker whose orders can be executed before they can be cancelled.
QPS advantage: acceptance is gated by a price move in the signal-implied direction.
QPS differs from conventional posting in one favourable respect: most signal-triggered
submissions become resting quotes only if the market moves in the same direction
while the orders are in flight. Mechanically, the burst prices are set so that—at the
moment of submission—they would cross the spread. Acceptance is effectively gated:
these post-only orders become resting quotes only if the best quotes move away during
the latency window—through taker trades and/or cancellations—so that the order
no longer crosses when processed. However, even a correct directional signal can
yield rejection if the anticipated move occurs after processing rather than during the
in-flight interval.
This gating matters when the signal is wrong or short-lived. If a QPS trader
submits a burst that does not align with the direction of the next price change, the
orders are simply rejected. They do not rest in the book at the touch (or near it) and
therefore are not, even briefly, in the position of being picked off in a latency race that
can produce negative post-fill price returns at short horizons. Under conventional
market making, by contrast, the trader typically already has a resting quote in the
book in the signal direction and must win the cancellation race in precisely these
wrong-signal episodes to avoid adverse fills.
The advantage is limited, however. Whenever a burst does succeed and an order
lands at the touch, the situation begins to resemble conventional market making:
25

the order must be monitored and may need to be cancelled quickly in response to
short-term information, including precisely the scenarios that trigger latency races.
Indeed, the risk can be higher conditional on landing, because QPS is designed to
secure first-in-line (or near first-in-line) queue position—making its quotes among the
first to be executed when fast takers arrive.
QPS-specific risk: multi-fill exposure. The offsetting cost is multi-fill risk. Because
QPS bursts bracket multiple price levels, a successful burst can result in simultaneous
acceptance at several prices; until cleanup completes, the trader holds several orders
across the ladder—a form of exposure that conventional single-level posting does not
create.
The exposure can be larger than it first appears. To win the FIL race with
high probability, QPS traders often maintain several in-flight bursts simultaneously.
Submission intervals are often less than round-trip latency, so outcomes of earlier
bursts are unknown when later bursts are sent. At peak activity, we estimate traders
may have around 10 bursts in flight, each spanning ∼10 price levels—on the order of
100 orders simultaneously. If all the orders are accepted, and an adverse move with
high taker volume follows before cleanup succeeds, the loss can be large. Consider
for example, a trader with 100 in-flight orders, each for $20,000 notional, and all
filled before cleanup. An adverse price move of 5 bp gives an immediate loss of
100 × 20,000 × 0.0005 = $1,000, enough to wipe hours of accumulated profit in
milliseconds.
We quantify the prevalence and cost of multi-fill events empirically. Define a
multi-fill as a burst in which orders at two or more distinct price levels are filled
before cleanup completes. We observe an average of 161 such events per day across
the QPS cohort. The distribution is right-skewed: the median involves fills at 2 price
levels, the average is 5.2 levels, and the 99th percentile reaches 11 levels.
We classify a multi-fill as “adverse” if the volume-weighted return across all filled
levels is negative at the 5-second horizon; 39.6% of multi-fills fall into this category. On
aggregate, these adverse events generate losses of approximately $88,000 per month
for the cohort—losses that are already reflected in the net profitability figures reported
in the following subsection. Put differently, cohort profits would be correspondingly
higher absent this source of drag. It bears noting that these losses occur despite
active management by traders who employ multiple mitigation strategies; the $88,000
monthly figure is therefore best interpreted as a lower bound on what unmitigated
multi-fill risk would cost—implying that net profitability is correspondingly higher
than it would be absent such efforts.
One mechanism that contributes to this mitigation is margin-based rejection.
When a trader’s margin is insufficient to support additional positions, Hyperliquid
26

rejects submissions with PerpMarginRejected. If a burst is accepted and a second
burst arrives before cleanup completes, the margin constraint can prevent the second
burst from landing—automatically limiting simultaneous exposure. We observe that
margin rejections cluster immediately after successful bursts—approximately 1.9%
of QPS submissions are margin-rejected, with rejections concentrated in the same
burst as an acceptance or in the burst immediately following—consistent with this
protective function. Some traders appear to deliberately size their margin to exploit
this effect, using the exchange’s own risk controls as an automatic backstop.
Fills with negative returns. We define the post-fill price return (PFPR) as the signed
change in the midprice following a fill, from the maker’s perspective. A positive
PFPR indicates that the price moved favourably; a negative PFPR indicates that it
moved against the maker. Fills with persistently negative PFPRs may reflect classical
adverse selection—where the taker possesses superior information—but may also arise
from noise trading or mechanical execution (e.g., TWAP) that happens to move prices
unfavourably. We adopt this neutral framing throughout. The standard defense is
rapid cancellation upon a signal change, but for QPS traders this risk is particularly
acute. The strategy revolves around securing orders at the front of the queue—a
position that, by construction, places the FIL holder first in line to receive fills when
fast-moving taker flow arrives at a quote that has just become stale (for example
in a latency-arbitrage scenario, cf [1]). Such fills often exhibit persistently negative
PFPRs. If the FIL holder fails to cancel before aggressive flow arrives, the resulting
fill is likely to be costly.
We identify negative-PFPR fills using two approaches. First, a threshold test:
fills where the midprice moves against the maker by at least 1 bp within 500 ms.
Approximately 0.7% of QPS fills meet this criterion. Second, a persistence test:
fills (excluding multi-fills) where the PFPR is negative at all of 500 ms, 1 second,
2 seconds, and 5 seconds: these fills experience short-term sustained adverse price
movements.
By the persistence criterion, 2.3% of single fills exhibit persistently negative
PFPRs, an average of 377 per day. Volume-weighted maker losses for these fills
average −0.82 bp at 500 ms, −0.91 bp at 1 second, −1.13 bp at 5 seconds, −1.58 bp
at 10 seconds, and −1.34 bp at 30 seconds. Aggregate losses from negative-PFPR
fills amount to approximately $41,000 per month.
Minimizing these losses requires cancelling orders before informed takers arrive11—
but not so aggressively as to cancel orders that might have yielded profitable fills.

This is a delicate calibration: the trader must avoid exactly the negative-PFPR fills

Operational risk. Operating the QPS is technically demanding. The trader must
master the exchange’s API: bulk submission, in-flight order cancellation, rate-limit
mechanics, order book update latency. Edge cases that occur once in a million
messages will occur daily at this submission frequency; failure to handle them robustly
invites disaster.
Beyond API fluency, the infrastructure itself must be fast, efficient, and correct.
State must be maintained across hundreds or thousands of orders; no order can be
forgotten or lost. Code must be low-latency and rigorously tested. A single bug—a
missed cancellation, a state desync, a malformed message—can produce losses that
dwarf many days of profits.
These are practical concerns for trading firms rather than academic questions, so
we do not elaborate further. We simply note that the operational bar is high, and
that this may be one reason why only a small number of traders attempt the strategy.
Having established the risk profile of QPS relative to conventional signal-based
market making—including the gating advantage, the multi-fill exposure, and the
vulnerability to negative-PFPR fills—we now turn to the rewards that accrue from
successful queue capture, and subsequently to the operational costs required to sustain
the strategy.
3.4.2. Rewards
The objective of the QPS is to secure first-in-line—or close to first-in-line—queue
position at prices that become the new best quote following a directional move.
Compared with the benchmark of conventional posting, which typically places the
trader at the back of the queue at the prevailing touch, success in this objective
confers two advantages: a higher probability of execution (the trader is first to meet
incoming flow) and more favourable PFPRs (fills occur at quotes that have just
improved, not quotes about to become stale); [2] provides a formal analysis. The
empirical question is whether the QPS actually achieves this objective—and if so,
how valuable the resulting positions are.
Recall from Section 3.1 that queues at the new best bid (after a price rise) or new
best ask (after a price decline) tend to become large. These opportunities present
themselves every 1.8 seconds on average. Does the strategy succeed in capturing
them? The evidence is clear: the QPS cohort captures 57.5% of all first-in-line fills.
Eight traders, out of approximately 53,000 active during the sample period, secure
a majority of some of the market’s most valuable executions. Note that the 57.5%
figure understates the cohort’s dominance in landing FIL orders. Many FIL orders
28

are cancelled before any fill occurs—because the price continues moving, or because
the trader’s signal reverses leading them to cancel to avoid negative-PFPR fills. The
57.5% reflects fills that actually execute at the FIL position, a conservative measure
of the cohort’s queue-priority advantage.
How valuable are these positions? To quantify, we compute markouts for FIL trades
captured by the QPS cohort: the signed change in the midprice at a given horizon
(e.g., 5 seconds) relative to the fill price, from the maker’s perspective—positive if the
price moved favourably for the maker. Volume-weighted markouts average +0.51 bp at
the 5-second horizon and +0.50 bp at 30 seconds. A full round trip (fill, then unwind
at the opposite side) would therefore be expected to yield a net profit (before rebates)
of approximately 1 bp. The QPS cohort systematically profits from queue priority.
The magnitudes underscore why QPS operates as a maker strategy: markouts on the
order of a few tenths of a basis point are far smaller than the taker fee, so the same
directional signals would not be profitably exploited through aggressive orders.
Not every QPS order lands first-in-line; many secure merely early queue positions.
These too are valuable. We therefore examine all maker fills captured by the QPS
cohort, not just FIL trades. Table 4 summarizes the results.

3.4.3. Costs
As noted above, both QPS and conventional signal-based market making require
sophisticated low-latency infrastructure, so their fixed operational requirements are
broadly similar. In contrast to the benchmark maker’s steady post–cancel rhythm,
the QPS operates at far higher message throughput and requires more complex
order-state management—burst submission, in-flight tracking, rapid cleanup, and
tight rate-limit constraints.
The QPS (as well as the conventional posting benchmark) does not incur direct
costs in the form of exchange fees—indeed, per-trade fees are negative. Because QPS
orders are passive by construction (post-only), every fill earns the maker rebate (0.3
bp on Hyperliquid) rather than paying the taker fee.
Instead, the true costs are operational: infrastructure, connectivity, development,
and the human capital required to build and maintain a low-latency trading system.
These fixed costs are substantial but are not reflected in per-trade economics. Once
the infrastructure exists, the marginal cost of submitting an additional rejected
order is effectively zero. Rate limits imposed by the exchange are a further binding
constraint: at the submission frequencies the QPS demands, traders are likely to
32

operate near or at the ceiling, making efficient use of each message slot a first-order
concern.
3.4.4. Competition and Barriers to Entry
Why do only eight traders employ this strategy? The answer has two parts:
competitive dynamics that limit equilibrium participation, and barriers to entry that
restrict who can participate at all.
Competitive dynamics. The strategy’s profitability depends on there being limited
competition. Each additional trader operating the QPS degrades the expected queue
position for all other QPS traders. This, in turn, would be expected to produce a
lower fill probability and poorer post-fill returns for all QPS fills [2], while risks and
operational demands would remain unchanged.
This suggests that entry should continue until marginal profitability reaches zero.
The equilibrium number of participants depends on the volume of non-signal-based
taker flow (the size of the pie, loosely speaking) and on heterogeneity in infrastructure
quality across potential entrants. We do not model this formally, but note the
structural parallel to latency-race models [1, 10], where speed rents are dissipated by
competition. The QPS is a different kind of race—not to cancel before a taker arrives,
but to land first when the price moves—but the competitive dynamics are similar.
Barriers to entry. The QPS requires low-latency connectivity to minimize round-trip
time; a potent short-term directional signal accurate enough to make the strategy profitable; and a sophisticated order-management system capable of tracking potentially
thousands of orders while executing cleanup efficiently, and, when the signal reverses,
cancelling open orders faster than competing taker. Building and maintaining this
infrastructure is technically demanding and capital-intensive—a fixed cost that filters
out all but the most sophisticated participants and, in turn, sustains the profitability
of those who remain.
3.5. Spread Refilling Is Anticipatory, Not Reactive
We now examine two empirical regularities often discussed separately—spread
refilling after spread-widening events, and the prevalence of very short-lived (“flickering”) orders—and ask what the QPSH implies for each. We begin with spread
refilling.
The conventional view of spread refilling is reactive: a spread-widening event
occurs, market participants observe the gap, and new orders arrive to close it [22, 13].
Under this view, the time between the widening of the spread and its subsequent
33

narrowing is bounded below by the observe-react-submit cycle—the time for a participant to observe the gap, decide to act, and have a new order processed by the
exchange.
If the QPS is active, however, a submission burst that can land in the newlywidened spread is often already in flight when the spread widens, en route to acceptance
for all orders that land at a vacated price level. This yields three testable predictions:
1. The aligned side—the side towards which the price moves—should refill first:
in-flight burst orders land within the widened spread, closing it entirely when
the bracket range spans the gap and narrowing it otherwise.
2. Refilling should occur faster than any plausible reaction time (i.e., the liquidity
was in flight before the spread-widening event, or SWE).
3. The initial spread-narrowing (or spread-closing) liquidity—the orders that first
land within the gap—should come predominantly from QPS traders.
Panel C in Figure 4 illustrates the refilling mechanism for the case of a rising top
ask. We now test these predictions formally.
We analyze all spread-widening events (SWEs). An SWE occurs when the spread
increases from one tick—its typical value—to two or more ticks. We set T = 0 at
the timestamp of the LOB event—a cancellation or trade—that causes the spread to
widen beyond one tick, and track which side first posts an order that narrows the
spread.
After an SWE triggered by an ask increase, the first spread-narrowing order can
come from the aligned side (a new bid above the pre-SWE best bid), the contra
side (a new ask below the post-SWE best ask), or both simultaneously (which we
operationalize as within 1 ms). Symmetric definitions apply for bid-triggered SWEs.
For each SWE, we identify which side first narrows the spread and measure the
time from T = 0 to this first spread-narrowing order. We exclude SWEs where no
quote improvement occurs within 500 ms. Table 7 reports the results, which confirm
all three predictions.
Prediction 1: The aligned side refills first. In 90.4% of SWEs, the first spreadnarrowing order comes from the aligned side. Contra-side refills account for only
7.0% of cases; simultaneous refills, 2.6%.

3.6. A Parsimonious Origin for Flickering Orders
As noted in Section 1, flickering orders have long puzzled researchers. The QPS
offers a parsimonious explanation for a substantial share of this activity: flickering
orders are, in part, the residue of the cleanup phase—the brief visibility of orders
that landed at off-price levels before being cancelled.
Recall from Section 3.3.1 that when a submission burst lands orders at multiple
price levels, then under the QPS, the trader retains at most one order (the one at the
new best price, if there is one) and immediately cancels the rest. These cancelled
orders become flickering orders. The data confirm this account.
Flickering is pervasive. Order lifetimes in our dataset are generally short. Of all
accepted orders (≈389 million in our sample), 1.5% are cancelled within 100 ms, 7.0%
within 200 ms, and 29.3% within 500 ms. The median order survives approximately
1.5 seconds (1,516 ms). At longer horizons, 42.9% are cancelled within 1 second,
35

69.6% within 5 seconds, and 89.3% within 30 seconds. Short-lived liquidity is not a
marginal phenomenon; it is the norm.
Flickering is concentrated. As with rejections, flickering activity is highly concentrated.
The top 5 traders by flickering volume account for 34.8% of all flickering orders; the
top 10 account for 55.4%; the top 20 for 73.9%; and the top 100 for 97.2%. The QPS
cohort generates 31.0% of all flickering orders and falls entirely within the top 40
traders ranked by flickering volume. (This share is modest despite the cohort’s high
flickering rate (Table 3), because QPS traders account for only a small fraction of
all accepted orders in the market.) Flickering is also concentrated in time: 91.3%
of QPS-generated flickering orders occur within 500 ms of a spread-widening event,
consistent with the cleanup mechanism described in Section 3.3.1.
The QPSH thus provides a parsimonious explanation for a substantial share of
flickering activity. At the same time, the fact that the QPS cohort accounts for “only”
31.0% indicates that flickering orders have multiple origins in this market. Other
strategies or phenomena must account for the remainder. The QPS is sufficient to
generate pervasive flickering; it is not the only source.
4. Conclusion
We have documented a massive, invisible flow of rejected orders that constitutes
the dominant form of interaction with the exchange we study—a venue whose BTC
perpetual contract alone handles approximately $3 billion in average daily trading
volume. This flow is not noise. We propose and demonstrate that it is the exhaust of
a pervasive, anticipatory strategy which we term the Queue Priority Strategy (QPS),
in which a small cohort of high-frequency traders floods the market with cross-side
post-only orders, hoping to land first in line when the price moves—and succeeding:
despite numbering just eight, they capture a staggering 57.5% of all first-in-line fills.
Moreover, this rejected-order flow carries strong predictive content: using the net
imbalance of rejected buys versus rejected sells in the final 100 ms before a price
change as a proxy for the cohort’s latent signal, the implied direction matches the
direction of the ensuing price change 91.3% of the time; their signals are also strikingly
unanimous, in that when one trader is net buying, the others almost always are too.
Taken together, our results suggest a unifying interpretation of two empirical
regularities: spread refilling and flickering orders. Because QPS bursts are typically
already in flight when a spread-widening event occurs, the first liquidity at the new
best price is anticipatory rather than reactive. Because submission bursts often
land orders at multiple price levels, the off-price orders are cancelled as part of
risk-management during cleanup—producing flickering orders. We do not claim this
36

is the only origin of either phenomenon, but it is, at minimum, a quantitatively
important one.
What the QPSH Explains—and What It Does Not. This study has told us a combination of everything and nothing. The QPSH is a simple and well-evidenced explanation
for the way in which the majority of updates to liquidity provision occur on Hyperliquid and possibly on other markets; it sets out the precise mechanism for the initial
replenishment of liquidity at the touch after a price move, along with an explanation
for a significant proportion of maker cancellations, and it does so in the context of a
risk-managed strategy with the clear goal of gaining queue priority. The evidence
also strongly suggests that the replenishment is targeted in response to an external
signal. In this last sense, it places liquidity provision on a footing similar to that of
liquidity removal by informed taker orders or cancellations, where signals also play a
critical role (in the case of taker orders, the taker fee means that a more convincing
signal is needed than for maker orders). However, just as for taker orders and many
maker cancellations, the QPSH has nothing to say about where such signals may
originate: it simply explains the mechanism by which they are translated into the
LOB. Nor does it contribute to our understanding of price changes over timescales
longer than the few seconds that are the immediate focus of high-frequency trading.
The hidden universe of rejected orders remains as an invisible but integral part of
the way in which markets translate information into prices; and this itself remains as
enigmatic as ever.
Limitations and External Validity. Our analysis is confined to a single venue, Hyperliquid, over a single month. Hyperliquid is a blockchain-based exchange where rejected
orders can be reconstructed from on-chain records; this data availability makes our
study possible, but it also raises questions about generalizability. We cannot directly
observe rejected orders on traditional exchanges like NYSE or NASDAQ. However,
several indirect signatures of the QPS are in principle observable in standard data.
These include
1. Concentration of flickering activity. Flickering orders should cluster temporally
around spread-widening events, and if trader identifiers are available, cancellations at or worse than the best price should almost always be accompanied by a
surviving order at the best price from the same trader (the cleanup signature).
2. Burst structure in acceptances. When trader identifiers are available, successful
submissions should arrive in multi-level bursts—several orders at adjacent prices
within milliseconds—rather than as isolated posts.
37

3. Speed and direction of spread refilling. The side toward which the price moved
should typically refill first, and refilling latency should be shorter than plausible
reaction time given the venue’s network latency.
4. First-fill concentration. If trader identifiers are available, a small cohort should
capture a disproportionate share of first-in-line fills after price changes.
Whether the same strategy operates at comparable scale on traditional venues is an
open empirical question.
Regulatory Considerations. The QPS involves submitting orders that are expected
to be rejected, and when accepted, are expected to be cancelled almost immediately
unless they are at the new best price. The trader generally wants to be filled on only
a small fraction of the orders submitted. Might this amount to spoofing?
We think not. First, the rejected orders never reach the book. Therefore, they
cannot create false impressions of supply or demand because no one sees them. Second,
the orders that do reach the book represent genuine willingness to trade: the bestprice orders are real bids for queue priority, and the trader actively wants them filled.
Third, the rapid cancellation of off-price orders is risk management, not manipulation;
traders cancel to avoid unwanted inventory, not to mislead other participants. Indeed,
the QPS exploits the exchange’s own mechanics for risk control: the post-only flag
ensures automatic rejection of orders that would cross the spread (avoiding adverse
selection), while cleverly exploited margin constraints prevent unwanted exposure
to multi-fill risk. The intent is to provide liquidity at a specific price, not to create
phantom depth.
Whether this activity represents a net benefit or cost to market quality is a
question we leave open.
Implications for Market Design. Our findings connect to the broader debate about
the design of limit order books. [10] argue that continuous trading creates mechanical
arbitrage rents and spurs socially wasteful speed competition. The QPSH is a vivid
illustration of this sort of phenomenon: traders invest heavily in infrastructure to
exploit latency, generating billions of failed attempts as collateral damage. Whether
alternative market designs—frequent batch auctions, for instance—would eliminate
or merely reshape this activity is beyond our scope, but the question seems worth
asking.
We began by invoking Sola Scriptura—the study of markets through what is written
in the (limit order) book. We have adhered to this approach, but what has changed is
the scope of the (limit order) book itself. Because Hyperliquid records every message
38

submitted to the matching engine—including the majority that leave no mark on
any conventional data feed—our text is far more detailed than what was previously
available. The invisible ink of rejected orders becomes legible, and with it an entire
stratum of trading activity that has, until now, been hidden in plain sight.
Appendix A. Hyperliquid Exchange Architecture
Hyperliquid is, to our knowledge, the first exchange where rejected orders can
be observed at scale. Because it operates as a fully on-chain limit order book on
a purpose-built blockchain, every interaction with the matching engine—including
those that produce no observable change in the book—is permanently recorded and
can be retrieved by anyone running a node. The result is complete visibility into who
is doing what on the exchange: not only rejected orders, but also trader identifiers,
profit-and-loss histories for each account, failed cancellation attempts, and the full
sequence of messages submitted by every participant.
Hyperliquid operates a limit order book with price-time priority: resting orders
at each price level are ranked by the sequence in which they were committed to the
chain, and incoming marketable orders execute against the earliest liquidity first.
The matching logic is, in this sense, structurally analogous to that of traditional
central limit order book markets such as NASDAQ, or even other crypto exchanges
like Binance and Bybit.
Hyperliquid’s blockchain architecture does, however, introduce a departure from
traditional price-time priority in how orders are sequenced within each block.
On a centralized exchange, a single matching engine receives all orders, and “arrival
time” is unambiguous: it is the instant an order reaches that server. Hyperliquid, by
contrast, runs on a network of geographically distributed validators, and orders are
batched into blocks with a median block time of approximately 100 ms.18
Within each block, a type-based hierarchy takes precedence over temporal priority:
cancellations and post-only orders are always processed before aggressive (GTC and
IOC) orders, regardless of arrival sequence. The stated rationale is maker protection:
by letting cancellations execute first, market makers can update stale quotes before
being picked off by faster takers, encouraging tighter spreads. From the official
documentation:
“Hyperliquid makes a fundamental adjustment to the onchain order book:
Cancels and post-only orders are prioritized above GTC and IOC orders.
18See https://hyperliquid-co.gitbook.io/wiki/architecture/hyperbft.
39

This ordering is enforced onchain by the L1 itself. The only correct way
for a node to execute a block on the Hyperliquid L1 is to sort cancels and
post-only orders first.”
More precisely, within each block Hyperliquid imposes an intra-block action hierarchy:
it first processes liquidity-adding orders, then processes cancellations, and finally
processes liquidity-taking orders. Within each category, actions are processed in
timestamp order.
The economic consequence is a structural advantage for makers. If a cancellation
and an aggressive order land in the same block—even if the aggressive order was
broadcast first—the cancellation executes first.
While the intra-block type hierarchy structurally favours makers, its practical
benefit is limited by the unpredictability of block assignment. A trader cannot control
or reliably predict which block a given message will land in. Two messages sent
simultaneously may arrive several blocks apart. For instance, a cancellation submitted
milliseconds after the placement of the order may in fact land in an earlier block,
causing it to fail because the order it seeks to cancel does not yet exist. The extent
to which traders can exploit intra-block ordering by type is therefore constrained by
substantial randomness in message routing—a question for high-frequency traders or
exchange operators rather than the focus of the present study.
Minimum observable lifetimes. Throughout this paper, all timestamps reflect the
moment the matching engine processes a message, not the moment the trader submits
it (see Section 2). An immediate implication is that no order can appear to live for
less than one network round trip: even if a trader cancels the instant it learns an
order has been accepted, the observed lifetime includes the outbound confirmation
and the inbound cancellation. On Hyperliquid, this floor is approximately 200–300 ms,
which explains the flat initial region of the order survival curves in Figure 3. The
same consideration informs our choice of 150 ms as the threshold for “in-flight” orders
in Section 3.5.
Generality of the QPS. Nothing in the QPS depends on features peculiar to Hyperliquid. The strategy’s impetus—the economic value of queue position at the best
price—is universal on exchanges that operate price-time priority, from NASDAQ to
Binance. Its two key mechanical requisites are equally ubiquitous: post-only order
types, which provide cost-free rejection of crossing orders, and margin checks on
leveraged positions, which QPS traders repurpose for risk management. Both are
standard on modern exchanges. It is natural to ask whether Hyperliquid’s higher
latency might favour the strategy. We see no reason to think so. If anything, the
40

opposite seems more likely: higher latency means bursts remain in-flight longer,
their outcomes uncertain for longer, adding to operational complexity and possibly
increasing risk. The intra-block type ordering does offer a general advantage to
makers, QPS traders included, but it is neither specific to the QPS nor sufficient
to explain it, especially given the unpredictability of block assignment noted above.
As noted in Section 3.6, empirical patterns documented on conventional equity markets are at least consistent with, and possibly indicative of, QPS activity on those
venues. In particular, [6] report that flickering orders on European equity markets
are concentrated among a small number of high-frequency participants and occur
disproportionately in the direction of recent price moves—buy-side flickering after a
price rise, sell-side after a decline—precisely the signature the QPS cleanup phase
would produce. If the QPS does operate on traditional venues, this considerably
strengthens the case that the strategy does not materially depend on Hyperliquid’s
idiosyncrasies. What Hyperliquid uniquely provides is not the phenomenon but its
complete legibility: rejected orders, invisible on conventional venues, are here recorded
in full.
