Lead-Lag Relationships in Market Microstructure
Anonymous authors
Abstract
We investigate high-frequency cross-asset lead-lag relationships using various market microstructure measures capturing price, liquidity, depth, imbalance, and volatility dimensions.
Findings reveal that information from one asset’s trade prices and order book imbalance
predicts another asset’s midpoint future behavior. We also discover lead-lag relationships
between diﬀerent volatility measures. Most of the lead-lag relationships in our sample exist
between fundamentally related instruments. Trading activity and liquidity strengthen the
lead of equity instruments, but this does not hold true for other asset classes. Our study
demonstrates that lead-lag relationships can be leveraged for short-term predictions based
on the history of the leading instrument.

Lead-lag relationships, market microstructure, cross-asset trading,

1. Introduction
Lead-lag eﬀects in ﬁnancial markets describe situations where some ﬁnancial instruments
are leading and provide information about the future price, liquidity, or volatility development of other instruments lagging behind. Such eﬀects can arise as a consequence of unequal
information dissemination across ﬁnancial instruments and markets, potentially implying a
violation of the semi-strong form of the market eﬃciency hypothesis.
The emergence of high-frequency trading (HFT) has substantially increased the speed
of information dissemination in ﬁnancial markets (O’Hara, 2015), shifting lead-lag eﬀects to
shorter time frames. In liquid ﬁnancial markets such as European and US equity markets,
the most dominant ﬁnancial instruments lead lagging instruments by seconds or milliseconds (Huth and Abergel, 2014; Dao et al., 2018). Although HFT increases the speed of
information dissemination, it does not result in fully eﬃcient ﬁnancial markets or create a
level playing ﬁeld for all traders (Goldstein et al., 2023). Lead-lag eﬀects that imply statistical arbitrage opportunities and return predictability within time frames exploitable only
by a small subset of the fastest high-frequency traders still contradict market eﬃciency and
pose risks to slower high-frequency and non-high-frequency traders. For instance, the fastest
high-frequency traders can leverage lead-lag eﬀects to "snipe" stale quotes of other traders
within a lagging instrument when the leading instrument indicates a price shift in the lagging instrument. Liquidity providers, who cannot adequately react to these lead-lag eﬀects,
incorporate the risk of the fastest high-frequency traders picking oﬀtheir stale limit orders,
resulting in higher bid-ask spreads and, consequently, higher transaction costs for investors.
These diametrical consequences for market quality due to high-frequency traders sniping

High-frequency traders focus less on

due to high-frequency traders’ ability to monitor changes in the order book of one asset and
rapidly react to these changes within the order books of other assets. As a result, lead-lag
relationships in modern ﬁnancial markets have not only shifted to shorter time frames but
are increasingly related to granular microstructure information and are ampliﬁed by HFT
strategies. Given the large number of ﬁnancial instruments covered by high-frequency traders
and the reduced relevance of fundamental information, these eﬀects may not be limited to
fundamentally related assets, such as a derivative contract and its underlying. They can
also exist among non-fundamentally related assets, as high-frequency traders rapidly disseminate market-wide information across all types of ﬁnancial instruments. This imposes
additional risks on liquidity providers, as they need to be constantly aware of existing crossasset lead-lag eﬀects. Unknown or changing eﬀects that they do not incorporate into their
quotes expose them to the risk of sophisticated high-frequency traders picking oﬀtheir stale
limit orders. Despite the economic relevance of changing and potentially unknown lead-lag

To shed more light on cross-asset lead-lag relationships in (ultra) high-frequency, we aim

Our asset universe consists of liquid stocks, equity index futures, bond futures, and
exchange-traded products (ETPs) including equity exchange-traded funds (ETFs), exchangetraded commodities (ETCs), and exchange-traded notes (ETNs) traded on Xetra and Eurex.
For each instrument, we calculate various microstructure measures that capture aspects of
prices, liquidity, depth, imbalance, or volatility using high-frequency data on a nanosecond

Then, we systematically examine lead-lag relationships between all resulting time
series to provide an extensive view on short-term dynamics in modern market microstructure.
This data-driven approach allows us to explore all potential lead-lag relationships in our asset
universe without any underlying assumptions such as the fundamental relationship between
two instruments. Unlike the existing literature which primarily focuses on analyzing lead-lag
relationships between the midpoints of two assets (e.g., Huth and Abergel, 2014; Dao et al.,
2018; Buccheri et al., 2021), we extend our analysis to consider scenarios such as whether
the bid-ask spread of the Euro Stoxx 50 future leads or lags the order book imbalance of
the DAX future. Thereby, we explicitly examine frequencies of up to 10 microseconds (µs)

We consider our contribution to be threefold. First, we provide evidence for several leadlag relationships that have not been documented by previous studies such as the relationship
between midpoint and transaction price volatility. Additionally, we show that these eﬀects
occur across various measures with midpoints and transaction prices having the strongest

relationship between them. Furthermore, we show that the determinants of lead-lag relationships in the equity market, that were identiﬁed by previous empirical studies (e.g., Huth

a novel measure, referred to as lead-lag strength (LLS), to quantify and assess both the
direction and magnitude of lead-lag relationships while controlling for the overall level of
correlation. This measure addresses the shortcomings of previous alternative measures for
non-synchronous correlation, such as the maximum correlation, the lead-lag time, or the
lead-lag ratio (LLR) proposed by Huth and Abergel (2014), which are insuﬃcient criteria
to assess the strength of a lead-lag relationship.

2. Literature Review
Several studies have documented signiﬁcant lead-lag relationships among diﬀerent assets and asset classes at various trading frequencies. The ﬁrst studies analyzing lead-lag
relationships focus on interdependencies between the transaction prices of stocks and their
corresponding index futures and ﬁnd that future prices lead the stock prices by up to 45
minutes (Zeckhauser and Niederhoﬀer, 1983; Kawaller et al., 1987; Herbst et al., 1987). Subsequent research suggests that lead-lag eﬀects between futures and stocks are bidirectional,
but the futures’ lead is more pronounced and stronger than the equities’ lead (Stoll and Whaley, 1990; Chiang and Fong, 2001; Hayashi and Koike, 2018). Hoﬀmann et al. (2013) show
that lead-lag eﬀects also exist between equity and bond futures, i.e., across asset classes, with
the DAX future leading the Bund future by nearly one second. Further studies analyzing
lead-lag relationships across asset classes ﬁnd, for example, that stock prices lead the prices
of high yield bonds, indicating that the stock market incorporates information faster than
the bond market (Tolikas, 2018). Also, Pitkäjärvi et al. (2020) ﬁnd bidirectional lead-lag relationships between equities and bonds. Lead-lag relationships have even been documented
in relatively new asset classes, such as cryptocurrencies. For example, Sifat et al. (2019)
observe bidirectional relationships between hourly and daily transaction prices of Bitcoin
and Ethereum.

Overall, the majority of studies examining lead-lag relationships between prices provide
evidence that more liquid and larger instruments take a leading role in disseminating pricing information to less liquid and smaller instruments, which lag behind (Zeckhauser and
Niederhoﬀer, 1983; Lo and MacKinlay, 1990). Speciﬁcally, Chordia et al. (2011) show that
systematic demand shocks are ﬁrst incorporated into the prices of more actively traded and
larger stocks, resulting in cross-correlation patterns in stock returns of large and small ﬁrms.
In addition, several studies demonstrate that security and market characteristics, such as
volatility, security size, or market frictions, determine the direction of lead-lag relationships
(Chordia et al., 2005; Kallberg and Pasquariello, 2008; Huth and Abergel, 2014).
Apart from lead-lag eﬀects between asset prices, lead-lag eﬀects between the liquidity of
diﬀerent assets have also been documented. For example, the liquidity of individual stocks,
measured by the relative quoted spread or depth at the best quotes (breadth), is inﬂuenced
by changes in the market-wide liquidity (Fabre and Frino, 2004).
This relation, known
as liquidity commonality, has also been observed in bond and foreign exchange markets
(Marshall et al., 2013). In addition, liquidity lead-lag eﬀects are also observed across diﬀerent
security types. Son et al. (2023) show that ETF bid-ask spreads are predictable by the
lagged bid-ask spreads of their constituents and vice versa. Analyzing diﬀerences in price
and liquidity discovery, Pelizzon et al. (2015) ﬁnd that although the futures market leads
the cash market in price discovery, the cash market leads the futures market in liquidity
discovery, as measured by the depth of the market.
Another research stream concentrates on volatility lead-lag eﬀects between diﬀerent markets and securities. The empirical studies provide a mixed picture on the lead-lag relationships in terms of volatility. While multiple studies (e.g., Kawaller et al., 1990; Arshanapalli

and Doukas, 1994) do not ﬁnd any systematic patterns of lead-lag relationships in volatilities, Koutmos and Tucker (1996) provide evidence for an interdependence of volatilities in
futures and spot markets. This is supported by Iihara et al. (1996) and Kavussanos et al.
(2008) who ﬁnd that the futures market leads the spot market both in terms of returns and
volatility.
A limited number of research studies investigates lead-lag eﬀects between asset prices,
liquidity, and volatility.
Darrat et al. (2003), for example, investigate interdependencies
between liquidity and volatility and ﬁnd that stocks’ trading volume leads stocks’ return
volatility. Furthermore, Korajczyk and Sadka (2008) ﬁnd cross-serial correlations between
returns and liquidity shocks, indicating that liquidity can be predicted by historical returns.
Goyenko and Ukhov (2009) state that stock volatility causes short-term bond illiquidity and
that stock illiquidity causes bond volatility. Hence, previous research also detects lead-lag
eﬀects between diﬀerent microstructure dimensions.
All the aforementioned studies analyze lead-lag eﬀects on a weekly, daily or intraday
basis using ﬁxed sampling frequencies of more than one minute. However, with the rapid
increase in the number of trades and the average trading speed due to HFT, cross-asset
lead-lag eﬀects are more likely to occur in the sub-second region and to be more short-living
(Alsayed and McGroarty, 2014). As a result, recent research regarding lead-lag relationships
has started to focus on smaller time scales by examining non-sampled high-frequency market
data, i.e., trades and quotes with high-precision timestamps. However, using high-frequency
data to analyze lead-lag eﬀects is challenging since trades and order book changes do not
occur at regular time intervals.

Addressing this issue, several studies apply the estimator proposed by Hayashi and

and detect various high-frequency lead-lag relationships, they fall short in the following aspects: (i) All studies focus on interdependencies of securities’ midpoints. However, as research in lower frequencies shows, lead-lag eﬀects are not only prominent among securities’
prices, but also occur between assets’ liquidity and volatility. Hence, we want to close this research gap by investigating high-frequency lead-lag relationships among various microstructure measures for prices, liquidity, imbalance, depth, and volatility. (ii) Previous studies
investigating high-frequency lead-lag eﬀects examine lead-lag relations that are fundamentally motivated and apply pre-deﬁned assumptions on relationships, such as the relationship
between derivatives and their underlying stocks. By using a data-driven approach, we want
to investigate high-frequency lead-lag relationships without any underlying assumptions in

4. Methodology
4.1. Estimating non-contemporaneous correlations between microstructure measures
Trading activities in ﬁnancial markets like trades, order submissions, or deletions occur
at irregular time intervals. As a result, microstructure measures that capture dimensions
like prices or liquidity are also observed at irregular time intervals. This creates a challenge
when attempting to measure (non-contemporaneous) correlation between two time series
of microstructure measures, because standard statistical techniques to estimate correlation
usually require time series of equally-spaced time intervals with the same length. Primarily,
earlier studies overcome this problem by downsampling the two time series at a lower and
common frequency. As a result, they can analyze lead-lag relationships by estimating regression models such as vector autoregressive models to then test for Granger causality (e.g.,
Chordia and Swaminathan (2000), Chordia et al. (2011), and Rapach et al. (2013)). However, downsampling the time series to a lower frequency, such as daily, hourly, or minutely,

4.2. Data-driven approach
We follow a data-driven approach that aims to provide an overarching picture of leadlag relationships in market microstructure. To meet this goal, we (i) deﬁne a broad asset
universe, (ii) use canonical measures applied in market microstructure research that capture
asset prices, liquidity, imbalance, depth, and volatility at a high-frequency scale, and (iii)
analyze non-contemporaneous correlations at diﬀerent time horizons.
Our data universe
covers 19 securities that represent diﬀerent asset classes (equities, bonds, commodities, and
cryptocurrencies) and security types (stocks, futures, and ETPs), and, hence, reﬂect the
heterogeneity of securities in modern regulated markets.
For each instrument in our sample, we calculate various microstructure measures based
on its trade and limit order book data. In particular, we investigate two price measures:
midpoint and transaction price, two liquidity measures: relative quoted spread and trading
volume, three depth and imbalance measures: breadth at level 1, depth at level 10, and
order book imbalance at level 1 (OBI), and two volatility measures: midpoint volatility
and transaction price volatility. Table A.1 in the Appendix provides details about these
measures.

Finally, we also deﬁne the leads and lags applicable for the HY-estimator. We use three
diﬀerent sets of horizons that cover a low frequency (LF) range from one to 60 seconds,
a high-frequency (HF) range from one to 500 milliseconds, and an ultra high-frequency
(UHF) range from 10µs to 500µs. All these horizons are used as leads and lags in the sense
of the HY-estimator ˆρ(ℓ):
lLF = {1s, 2s, 3s, 5s, 10s, 30s, 60s},
lHF = {1ms, 10ms, 100ms, 250ms, 500ms},
lUHF = {10µs, 50µs, 100µs, 250µs, 500µs}
l ∈{−lLF, −lHF, −lUHF, 0, lUHF, lHF, lLF}

The diﬀerent horizons shall reﬂect varying reaction times of professional investors in
modern ﬁnancial markets. The lower bound of 10µs is a conservative estimate of the reaction
time of the fastest traders in the market.6 The upper bound of 60 seconds shall reﬂect the
reaction time of a trader with a rather simple and low-cost infrastructure.
To systematically evaluate lead-lag relationships in market microstructure, we estimate
correlations on a daily basis for all instrument-measure combinations in our sample at all
time horizons in l. Note that we do not only examine the lead-lag relationships between two
ﬁnancial instruments (asset A and asset B) based on the same microstructure measure (e.g.,
midpoint), but we also analyze lead-lag eﬀects between the two assets measured by diﬀerent
microstructure measures. For example, we analyze the lead-lag relationship between the

relationships at all, allowing us to disregard them in the subsequent analyses. As described
in Section 4.2, the basis of our empirical analyses are the daily values of ˆρ(ℓ∗) and LLS for
each instrument-measure combination.

This suggests that the transaction price of an
asset leads the midpoint of another asset. A theoretical explanation for this ﬁnding provides
the phenomenon of cross-impact which is closely related to the more known behavior of
price impact. In general, price impact describes the eﬀect of a trade moving the midpoint
of the same asset. For instance, a buy-initiated trade theoretically leads to a positive and
permanent change in the midpoint of the same asset. In turn, cross-impact denotes the eﬀect
of a trade in asset Y moving the midpoint of asset X. While the literature on price impact is
exhaustive (Dufour and Engle, 2000; Cont et al., 2014; Brogaard et al., 2019), the literature
on cross-impact is rather limited (Pasquariello and Vega, 2015; Benzaquen et al., 2017; Cont
et al., 2023). Similar to our results, the few existing studies ﬁnd that trade information of
one asset can provide information about future midpoints of another asset.
Furthermore, Figure 1b shows that there are lead-lag-relationships between instrument
combinations of the same price measures. Speciﬁcally, changes in the midpoint (transaction
price) of asset Y provide information about future changes in the midpoint (transaction
price) of asset X. This is consistent with other studies that apply the HY-estimator, such as
Huth and Abergel (2014) or Dao et al. (2018).
Furthermore, lead-lag relationships in our sample exist between OBI and the two price
measures. The LLS of 0.55 between OBI and the midpoint implies that the OBI of asset Y
leads the midpoint of asset X in at least 5% of all instrument combinations. In contrast, both

5.3. Variation of lead-lag eﬀects over time
As indicated by the previous ﬁgures, the daily estimates of the LLS exhibit some variation over time for each instrument-measure combination. To investigate whether there is
a systematic component aﬀecting the strength of lead-lag relationships over time, we analyze the variation of the daily LLS with respect to other daily variables such as (1) trading
volume, (2) intertrade duration, and (3) midpoint volatility. Speciﬁcally, we examine the
distribution of the daily LLS values for the strongest measure-combination in terms of LLS
that is midpoint (X), transaction price (Y). We focus solely on the strongest measure combination to ensure our analysis is not biased by temporal variations across diﬀerent measure
combinations. Moreover, we only consider instrument pairs with an average LLS value equal
to or higher than 1 to obtain suﬃcient variation in the LLS variable that we aim to explain.
Because the LLS is measured between two instruments, we need to consider the distribution of factors like trading volume for both assets simultaneously. To address this issue, we
proceed as follows: First, we calculate the daily (1) trading volume, (2) intertrade duration,
and (3) midpoint volatility for each instrument in our sample. Next, we compute percentile
ranks for each of these time series. Then, we add up both percentile ranks within each
instrument pair to derive a combined percentile rank. For each instrument pair, we bin the
daily combined percentile rank values into ﬁve buckets, with the ﬁrst bucket representing
the lowest percentile ranks. For instance, when using trading volume, bucket 1 represents
the 20% of trading days where the instrument pair has the lowest combined trading volume.
Following this approach, we divide all daily values of LLS for the measure combination
midpoint and transaction price into ﬁve bins according to the respective factor. The resulting
distributions within each bin can be found in three box plots in Figure A.5 in the Appendix.
35
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5043260

In general, the distributions are rather stable over the diﬀerent groups of trading days. Note
that we also used indicators other than the three presented in Figure A.5 to explain the
variation over time, such as average turnover per trade, relative quoted spread, and the
proportion of trade throughs for a given instrument. Since these factors also cannot explain
the LLS variation, we do not report these results. Overall, we do not ﬁnd evidence that
the LLS variation over time depends on the common level of trading activity, liquidity, or
volatility of both instruments.
While there seems to be no systematic component aﬀecting the strength of a lead-lag
relationship for the same instrument pair over time, the results from Section 5.2 indicate
that the strength systematically varies in the cross-section of instrument pairs. For instance,
the ﬁndings show that, regardless of the measure combination considered, lead-lag eﬀects
predominantly exist between two instruments that are fundamentally related. Moreover, the
leading position is typically held by heavily traded assets, such as the FESX. In summary, our
results suggest the presence of determining factors aﬀecting the strength of lead-lag eﬀects
across instrument pairs. Therefore, in the subsequent section, we proceed by analyzing these
determining drivers of lead-lag eﬀects across instrument pairs.
5.4. Determinants of lead-lag eﬀects in market microstructure
In the existing literature that examines lead-lag relationships on a daily frequency, several
studies show that large-cap stocks tend to lead small-cap stocks with regard to transaction
prices (Lo and MacKinlay, 1990; Chordia et al., 2011). In the literature that deals with
high-frequency data, Huth and Abergel (2014) and Buccheri et al. (2021) ﬁnd that assets
with high trading activity and liquidity, indicated by short intertrade durations and narrow
36
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5043260
Preprint not peer reviewed

bid-ask spreads, tend to be in a leading position when analyzing lead-lag eﬀects between
midpoints. Despite the considerable explanatory power of several liquidity indicators, both
studies emphasize that these indicators are insuﬃcient to fully account for the strength and
direction of lead-lag relationships. Besides liquidity, Huth and Abergel (2014) show that also
the relative diﬀerence in volatility between two assets can partially explain lead-lag behavior.
The authors ﬁnd that less volatile instruments tend to lead instruments with higher volatility.
However, the tendency of the more liquid and less volatile assets leading the less liquid and
more volatile assets is only supported by empirical evidence based on a single microstructure
measure (midpoints) and two types of ﬁnancial instruments (stocks and their corresponding
index futures). We want to make use of our broad asset universe and investigate whether
trading activity, liquidity, and volatility are signiﬁcant and universal drivers of lead-lag relationships in modern ﬁnancial markets. Therefore, we analyze whether this relationship
holds true across all types of ﬁnancial instruments and microstructure measures in our sample. Following Huth and Abergel (2014), we use several indicators that capture diﬀerent
aspects of trading activity, liquidity, and volatility. These indicators are explained in more

5.5. The predictive power of lead-lag strength
Knowledge about lead-lag relationships in ﬁnancial markets can be leveraged to forecast
the evolution of microstructure measures of lagging instruments, which may imply a violation
of the eﬃcient market hypothesis. To analyze the predictive power of lead-lag relationships
indicated by our constructed LLS, we build a simple prediction setup: For each of the
three measure combinations discussed in section 5.2, we calculate the average of the daily
LLS based on the ﬁrst ﬁve months in our sample (January to May 2021) and use the last
month (June 2021) for an out-of-sample prediction analysis. For each instrument-measure
combination where the average LLS is greater than zero, suggesting Yt leading Xt, we utilize
the leading time series Yt to predict the direction of the next change in the lagging time
series Xt. In particular, if we observe a change in the time series of the leading instrument
(e.g., change in the transaction price of FESX), we predict that the time series of the
lagging instrument (e.g., change in the midpoint of FDAX) will subsequently change in
the same direction if ˆρ(ℓ∗) > 0. Otherwise, we predict the lagging time series to move in

Preprint not peer reviewed

the opposite direction. Thereby, we constantly update the prediction for the change in the
lagging instrument and only consider the most recent change in the leading instrument. We
refer to this prediction model as LLS-based prediction approach.

the prediction accuracy for each instrument-measure combination. The prediction accuracy
is equal to the number of correct predictions when the leading instrument successfully indicated the direction of the next change of the lagging instrument divided by the total number
of changes in the lagging instrument. As a benchmark, we consistently predict the more

slower traders. The LLS proposed in this study is a suitable indicator for identifying these
lead-lag relationships. As a result, the LLS can be leveraged to derive statistical arbitrage
trading strategies in the high-frequency context.
6. Conclusion
In the high-frequency world of ﬁnancial markets, data can be transferred and analyzed in
mere fractions of a second. The technological advances allow investors to scan markets continuously and take into account more than just price information. Hence, information-based
47
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5043260
Preprint not peer reviewed

trading depends also on order book activity other than trades and might be more related to
patterns in the market microstructure rather than fundamental information. Using historical
trade and order book data from a variety of instruments and asset classes traded on Xetra
and Eurex, we analyze cross-asset lead-lag relationships between diﬀerent microstructure
measures capturing dimensions like prices, liquidity, imbalance, depth, and volatility.
Our results demonstrate that traders react to changes in various microstructure measures
across diﬀerent instruments and asset classes within very short time intervals, ranging from
a few microseconds up to a few seconds. Speciﬁcally, we ﬁnd evidence that changes in the
transaction price and OBI of a ﬁnancial instrument contain information about the future
midpoint development of another instrument. Furthermore, the transaction price volatility
of various assets is informative about future changes in the midpoint volatility of other
assets. We primarily observe these lead-lag relationships among ﬁnancial instruments that
are fundamentally related, such as those sharing common underlying components (e.g., the
DAX and Euro Stoxx 50 future) or those exposed to similar risk factors (e.g., long-term and
medium-term bond futures). We conﬁrm the ﬁndings of previous literature regarding the
determinants of the strength of lead-lag relationships, showing that instruments with higher
liquidity and trading activity tend to lead other instruments in most of the microstructure
measures examined. However, this ﬁnding primarily applies to equity-related instruments
such as stocks, equity futures, and equity ETFs.
In contrast, for bond futures, the less
liquid assets tend to lead, indicating that the results from previous studies focusing on the
determinants of lead-lag relationships in the equity market are not directly transferable to
other asset classes. Finally, our results reveal numerous lead-lag relationships that imply
signiﬁcant predictive power for future price and volatility changes. High-frequency traders
48
This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5043260
Preprint not peer reviewed

can leverage this predictive power to gain an edge over slower traders. Consequently, slower
traders providing liquidity should incorporate these risks by increasing bid-ask spreads or
reducing liquidity provision. Our proposed measure for lead-lag eﬀects, the LLS, can be
leveraged to identify these lead-lag relationships between two microstructure measures of two
ﬁnancial instruments. Speciﬁcally, the LLS is signiﬁcantly associated with the predictive
power of the leading time series concerning future changes in the lagging instrument.
Overall, we provide new insights on non-contemporaneous cross-asset correlations in modern market microstructure. We ﬁnd evidence of high-frequency lead-lag eﬀects in price, imbalance, and volatility measures between fundamentally related instruments. In sum, our
results suggest that short-term lead-lag eﬀects in the HFT environment still involve the exchange of fundamental information, but at a faster pace and derived by the most granular
pieces of market microstructure information, such as a single order submission.
Our ﬁndings can help market operators and regulators to anticipate and assess potential
contagion eﬀects and threats to market eﬃciency and stability. Speciﬁcally, our results can
be leveraged to introduce protective mechanisms that consider lead-lag relationships among
multiple microstructure measures and across diﬀerent instruments. Similarly, professional
investors can utilize the LLS to identify lead-lag relationships that imply the predictability
of the lagging asset.
Market makers can incorporate these results into their quotations,
while the most sophisticated high-frequency traders can seek to exploit this predictability
by developing proﬁtable strategies that encompass multiple instruments.
