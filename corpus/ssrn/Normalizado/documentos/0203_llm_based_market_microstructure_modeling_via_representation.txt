LLM-Based Market Microstructure Modeling
via Representation-Induced Stochastic Games
Yifan Guo
February 24, 2026
Abstract
We develop a unified framework for high-frequency market modeling that couples (i)
microstructure-level dynamics of the limit order book (LOB), (ii) large language model (LLM)
representation learning over message-level sequences, and (iii) game-theoretic reinforcement
learning (RL) for strategic agents. Our core object is a representation-induced stochastic
game: agents observe an embedding Zt = Φ(Xt−k+1:t) produced by a transformer-style
encoder Φ applied to LOB histories, and choose actions as functions of Zt (and private
states). We formalize equilibrium concepts restricted to the representation filtration, establish
existence under standard continuity/compactness hypotheses, and provide stability results
that connect Lipschitz geometry of Φ to smoothness of best responses, robustness of equilibria
under encoder perturbations, and convergence behavior of RL updates. We further derive a
representation–game–impact chain linking local embedding geometry to liquidity formation,
price impact, volatility, and mixing rates of the induced Markov kernel. Finally, we outline
an empirical blueprint using reconstructed message streams, simulator-in-the-loop RL, and
adversarial stress testing in embedding space. Placeholder figures and algorithms are included
for a reproducible paper structure.

Introduction
Market microstructure studies the mechanism by which orders are submitted, executed, cancelled,
and strategically arranged by market participants. At high frequencies, the price formation
process is not purely statistical: it reflects strategic interaction among heterogeneous agents
with latency constraints, inventory objectives, and asymmetric information. Classical models
(e.g., Glosten–Milgrom, Kyle, and Almgren–Chriss) are foundational but impose low-dimensional
and parametric state representations that struggle to accommodate modern electronic markets
driven by message-level dynamics and rapidly adapting algorithms.
Recent progress in representation learning suggests that transformer-like encoders can
compress LOB message sequences into low-dimensional embeddings that preserve economically
relevant structure: directional order-flow pressure, queue resilience, latent liquidity regimes,
and adverse selection. In parallel, reinforcement learning provides tools for dynamic control
(execution, market-making) under uncertainty. However, in markets, agents are strategic: payoffs
depend on others’ policies, so learning and equilibrium become coupled.
Contributions.
This paper proposes a representation-induced stochastic game framework:
1. We define a representation filtration and formulate a Markov (stochastic) game where the
public state is Zt = Φ(Xt−k+1:t).
2

2. We study equilibrium existence and stability when strategies are restricted to depend on the
embedding.
3. We analyze how embedding geometry controls (i) sensitivity of best responses, (ii) stability of
volatility/impact, (iii) mixing of representation dynamics, and (iv) convergence of RL updates
with Markovian noise.
4. We provide a practical empirical protocol: message reconstruction, encoder training, simulatorbased multi-agent RL, and adversarial perturbations in embedding space.

Existence and Stability of Representation-Based Equilibria
5.1
Existence
Assumption 5.1 (Action Sets and Continuity). For each i, the action set Ai is nonempty,
compact, and convex. For each i, the payoff Ui(ai, a−i; z) is continuous in (ai, a−i, z) and concave
in ai for fixed (a−i, z).
Theorem 5.2 (Existence of Representation-Based Nash Equilibrium). Under Assumption 5.1,
for every z ∈S, there exists at least one representation-based Nash equilibrium a⋆(z).
Proof. By compactness and convexity of Ai and continuity/concavity of Ui, each best-response
correspondence is nonempty, convex-valued, and upper hemicontinuous. Kakutani’s fixed-point
theorem implies existence of a fixed point of the product correspondence, which is a Nash
equilibrium at state z.

Empirical Framework and Algorithms
9.1
Data Pipeline (Message Reconstruction)
We assume access to message-level LOB data enabling reconstruction of Xt. A practical pipeline
includes: cleaning, synchronization, rebuilding K book levels, and extracting event sequences for
encoder training.
9.2
Encoder Training Objectives
Training Φ can be done via:
• self-supervised next-event prediction (order type/price level/size),
• masked event modeling on message streams,
• contrastive learning across regime labels (volatility/liquidity buckets),
• multi-task objectives including impact/volatility proxies.
9.3
Simulator-in-the-Loop Multi-Agent RL
We recommend a microstructure simulator implementing M with realistic queue priority, latency,
and partial fills. RL agents interact via embedding states.
9.4
Adversarial Stress Testing in Embedding Space
Embedding perturbations can model distribution shifts, adversarial liquidity events, or encoder
drift.

Limitations and Future Directions
11.1
Endogenous Representation Learning
A natural extension is to make Φ itself an equilibrium object: agents jointly optimize policies
and representations, leading to a fixed point in the product space of encoders and strategies.
This requires a game-theoretic treatment of evolving information structures.

11.2
Multi-Asset and Multi-Venue Structure
Real markets are fragmented. Extending Φ to encode multi-venue message streams and cross-asset
coupling can capture routing, latency races, and predatory strategies.
11.3
Global Dynamics and Rare Events
The present analysis emphasizes local Lipschitz/spectral conditions. Flash crashes and liquidity
crises likely require global nonlinear tools, potentially combining robust control, distributional
RL, and stress testing.
11.4
Interpretability
Understanding how embedding coordinates map to economic primitives (inventory pressure, adverse selection, queue imbalance) remains an open problem. Geometry-aware probes, information
bottlenecks, and causal interventions are promising directions.

Implementation Notes
• State construction: Ensure consistent normalization across assets/venues; avoid leakage
from future messages.
• Encoder training: Use time-split evaluation and regime-stratified validation (volatility/liquidity buckets).
• RL training: Prefer off-policy with replay in nonstationary settings; consider populationbased training for robustness.
• Stress testing: Evaluate sensitivity curves vs. ε for impact, drawdown, and fill risk.
