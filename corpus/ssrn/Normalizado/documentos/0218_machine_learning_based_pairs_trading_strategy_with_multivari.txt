 
 
 
PROFITABILITY OF PAIRS TRADING STRATEGY IN AN ILLIQUID 
MARKET WITH MULTIPLE SHARE CLASSES 
 
 
 
John Paul Broussard – Mika Vaihekoski* 
 
Comments welcome. 
 
May 31, 2012 
 
 
 
Abstract 
 
We investigate the practical issues of implementing the self-financing pairs 
portfolio trading strategy presented by Gatev et al. (2006). We also provide new 
evidence on the profitability of the pairs trading under different weighting 
structures and trade initiation conditions. Using data from the Finnish stock 
market over the period 1987 to 2008, we find pairs trading to be profitable even 
after allowing for a one day delay in the trade initiation after the signal. On 
average, the annualized return can be as high as 12.5%, though requiring trading 
on days a pair is traded lowers the return approximately by three percentage 
points. On the other hand, lowering the threshold for opening a pair is found to 
increase returns even after accounting for trading costs, indicating that there 
might be a more optimal trade initiation threshold available than that presented 
in earlier literature. The profits are not related to market risk. Pairs trading 
strategy is found to produce positive alpha during the sample period.

INTRODUCTION 
 
One of the popular trading strategies found in a statistical arbitrageur's toolbox is pairs trading. 
Not only is pairs trading a popular trading strategy, it is simple to implement. An arbitrageur 
finds two stocks whose prices move together over an indicated historical time period.  If the pair 
prices deviate wide enough, the strategy calls for shorting the increasing-price security, while 
simultaneously buying the declining-price security. The idea behind the pair trade is to profit 
from convergence forces that eliminate short-term price deviations in favor of long-term 
historical pricing relationships. In a pricing world that is relatively efficient, simple strategies 
based on mean-reversion concepts should not generate consistent profits. Gatev, Goetzmann, 
and Rouwenhorst (2006), however, document pairs trading generates consistent arbitrage profits 
in the U.S. equity market which is considered the most efficient and liquid in the world.  Do and 
Faff (2010) extend Gatev et al. and show that although profitability of pairs trading has declined 
generally, the strategy generates substantial performance during turbulent market horizons, 
including the recent financial crisis. 
 
Since the publication Gatev et al. (2006) surprisingly few studies have investigated pairs trading in 
markets other than that of the USA.  A couple of exceptions are Perlin, (2009) who evaluates 
pairs trading in Brazil, and Andrade, di Pietro and Seasholes (2005), who evaluate pairs trading 
profitability in Taiwan. This could be due to the computational complexity of estimating profits 
from the pairs trading despite the prima facie simplicity of the strategy. In this paper we discuss 
some of issues in the practical implementation of the strategy not mentioned in Gatev et al., as 
well as analyze the role of the pairs trade initiation criteria and the effect on returns of requiring 
trading in stocks selected for pairs on days when a pair is opened. Furthermore, we provide 
computer code for the implementation. Finally, we provide evidence on the profitability of the 
pairs trading strategy in Finland, a stock market with much less liquidity than that found in the 
United States. Since the pairs trading strategy requires implementing two trades instead of one, 
evaluating performance in a less liquid market provides additional evidence regarding the 
strategy's global implementable efficacy.  
 
There are two additional reasons why evaluating the pairs trading strategy in Finland is 
interesting. First, the time period evaluated, 1987 to 2008, covers not only the global boom and 
bust in the technology sector, but also the financial institution crisis of 1990 and 2008 that 
impacted investors in Finnish equities. Evaluating the pairs trading strategy in such stressed 
market environments provides information to those evaluating the strategy's potential to act as a

risk management alternative to traditional purely directional trading strategies. Secondly, a unique 
institutional characteristic regarding common and preferred shares in Finland allows a closer 
examination of potential causes for price deviation offered not only by similar securities, but by 
securities that have access to the same cash flow source. 
 
The results of this effort show the pairs trading strategy is persistently profitable even in a market 
with reduced liquidity. Significant profits also seem to be generated by taking advantage of price 
deviations between common and preferred shares of the same firm. The contribution to the 
literature of the latter result is important because one of the reasons suggested for profitable 
trading opportunities due to pair price deviation is trader risk aversion regarding fundamental 
value calculation uncertainty. If the trader's assessment of fundamental value is highly uncertain, 
then prices may deviate persistently because trades made to take advantage of mispricing are not 
undertaken. Trader’s risk aversion, therefore, potentially reduces the profit potential of the pairs 
trading strategy because lack of trading activity may allow non-convergence of disequilibrium 
asset prices.  The finding that profits can be made from price deviation between assets with 
claims on the same cash flow source provides additional support for this naïve investment 
trading strategy.1

2.1 
Pairs methodology 
 
Similar to Gatev et al. (2006), our implementation of the pairs trading strategy proceeds in two 
stages. First, pairs of stocks are selected for trading using a twelve month period of data, termed 
the formation period. Second, trades are made on the pairs during the six months following the

Using the stocks that are traded throughout the 18 month period, one finds first those pairs of

distance measure, which is defined as the sum of squared deviations between the two normalized

series to construct the series in the manner similar to Gatev et al. 
 
Once the chosen number of pairs of stock series has been determined, the question is whether 
one can trade profitability on them during the trading period. The trading strategy is based on the 
idea that if the prices diverge, there is a profit to be made because the prices are expected to 
converge sooner or later. In practice, the pair is opened if a trading signal is observed. A positive 
trading signal is indicated if pairs’ normalized prices diverge more than two times the standard 
deviation of the price differences during the formation period.3  The trade is opened by buying 
the stock with lower normalized price, and selling (short) the higher priced equity. The long-short 
transactions are effected to yield a net position of zero. Thus, the trade is considered selffinanced. The pair trade is then closed by reversing the opening transactions once the normalized 
prices cross the two-standard deviation threshold.  If a pair remains open at the end of the 
trading period, it is closed whether or not price convergence occurred.

Using the return series for each pair, we can calculate the return for a portfolio of the pairs 
positions. Following Gatev et al. we use two different weighting schemes. The first weighting 
scheme is called the committed capital scheme, which essentially commits equal amounts of capital to 
each one of the pairs. If the pair is not opened, or it is closed during the trading period, the 
capital is still committed to the pair. Similar to Gatev et al. we assume zero return for non-open 
pairs, although in practice one could earn returns on idle capital. In our second and main 
approach, we utilize a fully invested weighting scheme. The fully invested scheme is less conservative as 
it assumes capital is always divided between the pairs that are open.

(open) pairs is given the same weight at any given time. Although computationally convenient, it 
requires frequent trading to keep the weights in balance, which ultimately raises costs. 
 
Our second approach, labeled as the value-weighted approach, utilizes equation (2) to calculate 
relative weights for each pair where weights for each pair follows wpt=wpt–1(1+rpt–1) with a starting 
value of one. In practice, we assume that each pair is given the same weight at the beginning of 
the trading period. If a pair is not opened, the return is zero, and thus the weight does not 
change. This implies that for the fully invested weighting scheme, the money from a closed pair is 
invested in the other pairs that are open (according to their relative shares). If a pair is re-opened, 
the money is invested back by redistributing the investment between the pairs according to their 
relative weights, i.e., wpt divided by the sum of the weights. Similarly, if a pair is not opened until 
later in the trading period, the weight will be one in absolute terms (as for others), but in relative 
terms the weight has likely changed.

the chosen number of pairs). As a result, their technique potentially allows for one stock to be 
selected in more than one pair (possibly even on opposite sides of the trade). Although this 
parallel rolling selection technique may be suitable in a marketplace with a vast array of stocks 
available, a sequential selection approach is more suitable in a marketplace where the number of 
stocks is limited, as is the case in Finland. As a result, our implementation differs slightly from 
the previous literature in that the time horizon of formation and trading periods are rolled 
forward by six months, instead of on a monthly calendar.  Specifically, the pairs are formed using

2.2 
Case Finland 
 
Finland is one of the Nordic countries that showed high economic growth after the Second 
World War. As with many other countries around the world, the last couple of decades have been 
a period of extremely high growth both in terms of economic activity and financial markets. 
There are, however, several features that differentiate Finland from other countries, especially the 
US, during the sample period analyzed in this paper, 1987-2008.  
 
First, the Finnish stock market (established in 1912) was in many ways underdeveloped and 
segmented from international markets until the 1990s.  The first steps in the integration process 
were taken in the early 1980s, e.g., when foreign interest in Finnish stocks started to increase. As 
a result, foreigners started to buy Finnish stocks, which led the government to restrict foreign 
ownership to 20 percent (later raised to 40 percent) of the total equity. The shares that the 
company made available to all investors (including Finnish investors) were labeled as unrestricted. 
Those only available to Finnish investors were labeled as restricted stocks. Both instruments were 
traded and priced separately by the stock exchange from 1984 forward. Unrestricted stocks 
traded typically with a premium to the restricted ones.  
 
Even though allowed before the 1980s, Finnish companies also started to issue in increasing 
numbers preference shares – common shares with lower voting rights than normal common stocks, 
but with often a higher dividend payment (hence the name).9 These stocks were often also 
available in unrestricted and restricted versions. As a result, some companies (e.g., Nokia) had as 
many as four listed stock instruments (common and preference, both available as restricted and 
unrestricted). However, the restrictions on foreign ownership were abolished from the beginning 
of 1993 and the number of listed stock series was cut almost by half (see Vaihekoski, 2004, for 
details). In addition to removing the ownership restrictions, the government also removed the 
one percent of the value tax levied on all trades (i.e., stamp duty) conducted on the stock 
exchange in an effort to increase on-exchange trading.

Second, liquidity in the Finnish stock market was fairly low, especially in the early 1990s due to

A third differentiating fact that directly relates to the efficacy of pairs trading profitability in 
Finland is connected to the time span short-selling was officially allowed in the Finnish stock 
market. Organized short-selling officially began at the Finnish exchange in May 22, 1995 when 
Suomen Optiomeklarit Ltd (SOM; which later merged with the Helsinki Stock Exchange, HEX) 
started trading LEX-short-selling instruments. These instruments are formed by combining two 
zero-priced forward contracts, one of which matures immediately and the other within the next 
nine months. The quoted price for the contracts was the premium paid by the buyer to the 
seller.10 In addition, the buyer provided collateral to the exchange (typically 125 % of the value of 
the loan), which acted as the counterparty between both sides of the trade. These instruments 
were available for the most liquid stocks. Part of the trading took place outside the exchange, 
although one had to pay stamp duty on trades outside the exchange.  
 
Initially the trading was partly hurt by unfavorable tax treatment of the dividends for those 
lending the stocks. The legislation was changed in 1997. At the same time stamp duty on short 
sales were mostly abolished, although the official organized trading was still favored as the stamp 
duty on OTC-trades remained if the loan was for more than ten trades (later changed in 2007). 
HEX set up a lending pool to facilitate lending of the stocks in year 2000 and further 
enhancements were made later in April 2004. After the merger of the HEX and SOM, the trading 
of LEX-instruments was moved to Stockholm in 2005, where short selling trading takes places 
under the name “Finnish Stock Loan”.

found in the US market, one has to keep in mind that short-selling could also take place as an 
OTC-trade (off-exchange) between two parties.11 It was also fairly common that within a bank,

the early 1990s. GDP growth was negative for a number of years beginning late 1990 and lasting

First, Finnish interest rates were unusually high before the crises began.  Policy makers decided to

Ultimately, the currency was allowed to float in the middle of the crisis in September 1991. 
Second, the Finnish banking sector experienced a severe crisis of its own due to lax lending 
policies and unpreparedness for the structural changes sweeping the industry in the late 1980s 
and early 1990s. Third, the collapse of the Russian Empire resulted in a vast reduction in Finnish 
exports. After the recession, however, the growth in the economy has been especially strong.  
One company in particular, Nokia, has been credited with the economic turnaround.

Main results 
 
Table 1 shows descriptive statistics for the monthly percentage (net) returns for the TOP 5 pairs 
trading strategies under two different weighting schemes. Monthly returns are calculated using 
daily cumulative return indices. The TOP 5 pairs are chosen to be those with the smallest 
distance measure. Panel A lists the results for the investment scenario allowing same-day

implementing the trade after seeing the trading signal. In addition, it allows one to form a more 
conservative view on the profitability as the lag allows for one to take into account potential 
difficulties in executing the trade as well as the effect of bid-ask spread in the market.  
 
As indicated in Table 1, the monthly net returns are positive and significantly different from zero 
across portfolio formation measures when the pair can be initiated same day. All but one case are 
significant even when the one day lag approach is evaluated. As expected the results in Panel A 
are superior to those in Panel B, indicating the importance of rapid execution of the trade.

Table 3 shows that on average the returns have been positive and typically higher during the first

surprising as it appears the strategy is potentially generating higher profits more recently. If the 
efficiency of the Finnish stock market has improved, as is the case with improved liquidity, lower 
spreads, and easier short-selling during the second sub-period, one would expect profit 
opportunities to decrease for both approaches. Since the difference in returns between fully 
invested and committed capital approaches has narrowed, one can speculate that this is, at least 
to some degree, due to the improved efficiency, reduction of multiple share series, or both. 
 
Table 3 also shows that in general, the equally weighted approach yields higher returns than the 
value-weighted approach ceteris paribus. The likely reason for this is that the cumulative returns for 
the top five pairs have not diverged much during the trading period, giving more weight on 
highly successful pair(s), or that the cumulative returns converged lowering the weight-induced 
gains made early in the trading period.  
 
Panel A in Table 3 also shows that the average excess return on pairs trading has been at par or 
slightly above that of the market portfolio. Similar to Gatev et al., the standard deviation is also 
lower than that of the market, leading to higher Sharpe ratios. This is the case also in two subperiods, but the committed capital approach provides higher Sharpe ratios than fully invested 
approach during the latter period, whereas the opposite is true for the first period. This is mostly 
driven by lower volatility which is not completely offset by the lower returns. Finally, in all cases 
but one, the pairs trading strategy produces negative first order autocorrelation into the result 
series, contrary to Gatev et al. The reason for this may be a manifestation of a bid-ask spread 
bounce in the data.  
 
Finding positive returns using a self-financing trading strategy raises the question whether the 
returns can be explained solely by the risk taken. To see if the returns are driven by the pair 
trading strategy’s exposure to market risk, we estimate the risk-adjusted returns (alpha) and 
systematic market risk (beta) using the CAPM model. The OLS results from the excess return 
market model regression are also reported in Table 3. The standard errors have been adjusted for 
heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation using Newey-West adjustment with three lags.  
 
The results show that the returns are not driven by the market risk over the whole sample period. 
The beta is not found significant in any of the cases at the five percent significance level over the 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=1729182

 
14
full sample period. This means that over the long term, the pairs strategy has also been market 
neutral, which is often a desirable property for many hedge funds strategies. Analyzing the 
subperiods, we can see that the fully invested approach had statistically significant negative betas 
during the first subperiod, but exhibited insignificant betas in the second subperiod. The strategy 
appears to have become more ‘market-neutral’ over time.  
 
Analysing risk-adjusted returns (alphas) we can see that they are all positive (even for subperiods) 
and all but one statistically significant at 5% level for the full sample period. Somewhat 
surprisingly, alphas for the fully invested approach alphas seem to be clearly lower during the 
latter period, whereas the alphas for the committed capital approach are slightly higher. This is 
again consistent with the idea, that the first sub-period may have offered a few extremely good 
trading opportunities, potentially due to multiple share classes, which did not exist in the second 
sub-period.  
 
 
4.3 
Additional analysis and robustness checks 
 
We also conduct a number of additional analysis and tests. First, we analyze the stocks selected 
for the pairs trading in more detail. The results are consistent with our expectation that many 
pairs are formed between different series or stock classes of a company’s equity. In fact, 55.2 
percent of the 210 pairs (42 trading periods times five pairs in each portfolio) are formed 
between company’s different share classes. More detailed analysis shows that 88.1 percent of the 
best pairs are formed by combining two different share classes for one company, after which 
their share decrease and only 23.8 percent of the least good pairs in the portfolios are formed in 
this manner. 
 
Second, we analyze the effect of weighting approach within a pair on the results. Using equal 
weights for long and short positions in each pair throughout the trading period, which 
corresponds to a net zero capital position, did not qualitatively change the results if trading costs 
were not taken into consideration. In all cases, the returns are slightly lower but the difference is 
not major. Moreover, as stated earlier, keeping the weights equal within a pair requires a lot of 
trading in practice, which would in effect lower the returns even further. Thus, this approach is 
likely to have limited interest among traders.

Third, we study the effect of different opening criterion (trigger level) on the results. Originally, 
the pairs trading signal was observed when the normalized prices diverged more than two times 
the standard deviation of the price differences during the formation period. Here we allow the 
criterion to vary from 0.5 to three times the standard deviation. We expect the returns to be 
higher when a lower criterion is used if trading costs are not taken into account. 
 
The results show that the smaller the criterion, the higher the average return. This is consistent 
with our hypothesis as lower criterion allows one to profit potentially from smaller price 
divergences. However, it requires potentially more trading costs that might not be offset by the 
returns generated. Next we re-estimate the model under the assumption that trading costs are 0.2 
percent per trade.17 The average returns are naturally lower, but somewhat surprisingly, the 
average return is still highest for the smallest criterion. This suggests that in practice one could be 
able to find an optimal criterion (trigger level) to initiate pairs trading as a function of trading 
costs and assumptions of the underlying pricing process.  
 
Gatev et al. (2006) note the similarities in pairs selection by using a cointegration approach and 
those selected using the squared summation of normalized price deviations (SSD). The idea 
behind using cointegration in pairs trading is that there is at least one common factor that drives 
asset prices to move together.  When pair prices diverge, the common factor acts to eliminate the 
divergence.  One potential problem with using cointegration to select pairs can be found in 
Hakkio and Rush (1991), who indicate cointegration is a long-run phenomenon that requires long 
spans of data to make proper common factor inferences.  Since we use daily data, and only over a 
year span of time to form potential trading pairs, empirical tests may not reveal a cointegrated 
relationship.  In fact, we find only 54% of the pairs in our sample are cointegrated during the 
formation period. Pairs appear to be moving together enough to be recognized by the SSD 
approach, but not moving together enough to be recognized through cointegration tests. 
 
In addition, we find the use of a closely related measure, half-life, to provide trading signals too 
often.18 Namely, we calculated the half-life to be only two days, on average, for those pairs 
exhibiting a cointegrated relationship.  Using the half-life as a rule to close the pair, would 
generate higher transactions costs than those indicated by the SSD framework because the

Finally, we study whether the profits are driven by the lack of liquidity in the Finnish market. In

even after accounting for infrequent trading.

CONCLUSIONS 
 
In this paper, we reviewed the pairs trading strategy approach in Gatev et al. (2006) and discussed 
its implementation in detail on the Finnish stock market. Combined with the fact that the Finnish 
stock market is more inefficient and many companies listed in Finland have multiple stock series 
offering potential for pairs trading, one may expect ex ante to observe potentially high returns 
from the strategy.  
 
The pairs trading strategy was tested using a sample period of 21 years from 1988 to 2008. The 
results show that the pairs trading strategy produces statistically significantly positive returns 
during the sample period. The results confirm the finding of Gatev et al. that the pairs trading is 
indeed a profitably strategy, and not specific to the US market. The returns also do not appear to 
be driven by the systematic market risk factor. The strategies were also able to provide statistically 
significant alpha during the sample period.  
 
We also conducted additional analysis on the strategy. First, we analyzed periods with extremely 
high returns in detail. The results indicate that the multiple share class environment in Finland 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=1729182

 
17
offered a great opportunity for pairs trading. As a result, pairs trading strategy should also work 
well in countries where multiple share classes are typical, e.g. China. 
 
In addition, using different threshold criteria to initiate the pairs, we found that the two standard 
deviation trade trigger criterion used in Gatev et al. could be reduced with a commensurate 
increase in pairs trade profits in theory, although in practice the benefits are partly offset by the 
costs. Somewhat surprisingly, our results indicate that, even after taking into account trading 
costs, the average return is higher for lower thresholds.  
 
The results of this paper also indicate high returns to pairs trading may be improved by using 
lower than traditional trade initiation thresholds. There may be, however, implementation 
problems that could cause observed returns to be lower than expected if the strategy is actually 
put into practice in an illiquid market. We analyzed this issue by imposing an ex post requirement 
that the trading volume be positive when a pair was opened. The results show that the average 
return was lowered by approximately three percentage points.  
 
The analysis in this paper uses data that ends in 2008, which is six years after that used in Gatev 
et al.  Further analysis on the subperiods, drivers of the profitability, time series optimality of the 
trading signal in different market conditions, potential for fine-tuning the trading criteria (e.g., as 
a function of bid-ask spread and time available until the end of the trading period), a more 
concentrated effort to detangle the effects of multiple stock series, as well as an additional 
evaluation of thin trading and transaction costs are left for future research.
