Optimal Statistical Arbitrage for
Generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process
with L´evy Stable Distribution
M´ıngsh¯u W´ang∗

We explore optimal statistical arbitrage for the generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
(OU) process driven by a L´evy stable distribution. Former results by Bertram
(2010) have limitations from the non-Gaussian nature of asset returns. By
extending the framework to incorporate L´evy stable processes, which better
capture the heavy-tailed characteristics of financial returns, we confirm that
optimal entry and exit points lie symmetrically around the mean, consistent
with previous Gaussian-based findings. We also derive first-passage time,
expected return, and variance under the new L´evy stable driven process.
1. Introduction
Statistical arbitrage has been a popular strategy for trading since the 1980s (Feng et al.,
2016). This strategy leverages fluctuations in price action irrespective of overall trends.
Methods like error correction models give StatArb traders a convenient way to short
relatively overvalued assets and long undervalued ones.
Bertram (2010) derived optimal levels where StatArb traders could enter and exit
trades. With a Gaussian driven Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process as basis, he discovered
that optimal entry and exit levels in fact lie symmetrical around each other. His result
contrasted popular StatArb strategies that used asymmetric entry and exit.
His findings have a key weakness, however, in that he relied on Gaussian driven OU
processes.
It is well known (??Officer, 1972) that asset price returns follow a L´evy
stable distribution more closely.
Stein et al. (2021) discuss properties of generalized
OU processes, including L´evy Stable and Poisson driven ones among others.
Their
framework lends a convenient base for us to extend Bertram’s results. We will show that
his findings do extend to the general case where a L´evy stable distribution drives the

6. Conclusion
We have shown that the optimal statistical arbitrage strategy for a generalized OU
process with a L´evy stable process has symmetric entry and exit levels a and m that
maximize expected return and Sharpe ratio. We have thus extended Bertram’s results
into a general case. Statistical arbitrage practitioners can still rely on finding symmetric
entry and exit even as asset returns follow a L´evy stable distribution.
The analytical derivation above concludes that m∗= −a∗, enforcing symmetry in the
optimal trading strategy. However, this outcome emerges from the specific structure of
the expected return function µ(a, m, c) and the underlying assumptions. The current
formulation of µ(a, m, c) does not explicitly account for the skewness βL of the driving
process. To allow for asymmetric optimal levels, future research may modify the expected
return to incorporate the directional bias introduced by βL. For example, one introduce
weighting factors that reflect the skewness:
µ(a, m, c) = c1(m −a −c)(1 + βL)
aα + mα

This modification breaks the inherent symmetry, allowing m∗and a∗to diverge.
