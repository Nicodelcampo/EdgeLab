DiceStock: Emergent Market Microstructure
from Stochastic Dice Mechanics
Gregory Ashaun Nelson
February 2026
Abstract. We present DiceStock, a multi-agent market microstructure simulator in which all
agent behavior is driven by a 100×100 stochastic dice grid partitioned into 10 functional bands.
Each simulation tick, 10,000 dice are independently rolled, producing band-level statistics that
govern heterogeneous agent trading strategies, volatility regimes, sentiment dynamics, and shortsqueeze mechanics. Despite the simplicity of the underlying random mechanism, the model
reproduces key stylized facts of financial markets: fat-tailed return distributions, volatility
clustering with slow autocorrelation decay, realistic volume dynamics, and endogenous liquidity
crises. We validate the model by running 3.6 million ticks (~37 simulated years) and performing
comprehensive statistical analysis across 13 categories of market properties. The DiceStock
framework demonstrates that complex market phenomena can emerge from minimal agent
heterogeneity driven by shared stochastic signals, contributing to the agent-based computational
economics literature on emergent price discovery.
Keywords: agent-based modeling, market microstructure, stylized facts, emergent behavior, order-driven
markets, computational economics
1. Introduction
Financial markets exhibit a remarkable set of statistical regularities—known as stylized facts—that
persist across asset classes, time periods, and geographic regions (Cont, 2001). These include
heavy-tailed return distributions, volatility clustering, absence of return autocorrelation, and
asymmetric volatility response. Reproducing these properties in simulation remains a central
challenge in agent-based computational economics (ACE).
Traditional ACE models achieve realism through sophisticated agent decision rules: trend followers,
fundamentalists, noise traders, and market makers interact through complex behavioral
specifications (LeBaron, 2006). While successful, these models often require extensive calibration
and raise questions about which agent features are truly necessary for emergent realism.
DiceStock takes a different approach. All agent behavior derives from a single shared stochastic
mechanism: a 100×100 grid of dice, each rolled independently every tick with values uniformly
distributed on [1, 20]. The grid is partitioned into 10 horizontal bands of 1,000 dice each, where
each band drives a distinct market function:

2
Bands 1–3: Directional traders at three timescales (scalpers, swing, position)
Band 4: Fundamental value anchor
Band 5: Short sellers and squeeze mechanics
Band 6: Market makers providing liquidity
Band 7: Volatility regime multiplier
Band 8: Exogenous event/shock generator
Band 9: Market sentiment
Band 10: Meta-regime (trend persistence and reversal probability)
Contributions. (1) We introduce the DiceStock architecture, demonstrating that a dice-griddriven multi-agent system produces realistic market dynamics. (2) We provide comprehensive
statistical validation across 13 categories of market properties. (3) We show that the core stylized
facts emerge from agent heterogeneity alone, without regime switching or squeeze mechanics
activating.
2. Related Work
Agent-based computational economics. The Santa Fe Artificial Stock Market (Arthur et al.,
1997) first demonstrated that heterogeneous adaptive agents could generate realistic price
dynamics. LeBaron (2006) surveys the field, noting that agent heterogeneity—not complexity—
drives emergent realism. Lux and Marchesi (1999, 2000) produce fat-tailed returns through
endogenous switching between fundamentalists and chartists. Farmer and Joshi (2002) show that
the ecology of strategies determines aggregate behavior. Chiarella et al. (2009) provide a
comprehensive heterogeneous agent framework.
Stylized facts. Cont (2001) provides the canonical enumeration: (1) fat tails with power-law index
2–5, (2) absence of return autocorrelation, (3) volatility clustering with long memory, (4) volumevolatility correlation, (5) asymmetric volatility, (6) aggregational Gaussianity.
Order book models. Bouchaud et al. (2002) and Smith et al. (2003) establish characteristic
spread and depth dynamics. Cont et al. (2010) develop a stochastic limit order book model.
DiceStock implements full price-time priority matching.

3. Model Description
3.1 Dice Grid
The core mechanism is a grid G of 100 × 100 = 10,000 cells. Each tick, every cell is assigned a value
g ~ Uniform(1, Ellipsis, 20) using a seeded Mulberry32 PRNG. The grid is partitioned into 10 bands
of 1,000 cells. For each band, we compute the mean μb, standard deviation σb, and 100 column
means cb,k. With 1,000 dice, the band mean has σ(μb) ≈ 0.183, while column means (10 dice) have
σ(cb,k) ≈ 1.83—providing much higher variance for directional signals.
3.2 Ten Bands
Bands 1–3 (Directional Traders): Each column represents one agent. Direction: BUY if c ≥ 14,
SELL if c ≤ 7. Band 1 (scalpers): every tick, 10-share, 60% market orders. Band 2 (swing): every 13
ticks, 80-share. Band 3 (position): every 65 ticks, 400-share.
Band 4 (Fundamental): Maintains latent fundamental Ft with drift proportional to μ4 − 10.5.
Value agents trade when |Pt − Ft|/Ft > 0.02.
Band 5 (Short Sellers): Tracks short interest SIt. Squeeze activates when four conditions are
met: utilization > 0.45, recent price rise > 3%, sentiment > 0.6, and κt > 1.2.
Band 6 (Market Makers): Refreshes all quotes every tick. Spread and depth depend on band
statistics. Withdraws liquidity during stress.
Band 7 (Volatility): Piecewise linear mapping of μ7 to κt ∈ [0.2, 5.0], with κ = 1.0 for μ7 ∈ [8, 13].
Bands 8–10: Events (extreme column means trigger shocks), sentiment (exponential smoothing),
and regime (trend persistence and reversal probability).
3.3 Order Book and Tick Lifecycle
DiceStock implements a continuous double auction with price-time priority. Each tick: (A) roll dice,
update environment; (B) generate orders from all bands; (C) match market orders (shuffled), then
limit orders; (D) update history, candles, delay buffers.
4. Methodology
We run 3,600,000 ticks (seed 42) corresponding to 9,230 trading days (~36.6 years), capturing all
38 internal state variables per tick. We compute log returns rt = ln(Pt/Pt−1) and evaluate 13
categories: summary statistics, return distribution, fat tails (Hill estimator, QQ), volatility clustering

6. Emergent Phenomena
6.1 Stylized Facts Without Regime Switching
The most striking finding: DiceStock reproduces fat tails (κ = 11.07), volatility clustering (ACF =
0.48), and power-law tails (α = 2.74) without κt ever deviating from 1.0. Agent heterogeneity
alone—operating at multiple timescales through a shared order book—is sufficient.
6.2 The Crash-Recovery Cycle
The simulation exhibits a remarkable trajectory: crash from $100 to $1.43 (years 0–2), depression
at $2–$5 (years 2–9), recovery to $100+ (year 10), then stable fluctuation (years 10–37).

7. Parameter Sensitivity
The band mean concentration problem: With 1,000 dice, σ(μ) ≈ 0.183, confining band means
to a narrow range. The kappa map assigns κ = 1 for μ7 ∈ [8,13]—never escaped. Design fix: use
column means (10 dice, σ ≈ 1.83) or narrow the unity-kappa range.
Other sensitivities: agent frequencies (1/13/65 create multi-scale structure), fundamental drift rate
(0.002), and squeeze thresholds (kappa > 1.2 requirement prevents activation).
8. Discussion
Strengths: Simplicity (all from uniform dice), reproducibility (seeded PRNG), efficiency (8,847
ticks/sec), full observability of internal state.
Limitations: Inactive regime dynamics, strong bid-ask bounce (−0.53 vs. empirical −0.1 to −0.3),
high annualized vol (459%), no agent learning, single asset, no wealth dynamics.
Future work: Agent adaptation via RL, multi-asset extension, wealth dynamics, calibration to
empirical distributions, network effects.

17
9. Conclusion
DiceStock demonstrates that independent uniform dice rolls can drive a multi-agent market
simulator reproducing key statistical properties of real financial markets: excess kurtosis of 11.07,
volatility clustering with ACF 0.48 at lag 1 persisting to lag 100, Hill tail indices of 2.7, and positive
volume-volatility correlation—all without GARCH, stochastic volatility, or regime switching.
The central insight: the most important stylized facts emerge from agent heterogeneity
operating through a shared order book, not from sophisticated mechanisms. The
minimum ingredients are: (1) heterogeneous agents at multiple timescales, (2) a limit order book
with price-time priority, and (3) a fundamental value anchor.
