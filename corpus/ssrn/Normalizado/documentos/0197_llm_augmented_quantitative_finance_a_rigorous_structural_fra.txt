LLM-Augmented Quantitative Finance:
A Rigorous Structural Framework for Market State Inference
and Forecasting
Yifan Guo
December 17, 2025
Abstract
Traditional quantitative finance relies on structured numerical data and explicitly specified stochastic
dynamics, often failing to incorporate unstructured information such as news, policy language, and market
narratives. Large language models (LLMs) can extract semantic representations from text but, when used
as end-to-end forecasters, typically lack probabilistic coherence, interpretability, and stability guarantees.
This paper proposes a rigorous LLM-augmented framework in which LLMs operate as latent state
inference engines rather than direct predictors. Textual information is mapped into a low-dimensional
semantic state that conditions regime-switching and state-space dynamics governing returns and volatility. The design preserves probabilistic structure while enabling disciplined incorporation of narrative
information unavailable to purely numerical models. We establish formal properties including validity of
text-conditioned transition kernels, exact Bayesian filtering recursions, explicit Lipschitz stability with
respect to semantic perturbations, and identifiability under a transparent normalization. The framework is
suited to leakage-controlled empirical evaluation and reproducible out-of-sample forecasting.
Keywords: Large Language Models; Market Narratives; Regime Switching; State-Space Models; Volatility
Forecasting.

Introduction
Quantitative finance has historically relied on mathematically explicit models grounded in stochastic processes
and econometric structure. These models are effective when relevant information is largely captured by
historical prices, volumes, and macro series. In practice, however, major market repricings are frequently
driven by narrative shifts: changes in policy guidance, evolving macroeconomic interpretation, and forwardlooking corporate language. Such information is embedded in unstructured text and is not fully represented
by contemporaneous numerical features.
Recent applications of large language models (LLMs) to finance often employ them as end-to-end predictors,
mapping text directly to future returns, volatility, or prices. While such approaches may yield signals, they
1

typically compromise interpretability, probabilistic discipline, and stability under distribution shift. This
paper adopts a structural alternative: the LLM should augment quantitative models by inferring a semantic
state that influences, but does not replace, classical stochastic dynamics.
Our contribution is structural rather than algorithmic. By separating semantic inference from probabilistic
forecasting, the proposed framework clarifies the role of text in market dynamics and supports formal analysis
of coherence, inference, stability, and identifiability that is not available in end-to-end LLM predictors. The
resulting model admits exact Bayesian filtering and yields explicit sensitivity bounds that directly motivate
regularization and evaluation design.

5
Empirical Evaluation Protocol
This section specifies a leakage-controlled evaluation protocol suitable for disciplined out-of-sample forecasting.
The intent is not to prescribe a single dataset, but to make the information timing, training scheme, and
comparison set precise and reproducible.
5.1
Information Timing and No-Look-Ahead
Let 𝑡index trading days. The information set used to form predictions at time 𝑡consists only of: (i) numerical
variables observable by a fixed cutoff time on day 𝑡(e.g., close-to-close returns, realized volatility computed
from intraday data up to the close), and (ii) text documents in T𝑡released prior to the same cutoff time, verified
by publication timestamps. Any delayed or revised texts (e.g., edited news articles) must be pinned to the
earliest timestamp available to avoid inadvertent look-ahead.
Semantic states 𝑆𝑡are generated by a frozen, versioned extraction procedure (fixed prompt template, fixed
model version, deterministic decoding, and caching keyed by document identifier and timestamp). All
preprocessing steps (deduplication, filtering, language detection, and any document selection rules) are
similarly versioned.
5.2
Training Scheme and Forecasting Setup
Forecasts are generated using a strictly chronological scheme (rolling or expanding window). At each
evaluation origin 𝑡, the model parameters are estimated using data up to 𝑡−1, and the predictive distribution
for 𝑌𝑡(or 𝑌𝑡+1, depending on the horizon definition) is produced conditional on the information set available at
the origin. When hyperparameters are tuned, the tuning procedure must be nested within the training window
(e.g., via an inner validation split) to avoid test contamination.

5.3
Baselines and Fair Comparison
Baselines are estimated under the same information timing: (i) numerical-only models such as HAR-RV
and GARCH-family volatility models, (ii) regime-switching or state-space models without text conditioning
(e.g., HMM/state-space with constant transition probabilities), and (iii) optional reduced-form text baselines
(e.g., linear predictors using hand-engineered sentiment), if included. All baselines must use the same target
definition, forecast horizon, and evaluation sample.
5.4
Metrics
Evaluation should include point-forecast accuracy (e.g., MAE on realized volatility), proper scoring rules for
density forecasts (e.g., negative log-likelihood and QLIKE for volatility forecasts), and directional metrics
when relevant (e.g., hit rate). When reporting uncertainty, time-series dependence suggests using block
bootstrap or HAC-style standard errors rather than i.i.d. assumptions.

Discussion
The proposed framework integrates LLM-derived semantics into quantitative finance without sacrificing
probabilistic structure. The key design choice is separation: semantic information affects forecasts only
through the transition kernel of the latent regime process. This separation yields three practical benefits.
First, interpretability improves. Changes in forecasts can be decomposed into (i) shifts in inferred regime
probabilities driven by 𝑆𝑡and (ii) regime-specific likelihood contributions governed by 𝑝𝑗(·). Second,
robustness analysis becomes concrete. The stability bound in Proposition 3 links semantic perturbations
to bounded changes in transition probabilities, motivating norm control on 𝐵𝑖𝑗and careful selection of 𝑑.
Third, evaluation becomes cleaner. Because 𝑆𝑡enters only through an explicit probabilistic channel, one can
perform disciplined ablations (e.g., replace 𝑆𝑡with lagged numerical proxies, shuffle texts within the training
window, or constrain 𝐵𝑖𝑗= 0) to isolate the incremental value of narrative information.
A limitation is that the framework assumes the semantic extraction procedure produces a meaningful,
reasonably stable representation for the relevant horizon. If the text stream is sparse or the semantic signal
7

is not aligned with the forecast target, the added channel may not improve performance. The framework
nevertheless remains diagnostically useful: the learned 𝐵𝑖𝑗and the regime posterior trajectories quantify
when and how the text channel influences forecasts.
7
Conclusion
We presented a rigorous LLM-augmented framework for market state inference and forecasting. By using the
LLM as a semantic-state inference module and embedding that state into a regime-switching probabilistic
model, the approach captures narrative-driven dynamics while retaining statistical discipline. We established
validity of the text-conditioned transition kernel, exact Bayesian filtering, explicit stability bounds with respect
to semantic perturbations, and identifiability under a transparent normalization. The framework is designed to
support leakage-controlled empirical evaluation and clear interpretability, providing a principled alternative
to end-to-end LLM forecasters in quantitative finance.
