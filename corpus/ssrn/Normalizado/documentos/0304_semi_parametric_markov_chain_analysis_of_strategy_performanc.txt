SEMI-PARAMETRIC MARKOV CHAIN ANALYSIS OF 
STRATEGY PERFORMANCE REGIMES IN ALGORITHMIC 
FOREX TRADING
Marijan Tolusic
University of Dubrovnik (Sveučilište u Dubrovniku), Department of Business Economics
mtolusic@net.efzg.hr
Accepted: 17th International Odyssey Conference on Economics and Business, FEB Zagreb,

Abstract
This paper introduces the Semi-Parametric Markov Chain (SPMC) framework for modeling 
strategy-specific performance regimes in algorithmic forex trading. Unlike conventional 
regime detection methods that operate on price return sequences, SPMC is fitted directly on the 
sequence of strategy trade outcomes, modeling performance persistence as a first-order (and, 
where warranted, second-order) Markov process. Using a dataset of 10,252 trades across six 
major currency pairs spanning 2019 to 2026, generated by a mean-reversion strategy with a 
rolling-performance filter, we estimate the SPMC transition matrix, test the Markov property, 
derive the stationary distribution, and compare the optimal Bayesian filter implied by the chain 
against the practitioner heuristic it formalizes. Key findings include highly symmetric state 
persistence (pₒₒ = p₂₂ = 0.793–0.794), rejection of the first-order Markov property in favor of a 
second-order specification, stationary distribution predictions consistent with empirical 
regime proportions, and an approximately ten percent improvement in cumulative performance 
under the optimal Bayesian filter. Critically, SPMC transition dynamics are shown to be 
empirically orthogonal to those of a price-based Markov Switching Model estimated over the 
same period, confirming that strategy performance regimes and market volatility regimes are 
distinct phenomena. The framework offers a principled, strategy-agnostic approach to adaptive 
trade filtering and contributes a novel intersection between financial regime-detection 
econometrics and algorithmic strategy evaluation.
Keywords: markov chain, algorithmic trading, performance persistence, bayesian filter, 
markov switching model

Introduction
Regime detection is a foundational problem in quantitative finance. The recognition that 
financial markets alternate between qualitatively distinct states has motivated a rich literature 
spanning Markov Switching Models (Hamilton, 1989), Hidden Markov Models applied to 
volatility, and various heuristic filters used by practitioners. The dominant paradigm in this 
literature is to detect regimes by analysing statistical properties of price returns: their mean, 
variance, or autocorrelation structure.
This paper argues that for strategy-specific filtering purposes, this paradigm is misspecified. 
The relevant regime for an algorithmic trader is not what the market is doing in aggregate, but 
whether a particular strategy's edge is currently active. These are empirically distinct 

phenomena. A strategy may generate strong, persistent profits during periods that a price-based 
model classifies as mean-reverting, and may underperform during periods classified as 
trending. The market state and the strategy state are not the same object, and modeling one does 
not necessarily reveal the other.
We formalize this distinction by introducing the Semi-Parametric Markov Chain (SPMC) 
framework. Instead of fitting a Markov model to price returns, SPMC is fitted directly to the 
sequence of strategy trade outcomes: the chronological record of wins and losses, measured in 
net pips. Performance states are defined by discretising this sequence, and transition 
probabilities between Good and Bad performance states are estimated by maximum likelihood, 
which reduces to simple frequency counting. No distributional assumptions are imposed on the 
returns within each state, hence the semi-parametric designation. The empirical distribution of 
trade outcomes serves as the likelihood component for Bayesian updating.
The framework is motivated by, and validated on, a mean-reversion strategy operating across 
six major currency pairs (EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDCAD, USDCHF, USDJPY, AUDUSD) 
over the period 2019 to 2026, yielding 10,252 trades. The strategy employs a rollingperformance filter: it takes positions only when the mean net pip outcome over the preceding ten 
trades is positive. This heuristic filter is a practitioner approximation of optimal Bayesian 
inference on a Markov performance chain. SPMC makes that approximation explicit, derives 
the optimal filter, and tests empirically whether the formal model improves on the heuristic.
Our findings are threefold. First, strategy performance exhibits significant state persistence: 
once in a Good regime, the probability of remaining Good is 0.793; once in a Bad regime, the 
probability of remaining Bad is 0.794. This symmetry is structurally meaningful. Second, a 
formal chi-squared test rejects the first-order Markov property, indicating that a second-order 
specification with four history-dependent states better captures the dynamics. Third, 
performance regimes identified by SPMC are empirically orthogonal to volatility regimes 
identified by a price-based Markov Switching Model estimated over the same period, with a 
correlation of only 0.026 between the MSM state indicator and the SPMC state indicator. These 
findings jointly support the central claim: strategy performance persistence is a real, 
measurable, and distinct phenomenon from price structure regimes.
The paper is organized as follows: literature review, theoretical framework, data and 
methodology, empirical results, discussion, and conclusion.
Literature review
The Markov Switching Model introduced by Hamilton (1989) established the foundation for 
econometric regime detection in macroeconomics and finance. Hamilton demonstrated that 
output growth could be modelled as switching between expansion and contraction states 
according to a latent Markov chain with constant transition probabilities, with regime states 
inferred via the Hamilton filter. This framework was subsequently adopted widely in financial 
econometrics, including applications to equity returns (Ang & Bekaert, 2002), volatility 
regimes (Gray, 1996), exchange rate dynamics (Engel & Hamilton, 1990), and interest rate 
modeling (Daly, 2008).

A central insight is that financial returns exhibit regime dependence: their distributional 
properties shift over time in ways not captured by linear, stationary models. Kim & Nelson 
(1999) provide a comprehensive treatment of Markov-switching state-space models. Guidolin 
& Timmermann (2006) extend MSM to multivariate equity and bond series, documenting 
outperformance over unconditional benchmarks in portfolio allocation. Hamilton & Raj (2002) 
survey applications across asset classes, consistently finding that two- or four-state models 
provide statistically significant improvements over single-regime alternatives. A 
comprehensive synthesis by Ang & Timmermann (2012) establishes that regime-switching 
models succeed precisely because the new behavior of financial variables often persists for 
several periods after a shift — documenting this persistence property across equities, bonds, 
currencies, and commodities, and showing that it induces non-linear risk-return trade-offs. This 
persistence property is the same mechanism that SPMC exploits, applied to strategy outcomes 
rather than price returns.
Despite this extensive literature, all price-based regime models share a common structure: they 
model changes in the statistical properties of price returns, and use those changes as proxies for 
qualitatively different market environments. The assumption that price-structure regimes 
translate directly into strategy-performance regimes is implicit and largely untested. The few 
studies that examine regime conditioning at the strategy level (see Bollen & Whaley, 2009, on 
mutual fund performance; Fung & Hsieh, 2001, on trend-following hedge funds) do so using 
price-based regimes as conditioning variables rather than modeling strategy performance 
dynamics directly. Nystrup, Madsen & Lindström (2018) use hidden Markov models with timevarying parameters to dynamically optimise portfolio allocation, finding that high diagonal 
transition probabilities — that is, regime persistence — are the critical property enabling the 
filter to add value over a static allocation. Bulla, Mergner, Bulla, Sesboüé & Chesneau (2011) 
test explicitly whether Markov-switching allocation strategies generate profits, confirming that 
symmetric, persistent transition structures are the key determinant. More recent work has 
focused on improving persistence estimation: Nystrup, Lindström & Madsen (2020a) propose a 
jump-penalised estimator that learns hidden state sequences without the rapid switching 
dynamics of standard HMMs, illustrated through a trading application. Nystrup, Kolm & 
Lindström (2020b) address the latency problem with a greedy online classifier applied to S&P 
500 intraday volatility. Despite this development through 2020, the entire stream operates 
exclusively on price or volatility sequences. No study models the chronological sequence of 
individual strategy trade outcomes as the primary object of inference or derives adaptive 
performance filters from it. That is the gap SPMC addresses.
A separate and smaller literature examines performance persistence in trading. Brock, 
Lakonishok, and LeBaron (1992) document technical trading rule profitability with significant 
autocorrelation in signal performance. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012) document time 
series momentum across 58 futures instruments including currencies, with positive autocovariance driving persistence over one to twelve months, confirming that the return 
autocorrelation the SPMC exploits is a genuine cross-market phenomenon. More recently, the 
market microstructure literature has examined order flow persistence (Hasbrouck, 1991) and 
strategic complementarities in execution (Glosten & Milgrom, 1985). However, none of these 
studies model the sequence of individual trade outcomes as a Markov process or use the 
resulting chain to construct adaptive performance filters.
On the methodological side, semi-parametric methods for Markov models have been developed 
in the econometrics literature (Semiparametric Markov models: see Gao, King, Lu & 
Tjøstheim, 2009; Chen, Hansen & Carrasco, 2010). These approaches relax distributional 

assumptions within latent states. Our framework draws on this spirit but applies it to an 
observable outcome sequence rather than a latent-state model, yielding a simpler and more 
transparent estimation procedure appropriate for strategy evaluation contexts.
The contribution of the present paper is thus to occupy a specific and previously unaddressed 
gap: modeling the chronological sequence of strategy trade outcomes as a Markov chain, 
deriving the optimal Bayesian filter implied by that chain, and formally testing whether the 
resulting filter is empirically distinct from and superior to both a practitioner heuristic and a 
price-based regime model.

The Markov Property and Transition Matrix
A sequence of performance states {S₁, S₂, ..., S_T} satisfies the first-order Markov property if:
P(S_{t+1} | S_t, S_{t-1}, ..., S₁) = P(S_{t+1} | S_t)
Under this assumption, the regime dynamics are fully described by a 2×2 transition matrix P:
P = [[p_GG, p_GB], [p_BG, p_BB]]
where p_GG = P(S_{t+1} = G | S_t = G) is the probability of remaining in the Good state, and 
p_BG = P(S_{t+1} = G | S_t = B) is the probability of recovering to Good from Bad. Row 
constraints require p_GB = 1 - p_GG and p_BB = 1 - p_BG. Parameters are estimated by 
maximum likelihood, which for an observed Markov chain reduces to frequency counting:
p̂_GG = N_GG / (N_GG + N_GB),    p̂_BG = N_BG / (N_BG + N_BB)
where N_ij denotes the number of observed transitions from state i to state j. The estimator is 
consistent and asymptotically normal under mild regularity conditions.
Stationary Distribution and Expected State Duration
Every irreducible, aperiodic Markov chain converges to a unique stationary distribution π = 
(π_G, π_B) satisfying πP = π. For the 2×2 case:
π_G = p_BG / (p_GB + p_BG),    π_B = p_GB / (p_GB + p_BG)

The stationary distribution provides the theoretical long-run proportion of trades in each state. 
Consistency between π_G and the empirically observed ON-percentage constitutes an internal 
validity check on the model specification. The expected duration in each state before switching 
is:
E[D_G] = 1 / p_GB,    E[D_B] = 1 / p_BG
These durations have direct practical implications for filter design: a rolling window of length k 
is well-calibrated if k ≈ E[D_G].
Optimal Bayesian Filter
Given the Markov structure, the optimal filter for deciding whether to take the next trade is the 
posterior probability of being in the Good state given the observed outcome history:
β_t = P(S_t = G | r₁, ..., r_t)
This posterior can be computed recursively. The prediction step propagates the previous 
posterior through the transition matrix:
β̂_{t|t-1} = p_GG · β_{t-1} + p_BG · (1 - β_{t-1})
The update step incorporates the likelihood of the current outcome:
β_t = [f(r_t | G) · β̂_{t|t-1}] / [f(r_t | G) · β̂_{t|t-1} + f(r_t | B) · (1 - β̂_{t|t-1})]
where f(r_t | S) is the empirical distribution of trade outcomes in state S, estimated nonparametrically. This semi-parametric approach avoids the Gaussian assumption of the standard 
Hamilton filter. The trading decision rule is: take the trade if β_t > τ, where τ is a threshold 
parameter optimised on training data. The practitioner's rolling-k filter is characterised formally 
as an approximation of β_t with fixed-window uniform weighting and a hard zero threshold, a 
special case of the more general Bayesian framework.
Second-Order Extension
If the first-order Markov property is rejected, the framework extends naturally to a second-order 
specification with four observable states: GG (two consecutive Good trades), GB, BG, and BB. 
Each history state carries a distinct conditional probability of the next trade being Good, 
producing a 4×2 prediction matrix. This extension preserves the computational simplicity of the 
SPMC approach while accommodating deeper memory in the outcome sequence. Formal 
testing of the Markov order is conducted via a chi-squared test comparing observed secondorder transition frequencies against first-order predictions.
Data and methodology
Strategy and Dataset
The empirical analysis uses trade records from a mean-reversion algorithmic strategy deployed 
across six major currency pairs: EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDCAD, USDCHF, USDJPY, and 
AUDUSD. The strategy operates on H1 (one-hour) price data and generates entry signals when 
price deviates from a 168-period simple moving average, entering in the direction of mean 
reversion. Exits occur on SMA crossback or trailing stop. The dataset contains 10,252 trades 
spanning January 2019 to March 2026.

Trade outcomes are measured in net pips after spread. The strategy employs a dynamically 
scaled position sizing system comprising four multiplicative components: a base lot size 
proportional to mark-to-market equity, a correlation multiplier rewarding periods of strong pair 
anti-correlation, a stretch multiplier scaling size with distance from the SMA, and a drawdown 
brake that reduces exposure during losing periods. The regime filter operates as a fifth 
multiplier: positions are sized at 100% during ON regimes and reduced to 25% during OFF 
regimes, rather than being skipped entirely. Trades in both regime states are recorded, enabling 
comparison between fully-weighted and quarter-weighted performance.
Benchmark: Price-Based Markov Switching Model
As a benchmark for orthogonality testing, a two-state Markov Switching Model is estimated on 
the raw H1 price returns of each of the six currency pairs. The MSM is fitted via the EM 
algorithm (Hamilton filter for the E-step, analytical expressions for the M-step), with two latent 
states distinguished by their mean and variance of returns. The high-variance state is designated 
TREND and the low-variance state MR, consistent with the convention in the literature. Per-pair 
MSM state probabilities are then averaged across the six pairs to produce a composite marketregime indicator.
Estimation and Testing Procedure
SPMC transition probabilities are estimated from the full 10,252-trade sequence using 
maximum likelihood. The Markov property is tested by comparing first-order and second-order 
transition probability matrices via a chi-squared test. The stationary distribution is derived 
analytically from the estimated transition matrix and compared with the empirical ONpercentage. The optimal Bayesian filter is implemented with non-parametric empirical 
distributions for f(r_t | S), and evaluated against the rolling-10 heuristic at two threshold levels 
(τ = 0.50 and τ = 0.60). Orthogonality between SPMC states and MSM states is assessed through 
Pearson correlation and a contingency-table chi-squared test.

Out-of-Sample Filter Validation
The filter comparison in the Bayesian Filter Performance subsection is evaluated in-sample. To 
address this, the transition matrix and emission probabilities are estimated on the first 70% of 
trades (trades 1–7,176; January 2019–January 2024) and the Bayesian filter applied 
prospectively to the held-out 30% (trades 7,177–10,252; January 2024–February 2026, n = 
3,076). No information from the test window enters the estimation. The rolling-10 heuristic is 
applied identically to both windows.
The trained transition matrix is highly stable across windows. Estimated on the training 
partition, P(G→G) = 0.9648 and P(B→B) = 0.9533, compared to P(G→G) = 0.9594 and 
P(B→B) = 0.9379 on the full sample. The maximum parameter shift is 0.016, confirming that 
the estimated regime structure is not an artefact of the training window and that the SPMC is not 
overfit to in-sample dynamics.
Table 2 reports OOS filter performance at threshold 0.40. Without filtering, the OOS baseline is 
9.2 pips per trade (28,176 pips total, win rate 43.7%). Both filters exhibit statistically significant 
discrimination between retained and filtered-out trades (Mann-Whitney U, p < 0.001), 
confirming that the performance autocorrelation detected in-sample persists in held-out data.
The Bayesian and rolling-10 filters achieve near-identical total net pips (68,654 vs 68,015) and 
Information Coefficients (0.1054 vs 0.1043, Spearman rank correlation between signal and net 
pip outcome). The substantive difference lies in capital deployment: the Bayesian filter retains 
73.5% of OOS trades versus 48.2% for rolling-10, generating the same total return from 52% 
more executed trades. This represents a meaningful advantage in a live trading context, where 
idling capital has an opportunity cost. The Bayesian filter achieves higher absolute 
discrimination (80.0 pips in−out vs 70.9), while rolling-10 achieves higher average pip per 
retained trade (45.8 vs 30.4) by being more selective. Both effects are consistent with the 
theoretical characterization in the Theoretical Framework section: the Bayesian filter uses the 
full posterior distribution over states, while rolling-10 uses a fixed-window frequency 
approximation that is more conservative.

Stationary Distribution and Internal Validity
From the estimated transition probabilities, the stationary distribution is:
π_G = 0.206 / (0.207 + 0.206) = 0.498,    π_B = 0.502
The model therefore predicts that the strategy will be in the Good state in 49.8% of trades in the

0.2 percentage points constitutes strong internal validity for the model specification. The 
expected state durations implied by the transition matrix are:
E[D_G] = 1 / 0.207 = 4.83 trades,    E[D_B] = 1 / 0.206 = 4.85 trades
The natural regime duration of approximately 4.8 trades is notably shorter than the practitioner's 
rolling window of k = 10. This suggests that the rolling-10 filter is over-smoothed relative to the 
natural rhythm of the strategy's regimes, averaging across approximately two regime cycles. 
Empirical tests confirm that shorter windows (k = 5) produce comparable total pips with 
superior per-trade quality, consistent with this finding.

largely unrelated to price-structure regimes.
The theoretical explanation for this orthogonality lies in the different objects being modelled. 
The MSM detects changes in the unconditional distribution of price returns, primarily through 
shifts in return variance. SPMC detects changes in the conditional distribution of strategy 
outcomes, which depend on the complex interaction between the strategy's entry and exit logic, 
current spread conditions, order flow patterns, and cross-pair correlations. These determinants 
of strategy edge are not adequately summarised by single-pair return volatility.
Discussion
The SPMC framework has several implications for both academic research and algorithmic 
trading practice. From a research perspective, the empirical orthogonality between SPMC and 
MSM regimes raises a fundamental question about what price-based regime models are actually 
measuring and whether that measurement is relevant for strategy-specific decisions. The 
finding that performance persistence explains eight times more strongly correlated with future 
trade outcomes than market volatility is (implying an R² ratio of approximately 65:1), suggests 
that the profession’s focus on price-structure regimes may be misallocated for the specific 
purpose of adaptive trade filtering.
The rejection of the first-order Markov property in favor of a second-order specification has 
both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, it implies that regime transitions are 

not memoryless: the probability of entering a Good regime depends not only on the current state 
but on the recent trajectory. Practically, it suggests that simple ON/OFF regime filters that 
condition only on the current state leave information on the table. A filter that distinguishes GG, 
GB, BG, and BB histories and applies state-dependent position sizing would capture the 
gradient visible in Table 4.
The near-perfect symmetry of the transition matrix (p_GG ≈ p_BB ≈ 0.794) is theoretically 
noteworthy. This symmetry implies that Good and Bad regimes are structurally equivalent in 
their persistence properties, suggesting a single underlying mechanism drives both the 
clustering of wins and the clustering of losses. This is consistent with a market microstructure 
explanation: periods when the strategy's entry conditions align with underlying order flow 
dynamics produce sustained profitability, while periods of misalignment produce sustained 
losses, and the duration of alignment or misalignment follows the same statistical process in 
both directions.
The natural regime duration of 4.8 trades is substantially shorter than the rolling-10 window, 
raising the question of whether practitioners systematically over-smooth their performance 
filters. The data suggest that a window of k = 5 would be more tightly calibrated to actual regime 
dynamics. However, the modest performance difference between k = 5 and k = 10 in the data 
indicates that the cost of over-smoothing is limited, possibly because the second-order structure 
provides corrective information that partially compensates for the excess lag in the first-order 
filter.
Limitations include the restriction to a single strategy type. The in-sample filter comparison of 
the Bayesian Filter Performance subsection is complemented by the out-of-sample validation in 
the Out-of-Sample Filter Validation subsection, which confirms that both filters exhibit 
statistically significant predictive power on held-out data (January 2024–February 2026) and 
that the transition matrix is stable across windows (maximum parameter shift of 0.016). The 
primary direction for future work is extension to the SMA trend-following strategy on the same 
infrastructure, which would provide a second empirical pillar and allow comparison of SPMC 
structures across strategy types.
Conclusion
This paper introduces and validates the Semi-Parametric Markov Chain (SPMC) framework for 
modeling and exploiting strategy performance persistence in algorithmic forex trading. By 
fitting a Markov chain directly to the sequence of trade outcomes rather than to price returns, 
SPMC captures performance regimes that are empirically orthogonal to the volatility regimes 
identified by conventional Markov Switching Models.
Estimated on 10,252 trades across six currency pairs spanning 2019 to 2026, the framework 
reveals strong and symmetric state persistence (p_GG = p_BB ≈ 0.794), a natural regime 
duration of approximately 4.8 trades, a stationary distribution consistent with empirical regime 
proportions, and rejection of the first-order Markov property in favor of a richer second-order 
specification. The optimal Bayesian filter derived from the estimated chain outperforms the 
practitioner rolling-10 heuristic by approximately 10% in cumulative performance and reduces 
maximum drawdown by 21%, while the heuristic itself is characterised formally as a 
computationally simple approximation of the optimal filter.

The central contribution is the demonstration that performance persistence is a real, measurable, 
and distinct phenomenon from price-structure regimes: the SPMC state indicator correlates at 
+0.210 with future trade outcomes but at only +0.037 with the price-based MSM regime 
indicator. This empirical orthogonality supports the theoretical claim that strategy edges have 
their own regime dynamics, shaped by the interaction between strategy logic and market 
microstructure, that price-based models cannot detect.
The framework is generalisable to any strategy type that produces a chronological sequence of 
discrete trade outcomes, requires no proprietary data beyond the strategy's own trade log, and 
has estimation complexity of O(n) in the number of trades. These properties make it a practical 
tool for adaptive trade filtering in both research and live deployment contexts. Future work will 
extend the analysis to the SMA trend-following strategy and investigate SPMC structure 
stability across market regimes and currency pairs. The Out-of-Sample Filter Validation 
subsection establishes that the mean-reversion SPMC generalizes beyond the estimation 
sample; confirming this for a qualitatively different strategy type remains the principal 
outstanding empirical question.
