Abstract 
In finance area, it is well known that the cross-market efficiency among financial 
markets is time varying and is relatively weak when the market is bearish and volatile. 
At first glance, this characteristic contradicts the exchange regulators’ objective of 
improving market efficiency since the increasing correlation implies internal market 
inefficiency. Generally, the researcher attributes these characteristics to the 
globalization of financial markets (increasing correlation). In this study, we provide an 
additional explanation, which is the increasing similarity of market microstructures in 
exchanges. Additionally, we confirm the statement that strong co-movement 
patterns does not necessarily mean the market is internally inefficient, as suggested 
by Dwyer and Wallace (1992). Furthermore, we suggest that there are significant 
interaction effect of the internal market efficiency and the market co-movement on 
the cross-market efficiency, and that compared to the market in normal condition, 
the cross-market efficiency depends heavier on the internal market efficiency and 
the market co-movement when the market is in emergency condition.

1. Introduction 
 
The study of the co-movement patterns among international financial markets 
has drawn a great deal of scholars’ interests. It has been established in prior 
literatures that the correlations among international markets (i.e., co-movement) are 
time-varying and relatively strong in bearish and volatile market. Researchers 
attribute these characteristics to the globalization and integration of international 
markets (Phylaktis and Xia, 2009). The understanding about the correlation pattern is 
of interest to both scholars and investors since it plays a significant role in evaluating 
the potential gains and losses of international portfolio diversification strategy. The 
underlying theory is that the weaker the correlation across markets, the greater the 
potential benefit of the international diversification strategy. However, the potential 
benefits would be reduced if the co-movement of markets is strengthened.  
 
In addition, the dynamic pattern of co-movement across international financial 
markets also plays an important role in understanding the relationship between 
self-market efficiency and cross-market efficiency. Compared to previous researches 
(based on index option markets or exchange rate markets), which generally does not 
make distinctions between market co-movement and cross-market efficiency, this 
study suggests that the market strong co-movement is unequal to the cross-market 
inefficiency, as suggested by Dwyer and Wallace (1992).  
 
To illustrate this statement, consider two markets1, namely home market2 and

2. Background Knowledge 
 
In this section, the basic concepts of market microstructure, efficient market 
hypothesis, cross-market efficiency and market co-movement are explained to aid 
the understanding about the topic. 
2.1 Market Microstructure 
 
Market microstructure, defined by O’Hara (1995), is the research about how 
specific trading mechanisms affect investors’ decision-making process. Empirically, 
the study on market microstructure aims to answer the question of how the change 
of a specific trading rule affects the market efficiency and liquidity. 
 
According to Madhavan (2000), studies on market microstructure can be 
classified into four categories. First, price formation and price discovery, which aims 
to investigate how latent demands are translated into realized prices and volumes,

including investigation of determinants of trading costs and processes of information 
incorporating into prices. Second, market structure and design issues, which concern 
the relationship between price formation process and trading protocols. Third, 
information and disclosure, which assumes that the behavior of traders and their 
affected by the “black box3”. And the final refers to the topic of informational issues 
arising from the interface of market microstructure with other areas of finance, such 
as corporate finance, asset pricing, and international finance.  
 
However, studies on market microstructure focus on the impact about a change 
of trading rules or regulations in a single market, but pay little attention to the 
impact caused by a change of microstructure in one market to other related markets. 
This influence cannot be ignored because any market is not completely isolated from 
international financial markets, and also because of the tendency of globalization.  
2.2 Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) 
 
The Efficient Market Hypothesis, first defined by Fama (1970), refers to an 
efficient financial market in which price reflects all value related information (all of 
market, public and private information) and in which the investor cannot always 
achieve excess returns. According to Fama (1970), depending on the content of 
information, which is incorporated in the current price, market efficiency can be 
categorized into three forms. 
 
The first and weakest form is the weak-form efficiency, which means that 
historical data cannot be used to predict the future price movement. Therefore,

information has already been incorporated in the current price. In other words, 
investors with public information cannot earn excess returns, and only the investors 
with private (inside) information can take advantage of the market under this form of 
market efficiency. This type of market assumes prices adjust to newly released 
information in a rapid and unbiased way. Compared to the weak form efficiency, 
under semi-strong form, neither fundamental analysis nor technical analysis can 
generate excess returns.  The final and strongest form of market efficiency is 
strong-form efficiency, which states that all available information (all of market, 
public and private information) cannot be used to predict future price movement. 
Therefore, investors cannot earn excess returns regarding the information they 
processed. 
2.3 Cross-Market Efficiency 
 
Generally, the cross-market efficiency is investigated from the perspective of 
mispricing and based on the index option market or exchange rates market (Brunetti 
and Torricelli 2007; Vipul 2008; Kühl, 2010). In the current study, the cross-market 
efficiency, as a sub-concept of general market efficiency theory, refers to the idea 
that the past price movement in one market cannot be used to predict the future 
price motion in another market since the current price has already incorporated all

2.4 Market Co-movement 
 
Market co-movement refers to average cross-correlation or synchronicity which 
is the fraction of stocks that move together at a given time (Morch et al., 2000). 
Empirically, co-movement between two financial markets is studied by analyzing the 
co-integration or cross-correlation between the two market composite indices. 
 
3. Literature Review 
 
The literature related to this topic can be divided into two branches, studies on 
market microstructure and studies on market co-movement, respectively. Literatures 
on market microstructure are reviewed based on the theoretical framework 
suggested by Asmar and Ahmad (2011). Additionally, this study suggests that factors 
of settlement system, frictions and capital sources have impacts on market 
microstructure as well.  
3.1 Market Microstructure 
 
As suggested by Lagorade-Segot (2009), microstructure of emerging markets 
(such as informational efficiency, volatility and liquidity levels) are affected by 
economic and political contexts of insider trading regulations, trading system 
automation and accounting standardization. Additionally, he suggested that specific 
reforms of market have different marginal impacts on market microstructure. 
However, in this study, we use market microstructure and trading rules of equity 
market interchangeably.

Based on the work of Asmar and Ahmad (2011), market microstructure could be 
analyzed from the two sub-dimensions of trading mechanism and market regulation.  
3.1.1 Trading Mechanism 
 
Trading mechanism refers to the trading methods, and could be studied in terms 
of market type, trading sessions, order forms and market transparency (Asmar and 
Ahmad, 2011). 
3.1.1.1 Market Type 
 
There are three dimensions for market type, includes degree of continuity, role 
of market makers and degree of automations. Since most of the financial markets 
over the world are hybrid4, involving market makers, brokers, dealers and clearing 
house, and  electronic automation5, there is limited literature about the impacts of 
a change of market type.  
 
Pagano and Schwartz (2003) investigated the effects of the introduction of 
electronic closing call auctions on market quality in Euronext Paris, and they revealed 
that the new degree of continuity could lower the transaction costs and improve 
market’s ability of price discovery .  
 
Chelley-Steeley (2008) examined the effects of the introduction of closing call 
auction at the London Stock Exchange in 2000, and concluded that after the 
effectiveness of the closing call auction, opening and closing market quality has 
improved and that the greatest improvement is with the most inactive securities.  
Comerton-Forde, Rydge and Burridge (2007) investigated the impact of

Contrary to previous researches, they revealed that there is a decrease in market

efficiency, price volatility and relative volumes and absolute spreads.  
Similarly, Gerace, Tian and Zheng (2009) investigated the market liquidity before 
and after the introduction of a new pre-market call auction system on July 1, 2006 in 
the Shanghai Exchange. And they concluded that after the increase of transparency 
during the opening call auction, there is an increase in participation during the 
auction and a reduction of orders placed in the continuous session, which results in 
lower price volatility and narrower proportional bid-ask spreads. 
 
Huang and Stoll (1996) compared the bid-ask spreads in dealer (quote-driven) 
markets and in auction (order-driven) markets, and showed that the auction could 
lower the transaction costs for investors.  
In addition, Madhavan (1992) suggested that the dealer market could lower the 
volatility and improve the pricing efficiency in the market.  
Generally, markets with call auction and electronic systems improve the market 
quality. 
3.1.1.2 Trading Session 
The trading session refers to the defined period time of trading by exchange 
authority. In his study, Asem (2007) examined the market volume of the NYSE when 
its trading hours were extended on September 30, 1985, and found out that the 
volume declined initially, as predicted by closure models, but gradually increased to

its pre-extension levels, which suggested that the change of trading session has a 
temporary impact on investors’ trading decision. 
Alexakis and Balios (2008) investigated the influence of change of microstructure 
in the Athens Stock Exchange. They concluded that structure and duration of trading 
session have little effect on volatility and informational efficiency, and that the 
continuous trading seems to be a more efficient regime than trading with halts. 
 
Generally, markets with halts are less efficient, and a specific time period of 
trading has little impact on market quality.  
3.1.1.3 Order Forms 
 
Orders represent the intent of trading (buying or selling) a listed security in 
secondary market, and could take the forms of market order, limit order, stop order, 
and other forms which could control the price, quantity and execution of trades. 
Easley and O’Hara (1991) examined the effect of order forms on security prices, 
and revealed that the trading behavior of investors is affected by the order forms, 
which results in the change in information about market order flow, and then affects 
the price formation process. Harris and Hasbrouck (1996) compared the execution 
performance for market and limit orders, and revealed that the limit order commonly 
outperforms market order. 
3.1.1.4 Transparency 
 
Market transparency is about the quality of the information observed regarding 
price formation processes by market participants. Generally, the information includes 
past and current prices, volumes, bid-ask spreads, quotes and identities of market

participants. The previous research on the impact of transparency suggests that a 
high level of market efficiency or quality is associated with high levels of 
transparency (Pagano and Roell 1996; Madhavan, Porter and Weaver., 2005; and

3.1.2 Market Regulation 
 
Market regulation, which is defined as the rules about how to trade, could be 
analyzed from the perspectives of rules of order priority, tick size and spreads, listing, 
trading unit, price thresholds, trading status, shorting selling, and off-market trading 
(Asmar and Ahmad, 2011). 
3.1.2.1 Rules of Order Priority 
 
The order priority refers to the rules about how the orders are matched in the 
market. Generally, orders are prioritized by of price first and then by time. Demarchi 
and Foucault (2000) suggested that the choice of secondary priority is important to 
encourage traders to provide liquidity, and the time priority as the secondary priority 
could give incentives for price competition among traders.  
3.1.2.2 Tick Size and Spreads 
 
Tick size refers to the minimum change on the price. Generally, the tick size could 
be in decimals or fractions, and could also be varied or fixed within different price 
ranges. Ascioglu, Comerton-Forde, and McInish (2010) examined the different tick 
sizes in Tokyo Stock Exchange which are based on stock prices solely. They suggested 
that the tick size should be determined by trading activity and stock price, and 
posited that, transaction costs would be unnecessarily high, if the minimum tick size

13 
 
is set inadequately. 
Hsieh, Chuang and Lin (2008) examined the impact of tick size reduction in 
Taiwan Stock Exchange, and showed that this change of market microstructure 
reduced bid-ask spread, market depth, market liquidity, and binding-constraint 
probability, which led to improvement in market efficiency and reduction in trading 
costs in the market. Similarly, many other researchers investigated the impact of 
reduction of tick size in different stock exchanges, and came to a similar conclusion. 
(For example, Pavabutr and Prangwattananon (2008) made their investigation based 
on the Thailand Stock Exchange, Alampieski and Lepone (2009) studied the change of 
tick size in the Sydney Stock Exchange, and Kurov (2008) studied the tick size 
reduction in the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME)).  
Theoretically, the smaller the tick size, the small transaction cost it incurred in 
trading, and thus the higher the market quality. 
3.1.2.3 Listing 
 
The rules of listing refers to the requirements imposed by the exchange authority 
to the individual company for being listed in the secondary market. Generally, less 
strict rules would result in more unfair trades, which would harm the investors, and 
then lead to less efficiency of the market. On the other hand, more strict rules will 
result in a healthier trading environment, and thus more participants in the market, 
which leads to a higher level of market efficiency. 
3.1.2.4 Trading Unit 
 
In an exchange, securities are generally traded on the basis of a standardized

exchanges to meet the specific requirement of an individual company. 
Amuhud, Mendelson and Uno (1999) investigated the impact of “round lots” in

can increase the number of individual investors, stock liquidity, and stock prices. 
Similarly, Hauser and Lauterbach (2003) reached similar conclusion on the basis of 
the Tel-Aviv Stock Exchange. 
 
Theoretically, lower trading unit will result in less transaction costs and more 
efficiency in the market pricing process. 
3.1.2.5 Price Thresholds 
 
Price thresholds provide information about the price ranges securities can be 
traded based on the last closing price. Theoretically, a price threshold will limit the 
market’s ability to reflect on the newly released information. 
Ryoo and Graham (2002) examined the market efficiency of the Korean stock 
market, in which five regimes of daily price limits are employed. And they found a 
positive relationship between daily price limits and proportion of stock prices 
movement in random walk. In other words, as daily price limits increase, the stock 
price is more efficient.  
Chan, Kim, and Rhee (2005) investigated the impact of wide price limits in the 
Kuala Lumpur Stock Exchange from the perspectives of information asymmetry, 
arrival rates of informed traders, and order imbalance. They concluded that the price 
threshold has no positive effect on those perspectives, and suggested that price

thresholds helped to improve price discovery and generated great transaction costs.  
3.1.2.6 Trading Status 
 
In the case of news announcements or corrections of order imbalance, the 
exchange authority may suspend the trading of a security. And the implementation 
of the rules of trading status gives investors fair opportunities to reflect on the new 
information, and could improve the market efficiency. 
3.1.2.7 Short Selling 
 
Short selling refers to the sale of securities in the market, which are borrowed 
from a third party, and brought back at a future date to return them to the borrower. 
This trading strategy is applied when the investor predicts that the price will decline 
in the future. Generally, the market with short selling available is more efficient than 
the market with short selling forbidden since, in the former market, investors could 
earn excess returns when prices are increasing and declining, which makes the price 
in the market close to the intrinsic value. 
Boehmer, Jones, and Zhang (2008) suggested that short sellers are important for 
efficient stock prices. And in a later research, Boehmer and Wu (2009) suggested that 
greater shorting flow reduces deviations of transaction prices from a random walk, 
and accelerates the incorporation of public information into prices. In other words, 
short selling could be helpful to discover the efficient price of stocks. Therefore, the 
less short sale restriction there is, the more the market informational efficiency, and 
vice versa.

3.1.2.8 Off-Market Trading 
 
Off-market trading refers to the trade of listed securities outside the formal 
exchange. The use of off-market trading is to remove the impact of trading large 
blocks of shares on the price. In their study, Booth et al. (2002) suggested that 
upstairs traders (off-market traders) are associated with less information content and 
lower influence on price.  
3.1.3 Other Related Factors 
 
In addition to the theoretical framework suggested by Asmar and Ahmad (2011), 
we suggest that the rules about settlement system, friction, and sources of capital 
also have impact on market microstructure. 
 
Theoretically, if investors have to wait long time for selling their inventory, they 
lack the ability to reflect on newly released information in a rapid way, which could 
result in a deterioration in market efficiency.  
 
In addition, the high friction of trading (i.e., the high commission and stamp duty) 
will make investors lack the incentive to reflect on the new information since the 
friction must be subtracted from the gain of the transaction, which makes the market 
less efficient compared to the market with low trading friction. 
 
Regarding the sources of capital, a multi-sources market, in which both domestic 
and foreign investors could trade listed securities, could provide heterogeneous 
investing opinions and increase the participation in the market, which could result in 
an improved market efficiency compared to the single-source market, in which only 
domestic investors could participate in the transaction.

3.2 Market Co-movement 
Generally, the study on market co-movement is based on series of 
low-frequency data (daily or longer time period data) and market composite indices 
rather than high-frequency data (intraday data) and individual stock price.  
Rua and Nunes (2009) investigated the cross-country co-movement patterns of 
four developed countries (Japan, Germany, UK and US) from both time and 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=1928195

 
18 
 
frequency domains on the basis of market price index as well as for the ten economic 
sectors that make up the index. By using wavelet analysis, they revealed that the 
co-movement pattern between all pairs of these four countries is not constant over 
different time spans.  
Chiang, Tan and Li (2007) studied the dynamic correlations of stock returns by 
using Chinese A-share and B-share composite indices (daily) in both Shanghai and 
Shenzhen exchanges. They concluded that the correlation coefficient is time-varying 
and affected by restrictions and trading activities. Similarly, Aslanidis, Osborn and 
Sensier (2009) found the same result by investigating the STCC (Smooth Transition 
Conditional Correlation) by using monthly data of the S&P composite index and the 
Financial Times All Share Index (FT). 
In their article, Brockman, Liebenberg and Schutte (2010) suggested that the 
amount of information production is negatively related to market co-movement. In 
other words, if information production is ample, the co-movement is low, and vice 
versa.  
Phylaktis and Xia (2009) investigated contagion effect6, from the perspective of 
asset pricing at the sector level. They suggested that the integration between sectors 
is time-varying and heterogeneous, and that some of the market is isolated from 
international markets during crisis periods. 
Brooks and Negro (2006) showed that a company’s global nature is positively 
related to its exposure to global shocks and negatively related to its exposure to

Beine, Cosma and Vermeulen (2010) showed that stock market co-movement 
across return distribution is affected by macroeconomic variables asymmetrically. To 
be specific, global trading increases co-movement across the return distribution, 
“financial liberalization increases left tail co-movement” (p. 184), whereas only minor 
impact on right tail, and “exchange rate volatility has a strong positive impact on 
lower tail co-movement” (p.191). 
Bodart and Reding (1999) focused on the impact of the exchange rate regime on 
the correlation between time-varying stock market volatilities. Their analysis showed 
that an increase in exchange rate volatility was accompanied by a decline in 
international correlations. On the other hand, Kanas (2002) investigated whether the 
volatility of exchange rates was affected by the volatility of stock market returns for 
the US, UK, and Japan. Evidence presented suggested that the volatility of the home 
countries’ stock returns was a determinant of exchange rate volatility for all three 
countries. 
3.3 Summary 
However, to our knowledge, there is limited literature about the relationship 
between market microstructure and market co-movement. In this study, we 
hypothesize that similar market microstructures will result in similar investment 
decision made by trading participants, which leads to strong market co-movement 
and high correlation among the markets.  
 
Additionally, to distinguish the two concepts of market co-movement and

partially determined by the market co-movement and partially by self-market

4. Measurements 
 
Traditionally, the measurements on market efficiency and market co-movement 
are solely from frequency domain, which do not take into account the difference 
between short-term and long-term investment horizons (Rua and Nunes 2010). 
Naturally, investors with short-term interests are more interested in high frequency 
dependency and short horizon co-movement, whereas the long-horizon investors are 
more interested in low frequency information and long horizon correlations.  
Additionally, cross-market efficiency and market co-movement are often used 
interchangeably. However, there is a need to clarify the difference between these 
two concepts. The co-movement differs from cross-market efficiency from the 
perspective that the former concept emphasizes the simultaneous price movement, 
while the later analyzes the dependency pattern by considering the time sequence of 
price movements in the two markets. Therefore, using co-movement measurements 
to investigate the cross-market efficiency fundamentally ignores the embedment of 
time sequence which should be included when considering the issue of market 
efficiency.  
4.1 Measurement for Market Efficiency 
 
The essence of measuring price efficiency is to detect the dependency of price

predict the following price movement. The target for analyzing could be abnormal

Since both of the Shanghai Exchange and the Hongkong Exchange are internal 
inefficient markets, this section will develop the reasoning in the introduction section, 
and make testable predictions for further examining. 
 
First, the market microstructure influence the internal market efficiency, but the 
specific effect is determined by the type of the change of the market microstructure. 
Second, given the internal market efficiency in each market constant, the higher the

based on the cross-listing indices reveals several interesting findings. First, only the 
change of the internal market efficiency of the Shanghai Exchange has effect on the

influence on the cross-market efficiency. In the financial crisis period, the 
cross-market efficiency is positively related to the internal market efficiency in the 
Shanghai Exchange, whereas the cross-market efficiency is negatively related to that 
in the Shanghai Exchange when the market is in normal condition. The observation 
suggests that, when the market is in emergency condition, the improvement of the 
internal market efficiency of the emerging market can help improve the cross-market 
efficiency. However, when the market is in normal condition, the improvement of the 
internal market efficiency of the emerging market has negative effect on the 
cross-market efficiency. Second, the market co-movement has different roles in 
different periods. In the financial crisis period, the strengthening of the market 
co-movement will lead to the improvement of the cross-market efficiency. However, 
when the market is in normal condition, the increasing of the degree of the 
correlation would deteriorate the cross-market efficiency.  
 
10. Conclusion 
 
In this study, we delineate the market co-movement by the cross-wavelet 
coherence, the market efficiency by the entropy and the cross-market efficiency by 
the mutual information, respectively. Additionally, we examine the relationships 
among these three concepts on two time periods, the financial crisis (extreme 
market) time period and the releasing (normal condition market) time period, 
respectively.  
 
First, the market microstructure has significant effect on the internal market 
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=1928195

 
61 
 
efficiency in each market. However, such effect is also affected by the external 
environment (such as the condition of the market) and the frequency of the change.  
 
Second, significant interaction effect of the internal market efficiency and the 
market co-movement are observed. After controlling of such interaction effect, the 
improvement of the internal market efficiency of each market can improve the 
cross-market efficiency between the markets during the releasing period. However, 
when the market is in emergency condition, only the improvement of the internal 
market efficiency of the emerging market (e.g., the Shanghai Exchange) has positive 
effect on the cross-market efficiency, whereas the status of the internal market 
efficiency of the developed market has negative (or no) effect on the cross-market 
efficiency.  
Additionally, the effect of the market co-movement is inconsistent with our 
prediction, which suggests that the strong co-movement pattern can improve the 
cross-market efficiency rather than deteriorating it. In this vein, the strong market 
co-movement is unequal to the cross-market inefficiency.  
Finally, regarding the explanatory power of the regression model, for the 
financial crisis period, the cross-market efficiency is mainly determined by the 
internal market efficiency of each exchange, the market co-movement and their 
interactions. On the other hand, for the releasing time period, the explanation power 
of the internal market efficiency and the market co-movement decline greatly.
