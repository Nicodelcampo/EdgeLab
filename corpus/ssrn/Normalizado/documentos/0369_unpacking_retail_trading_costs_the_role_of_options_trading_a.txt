Unpacking Retail Trading Costs: the Role of Options
Trading and Limit Order Usage
Yashar H. Barardehi, Vincent Bogousslavsky, and Dmitriy Muravyev∗
First draft: February 20, 2025
Current draft: December 28, 2025
Abstract
Prior studies of retail trading costs focus on stock market orders, largely overlooking
the role of limit orders and the boom in retail options trading. We show that these
underexplored factors are critical for assessing overall retail trading costs. First, nonmarketable limit orders account for 17% of stock volume but more than 34% of option
volume in Form 606 reports by retail brokers.
Second, using trader-level data on
451,299 option and 607,922 stock retail trades, we estimate effective spreads using
actual trade direction to average 1.07% for options, much higher than 0.07% for stock
trades. But these realized option costs are 60% lower than the 2.59% conventional
effective spreads because retail investors commonly use limit orders to reduce trading
costs. These patterns hold across trader styles. Finally, individual trader order choices
rather than broker defaults drive execution quality, with trader execution performance
persisting over time.
JEL Classification Codes: G13, G14
Keywords: trading costs, retail trading, options, bid-ask spread

Introduction
The recent surge in retail trading has been particularly pronounced in options. Using Form
606 reports, we estimate that individual retail investors account for about 40% of total option
volume and about a quarter of total equity volume. Although commission-free trading has
slashed explicit costs, retail investors still pay implicit costs through bid-ask spreads. Option
spreads often exceed several percent even for most active contracts (Muravyev and Pearson,
2020). Thus, active investors who routinely cross these wide spreads can suffer substantial
losses. Retail investors are most vulnerable because they often want to trade immediately
and may not fully grasp trading costs.1
Despite the practical importance of trading costs, particularly in options, little is known
about retail investors’ actual costs and execution strategies. Investors can choose between
market and limit order types.2
A market order executes immediately but may pay the
spread, whereas a limit order specifies a target price and may earn the spread but risks
non-execution. Prior work has focused on retail investors’ marketable orders and neglected
limit orders. Bryzgalova, Pavlova, and Sikorskaya (2023) identify option market orders that
receive price improvement via option auctions (SLAN) but exclude other orders. Huang,
Jorion, and Schwarz (2024) study execution costs across brokers by placing market orders
and acknowledge that “other orders such as marketable and non-marketable limit orders
may be treated differently.”3 We show that limit orders are prevalent, provide estimates of
retail trading costs that reflect all order types, and explore trader-level execution.
Although limit orders typically incur lower costs than market orders (e.g., Harris and Hasbrouck, 1996; Anand, Samadi, Sokobin, and Venkataraman, 2024), prior research assumed

trading costs. A trader-level dataset from Bogousslavsky and Muravyev (2024) provides detailed records of verified retail trades across multiple brokers in both options and underlying
stocks from 2020 to 2022. For each trade, the dataset includes trader ID, ticker, option
parameters, timestamp, trade price, size, and direction. We merge these retail trades with
public market data, including the pre-trade National Best Bid and Offer (NBBO), using
OPRA for options and TAQ for stocks. This merge yields 451,299 uniquely matched option
trades worth $477 million in premiums, and 607,922 stock trades worth about $4 billion. We
extensively validate this merged dataset. For example, most option trades execute at the
pre-trade NBBO, alleviating potential time-mismatch concerns. Most importantly, estimates
of the limit order share from trader-level and Form 606 data match over time.

quoted spread for stock trades is 0.16% in our sample. The realized costs can be lower than
quoted spreads due to retail price improvement, hidden liquidity for market orders, or limit
orders. Indeed, actual round-trip trading costs, measured by effective spreads that account
for true trade direction, are much lower at 0.07%. For purchases we define the directionadjusted effective spread as twice the percentage difference between the retail execution

Retail trading costs in options dwarf stock costs, and thus are crucial for understanding
total retail costs. Options quoted spreads average 3.62% for our trade sample. Although
conventional effective spreads are lower with a 2.59% average and 1.38% median, they still
vastly exceed stock trading costs.4 Regularly paying such large spreads can result in large

To measure true option costs, correcting the conventional effective spread for true trade
direction is not enough. Due to wide quoted spreads, aggressive retail limit orders set the
NBBO and therefore the pre-trade midquote.
When the midquote embeds the trader’s
own quote, the conventional benchmark makes the trade appear artificially favorable and
understates costs. We address this mechanical bias by computing a “true” effective spread
that uses actual trade direction and, for trades that set the NBBO, excludes the trader’s own
quote from the pre-trade midquote calculation.5 In options, multiple exchanges quote the
same NBBO at any time. Thus, an aggressive limit order from a single exchange stands out,
and the midquote typically reverts back once it executes. Market orders and non-aggressive
limit orders, which account for 78% of all option trades, do not need this adjustment.
Strikingly, true effective spreads for retail option trades are on average 1.07% (0.66%
median), about 60% lower than the conventional effective spreads. The gap between conventional and true effective spreads arises from our ability to observe true trade direction.

We examine what drives retail investors’ choice between market and limit orders. In
theory, investors strategically choose between them to balance the cost savings of limit
orders against the execution risk. Retail investors in our sample behave consistent with this
trade-off. For instance, they rely more on market orders when market depth is high, which
aligns with theories of optimal order choice (e.g., Parlour, 1998).
We focus on the realized costs of executed trades as our data do not include trade
cancellations. Opportunity costs make limit orders less attractive because, as the market
price moves away from a limit order’s price, a trader may have to cancel the order and trade at
a worse price. Since liquidity taking is typically more expensive in options (wider spreads),
traders may accept higher non-execution risk to avoid paying the spread via marketable
orders. While opportunity costs are certainly relevant, they do not overturn our central
results about the option limit orders shaping retail costs. Prior studies that observe and
account for cancellations conclude that limit orders generally lower trading costs relative
to market orders, even after accounting for opportunity costs (Harris and Hasbrouck, 1996;
Anand et al., 2024). To bound the scope of non-execution, we estimate limit-order fill rates
to be around 30% for retail option trades in Form 606 reports.

Retail Order Type Usage in Forms 606
All retail brokers in the U.S. must submit monthly Form 606 reports to comply with SEC
Rule 606 under the Security Exchange Act. We collected Form 606 reports for all major
brokers over January 2020 to June 2025, which gives us comprehensive coverage over the
entire retail trader population.9 These reports include monthly information on market orders,
marketable limit orders, non-marketable limit orders, and other orders for S&P 500 stocks,
other stocks, and options. Information is reported at the aggregate and not the security
level. Figure 3 provides an example. The “summary” part of the form reports the fraction
of trades by each order type received by the broker. The “venues” part reports the share of

executed volume by order type as well as the associated total and per-hundred-shares PFOF

The PFOF data let us compute executed volume for each order type by dividing the
total PFOF received by the average PFOF received per 100 shares (Dyhrberg et al., 2025).
Specifically, for each broker and asset class, we calculate monthly shares of PFOF-receiving
trading volume associated with different order types as well as the broker’s market share
in the overall PFOF-receiving volume. Some brokers do not take PFOF for stock trades—
e.g., Fidelity, Vanguard, so we cannot estimate their total volume and order type shares.
However, all brokers take PFOF for option trades. We then add executed volume across
execution venues and brokers to obtain the total market volume by month.10
We provide several insights about retail trading using Form 606 data. First, retail trading accounts for 39% and 23% of total trading volume in options and stocks, respectively,
reported in Panel A of Figure 1. Specifically, for each month, we divide the aggregate trading
volume in Form 606 reports across all brokers by total market volume reported by the Option Clearing Corporation (OCC) for options and Cboe Global Markets (Cboe) for stocks.11
Thus, retail investors are important drivers of total market activity. Second, options become
increasingly important part of retail trading. Options accounted for 22% of the combined
notional volume in equities and options in early 2020, which increased to almost 40% by

sum, options deserve more attention from academics and regulators.

3
Trade Data and Trading Cost Measures
How does this widespread use of limit orders by retail investors affect their trading costs?
This section describes our trader-level data as well as our approach to measure retail trading
costs in stocks and options.
3.1
Trader-Level Retail Sample
We construct a sample of retail trade execution costs using the retail stock and option trade
data of Bogousslavsky and Muravyev (2024), which we refer at as trader-level data. Retail
investors who wish to use advanced analytics can subscribe to a trading journal, which will
automatically import and verify trades from their brokerage account. Bogousslavsky and
Muravyev (2024) gather stock and option trading data from one trading journal for which
trading information was publicly available by default. A typical option trade in this dataset
is the purchase of an option on the S&P index or a high-price technology stock held for a few
hours. We also observe which of the list of retail brokers supported by the journal investor
trades through, with TD Ameritrade being a top option broker. We refer to Bogousslavsky
and Muravyev (2024) for detailed information about the dataset.
For about half of retail investors in the original dataset, we observe complete trade price,
size, direction, and time information. We use this information to match journal trades to
11

option trade records in OPRA and stock trade records in TAQ.
For option trades, we match journal trades data with OPRA trade and quote data provided by SpiderRock.13 Because the timestamps of retail trade data have one second resolution, we match option contract trades between the two databases using the following
algorithm. We first match trades by underlying symbol; date; time (hour, minute, second);
option parameters (call/put; strike price; expiration date); and transaction price. We remove
a matched trade if its non-missing trade size in journal data differs from the OPRA trade
size to which it is matched. This step results in matching of 746,661 option trades out of all
the 795,899 journal option trades for which we have timestamps. The one-second timestamp
resolution is sufficient because individual options contracts on a specific stock do not trade
every second, even if all option contracts on that specific security are collectively are very
active.
Among these matched retail trades, 485,417 are one-to-one matches; i.e., cases where an
individual journal trade matches an individual OPRA trade. For matched trades of this type
with missing trade size in retail data, we use OPRA trade size and keep them in the sample.
All such cases enter our final sample.
One-to-many matches occur for 198,717 journal trades, where one journal trade is matched
with multiple OPRA trades. 22,610 trades represent many-to-one matches, where multiple
journal trades match a single OPRA trade; and lastly, 39,917 journal trades represent manyto-many matches, where multiple journal trades match multiple OPRA trades. Among these
non-one-to-one matches we first remove rows where trades sizes reported by journal data and
OPRA data do not match. From the remaining matches, we randomly draw a single row and
discard the remaining rows associated with that particular case. This approach is sensible
because we only need pre-trade best bid and ask prices from OPRA to estimate retail costs.

For stock trades, we follow a slightly different approach because the one-second timestamp
resolution becomes much more binding. Popular stocks tend to trade many times within one
second, so we match them to the national best bid and ask price (NBBO) at the start of
retail trade’s second rather than immediately pre-trade. First, out of the 2,275,555 journal
stock trades, we focus on the 1,085,000 trades in the 100 most actively traded stocks in
the journal data to manage computational resources devoted to an individual observation.
We then use tickers, dates, and timestamps to match each journal trade to the prevailing
NBBO at the end of the previous second in TAQ data, with NBBOs constructed following
Holden and Jacobsen (2014). Our final sample contains 607,922 retail stock trades that occur
within regular trading hours. We primarily focus on option trades and use stock trades for
validation and benchmarking.
While the journal dataset reports order direction, we do not observe order type. We
classify buy (sell) orders executed below (above) the prevailing quote midpoint as limit.

median is only 2 days, suggesting that retail investors prefer ultra short option expirations.
21.8% of their trades are in 0DTE options that expire the same day. Bryzgalova et al. (2023)
focus on orders executed in single-leg price improvement auctions (SLAN flag in OPRA).
These represent 27% of trades in our sample. Retail investors prefer to trade in the morning
as their median trade time in our sample is 10am.
3.2
Trading Cost Measures
We measure retail trading costs using relative quoted, effective, and realized spreads. These
measures are standard in the literature when the true trade direction (buy vs.
sell) is
unknown (Bryzgalova et al., 2023; Dyhrberg et al., 2025).
We calculate these measures
using the pre-trade NBBO midpoint, determined at the time of transaction for options and
14

at the end of the preceding second for stocks. The relative quoted spread is the bid-ask
spread divided by this midpoint. The relative effective spread is calculated as twice the
signed difference between the transaction price and the midpoint, scaled by the midpoint.
The trade direction is signed as +1 for buys and −1 for sells, estimated using the Lee and
Ready (1991) algorithm.
The availability of true trade direction data allows for two additional cost measures.
First, the signed unadjusted relative effective spread mirrors the standard effective spread but
utilizes the true trade direction (+1 for buys, −1 for sells) instead of Lee and Ready (1991)
estimates. Second, we compute the realized spread by replacing the pre-trade midquote in
the effective spread calculation with the midquote one minute after the transaction. We also
considered a 10-minute post-trade horizon. While results were robust, the 10-minute window
introduces substantial noise due to the high volatility of option prices and the arrival of new
public information.
Our primary measure of trading costs is “true” effective spread, defined as a signed
relative effective spread (from above) that adjusts for the mechanical effects of aggressive
limit orders. Retail investors may post a limit order inside the prevailing NBBO. Such an
order shifts the midpoint, causing the unadjusted measure to understate true trading costs.
Figure 2 provides an example. Suppose the prevailing bid and ask prices are $2.40 and
$2.60, respectively, resulting in a midpoint of $2.50. An investor posts a limit buy order
at $2.55, which shifts the prevailing bid to $2.55 and the midpoint to $2.575. If filled, the
signed unadjusted relative effective spread is +1 × (2.55 −2.575)/2.575 × 100 ≈−0.97%.
However, the prevailing midpoint that shaped the investor’s decision was $2.50, implying
a true trading cost of +1 × (2.55 −2.50)/2.50 × 100 = 2%. The unadjusted estimate thus
understates the cost by about three percentage points.
To compute the true effective spread, we undo this mechanical effect. We leverage SpiderRock OPRA data, which records both the first- and second-best pre-trade quotes. We
identify trades that: (1) occur at the best bid or ask price; (2) occur when a single exchange
15

defines the top of the order book; and (3) execute on that same prevailing exchange. For
these trades, we substitute the second-best bid (or ask) to calculate the adjusted reference
midpoint. In principle, aggressive limit orders could be informed and permanently reset the
NBBO. In practice, however, retail limit orders tend to be uninformed. We confirm this by
showing that the adjusted pre-trade midquote aligns with the actual midquote one minute
after the trade (accounting for trade direction). This indicates that the midquote typically
reverts to its level prior to the placement of the aggressive limit order. This midquote reversal is consistent with aggressive limit orders being picked off by uninformed option market
makers.
To prevent extreme outliers from driving the estimates, all outcome spread variables are
winsorized at the 0.1% and 99.9% levels each day. This is particularly important given that
unadjusted spread measures can exhibit significant skewness. Our results remain qualitatively identical if this step is omitted; however, the inclusion of extreme values substantially
increases the residual variance.

Retail Trading Costs
This section documents retail trading costs for stock and option trades. We first estimate
sample averages and then leverage trader heterogeneity to test whether trader choices or
broker defaults drive retail costs.
4.1
True vs Conventional Spreads
Figure 5 compares our measure of true retail trading costs against conventional spread measures.
The conventional quoted spread is high at 3.62%, and the conventional effective
spread, which assumes all trades demand liquidity, averages 2.59%. In sharp contrast, the
true effective spread, which accounts for trade direction and aggressive limit orders, is significantly lower at 1.07%. This discrepancy indicates that conventional measures substantially
16

overstate retail trading costs: the true cost is approximately 60% lower than the conventional
effective spread and 70% lower than the quoted spread.
Table 3 details the distribution of trading costs, isolating the primary driver of the wedge
between our measure and standard metrics. We find that the cost savings observed in the
average are also present in the median trade. The median true effective spread is 0.66%,
representing a savings of approximately 52% relative to the median conventional effective
spread of 1.38%. Unlike conventional measures which are bounded at zero, the distribution
of true effective spreads is shifted significantly to the left with 30.25% of negative values.
This confirms that a substantial portion of retail option volume involves liquidity provision,
resulting in negative execution costs that are obscured by conventional metrics.
The table also quantifies the mechanical bias introduced when aggressive retail limit orders set the NBBO. The unadjusted true effective spread averages only 0.60%. Consequently,
had we ignored the mechanical effect of limit orders on pre-trade NBBO, our results regarding the low cost of retail trading would be even stronger. However, we view the unadjusted
metric as biased because it credits the retail investor for the temporary price impact of their
own order. Our adjusted measure (1.07%) removes this effect, providing a more conservative and accurate assessment of execution quality. This result highlight our methodological
contribution to measuring treading costs in the presence of limit orders.
We validate this adjustment by examining realized spreads. The 1-minute conventional
realized spread averages 1.03%, which is almost identical to our true effective spread. Extending the horizon to 10 minutes yields a similar mean (0.97%) but drastically increases the
standard deviation (from 15.88% to 43.39%), as option price volatility is the main driver of
realized spreads at longer horizons. Since the realized spread measures the effective spread
net of price impact, this equivalence implies that retail trades have negligible permanent
price impact and are largely uninformed. So are marketable orders that execute against
the aggressive retail limit orders, which is consistent with uninformed option market makers
picking off stale limit orders.
17

Finally, the table reports execution costs for a control sample of option trades in OPRA
that are a mix of retail and non-retail—as we described in Section 3.1, control trades occur
in the same underlying-day but possibly different option contracts. In terms of conventional
measures, retail and non-retail executions appear remarkably similar. The average conventional effective spread for retail trades (2.59%) matches almost exactly that of the control
group (2.68%). Furthermore, both groups achieve comparable price improvement relative to
the quoted spread (1% for retail vs. 0.9% for control). While we cannot compute “true”
effective spreads for the control sample because trade direction is unobserved, the alignment
in conventional metrics suggests that retail investors face similar market conditions to other
traders. This suggests that the substantial cost savings that we identify (1.07% vs. 2.59%)
reflect limit-order placement strategies pursued by retail investors. Crucially, these strategies
are invisible to standard metrics.
We next compare retail trading costs in options to those in stocks. Figure 6 plots the
signed effective and quoted spreads for both asset classes, revealing a stark disparity. Unsurprisingly, stock trading costs are an order of magnitude lower than option trading costs.
While the average conventional quoted spread for stocks is 0.29%, the true effective spread,
which accounts for retail liquidity provision, is negligible, averaging just 0.07%. This stands
in sharp contrast to the 1.07% true effective spread observed in options.
Table 4 details the distribution of the stock trading costs in our retail sample.
The
median true effective spread is nearly zero (0.05%), and the 25th percentile is negative. Our
trader-level records do not report sub-penny increments, so we only capture full-penny price
improvement. If the trader-level data rounds sub-penny executions to the nearest penny, we
understate costs for marketable orders.
These findings align with Bogousslavsky and Muravyev (2024), who use an expanded
version of this dataset to show that investors are close to breaking even on their stock
trades.
They conjecture that minimal transaction costs in modern U.S. equity markets
drive this result; our finding of a 7 basis point average effective spread lends direct support
18

to this view. Consequently, while the existing literature predominantly focuses on equity
execution, our results suggest that the primary friction for retail investors lies elsewhere.
Given the substantial increase in retail option volume, the large wedge between stock and
option trading costs (0.07% vs 1.07%) suggests that future research and policy focus should
shift toward the options market.
Table 4 reports the distribution of estimated retail trading costs in stocks. For equity
trades in top-10 option tickers, the average effective spread is about 0.
The difference
between quoted spread and signed effective spread reflects the use of limit orders, retail
price improvement, and hidden liquidity. Using an expanded version of the dataset under
consideration, Bogousslavsky and Muravyev (2024) find that investors are close to breaking
even on their stock trades. They conjecture that minimal transaction costs for small retail
trades in modern U.S. equity markets can explain this result. The results in Table 4 lend
support to this conjecture. The large difference between retail option trading costs and stock
trading costs is worth emphasizing given the substantial increase in retail option trading in
recent years.
We next interpret our main results. The Form 606 analysis in Section 2 establishes that
limit orders are a primary tool for retail option investors. We confirm this in our transaction
data: true effective spreads are substantially lower than conventional measures, driven by
retail investors frequently posting competitive quotes. This implies that conventional execution quality metrics, which assume retail investors are solely liquidity takers, yield biased
estimates of trading costs.
These findings force a reassessment of the aggregate fees paid by the retail sector. Because
option market makers (OMMs) are the counterparty to most retail trades, the effective spread
represents the net compensation paid by retail investors for liquidity. Our estimate of 1.07%
indicates that by using limit orders, retail investors capture a significant portion of the spread
that would otherwise accrue to MMs.
A caveat to our cost estimate is the unobserved opportunity cost of non-execution. While
19

the effective spread measures the cost of successful trades, it does not account for the risk that
a limit order fails to fill. Theoretically, risk-neutral investors should be indifferent between
the lower explicit costs of limit orders and the certainty of market orders (Hasbrouck, 2021).
However, empirical evidence in equity markets suggests retail limit orders generate surplus
even after adjusting for non-execution risk (Anand et al., 2024). Given the wider spreads
in options, the economic incentive to bear non-execution risk via limit orders is likely even
stronger than in equities.
Finally, we connect our execution cost estimates directly to retail profitability. In Table 10, we formally show that every basis point of true effective spread paid passes directly
through to reduce investor returns, further validating our spread measure. We regress the
realized net return of retail option trades on our true effective spread. A trader’s net return
is simply the asset’s gross return minus transaction costs. Therefore, if our “true” effective
spread accurately captures the actual friction incurred by the trader, a 1% increase in the
spread must reduce the net return by exactly 1%. Any deviation from a coefficient of −1
would imply that our measure is biased or missing a component of the cost. The results
strongly validate our measure. In the specification controlling for underlying fixed effects
(Column 2), the coefficient on the effective spread is exactly −1.000 (standard error 0.092).
This unit slope confirms that our adjusted effective spread is an unbiased proxy for the
realized cost of trading.
More broadly, Bogousslavsky and Muravyev (2024) analyze the parent-trade version of
this dataset and document that retail investors lose 0.97% per trade on average. This figure
aligns closely with our estimated true effective spread of 1.07%, suggesting that transaction
costs, rather than poor market timing or stock selection, account for the vast majority of
aggregate retail losses.

4.2
Variation in Trading Costs
Next, we regress two measures of trading costs on trade and contract characteristics to
examine what drives variation in execution quality. Our first measure is the true effective
spread, which captures the absolute trade cost. Our second measure is the effective-to-quoted
(E/Q) ratio, which is a common measure of trade execution quality with lower E/Q reflecting
better quality. In the contexts of our study, where traders chose between market and limit
orders, the E/Q ratio also proxies execution skill as it captures a trader’s performance in
minimizing costs relative to the prevailing market prices. E/Q ratio of zero means execution
at the midquote (great execution), while E/Q ratio of one means no price improvement
relative to the quoted spread. Unconditionally, the E/Q ratio is about 0.3 in our sample.
We estimate five specifications, progressively adding fixed effects for the trading date,
underlying stock, broker, and individual trader. Table 5 reports the results for effective
spread in Panel A and the E/Q ratio in Panel B.
We document several stylized facts about the drivers of retail costs. First, spreads vary
with option moneyness, making it essential to control for this characteristic. At-the-money
(ATM) options display lower effective spreads, while out-of-the-money (OTM) options incur
higher costs. Trading activity concentrates around the current price (ATM), whereas OTM
options are less active and pose higher inventory risks to market makers, resulting in wider
spreads. Also, ATM options have a higher price than OTM options, which mechanically
affects relative spreads.
However, we note that the association between moneyness and
execution quality (E/Q ratio) switches signs across fixed effect specifications. This suggests
that while moneyness dictates the absolute cost level (spread), it does not drive execution
skill.
Second, effective spreads are higher for 0DTE contracts. Absent fixed effects, a 0DTE
option is associated with a 0.67% higher effective spread (t-statistic of 6.54). This suggests
that retail investors pay a premium for immediacy when trading ultra-short-term contracts,
likely because market makers charge more to warehouse the high gamma risk associated with
21

expiring options. Importantly, however, this contract characteristic does not drive execution
skill. While 0DTE contracts dictate higher absolute costs, they have little explanatory power
for the E/Q ratio compared to trader identity. This confirms that execution performance
primarily reflects trader attributes, e.g., ability to effectively employ different order types,
rather than security attributes, e.g., time to expiration.
Buy trades are associated with lower effective spreads and E/Q ratios compared to sell
trades. In the baseline specification, a buy trade reduces the effective spread by 0.37% (tstatistic of 4.32). This asymmetry supports the hypothesis that retail investors are more
likely to provide liquidity (use limit orders) when patiently opening a position (buying), but
demand liquidity (use market orders) when urgently closing a position (selling).
Fourth, we examine whether single-leg price improvement auctions (SLAN) reduce trading costs. Trades executed via SLAN have a much larger lower E/Q ratio (by −0.23 in
column 5). This confirms that the auction mechanism functions as intended, generating
competition that yields price improvement inside the NBBO. Similarly, complex (multi-leg)
orders are associated with lower effective spreads (−0.26 in column 5). This likely occurs
because market makers price the net risk of the package, which is often lower than the risk
of the individual legs, and pass some of those savings to the investor.
Finally, Panel B provides our most important finding regarding the source of execution
quality. We rely on a variance decomposition of Model (5) to distinguish between structural,
behavioral, and temporal drivers of trading costs. Conceptually, absolute costs (effective
spreads) should be largely structural, determined by the liquidity and volatility of the underlying asset. In contrast, execution quality (E/Q ratio) should be behavioral, determined
by the investor’s ability to capture the spread via limit orders. The results document a sharp
dichotomy that validates this distinction. Absolute trading costs are driven by security attributes: underlying stock fixed effects explain 54% of the variation, while trader fixed effects
explain only 34%. However, execution quality is driven by the investor attributes. Trader
fixed effects explain 71% of the variation in the E/Q ratio, whereas stock characteristics
22

explain only 14%. Finally, date fixed effects explain less than 5% of the variation in both
measures. This suggests that general market conditions and time trends play a secondary
role in determining variations in retail execution outcomes relative to the specific asset and
trader attributes.
This result has a critical implication for the debate on retail market structure. If execution
quality (E/Q) were primarily determined by how brokers route orders to wholesalers, we
would expect broker fixed effects to have a high explanatory power. Instead, comparing
Model (4) and Model (5) in Panel A reveals that broker fixed effects explain little variation,
as reflected in the adjusted-R2 that is below 1%. In contrast, adding trader fixed effects
dramatically increases explanatory power, elevating the adjusted-R2 to 5.1%. This evidence
strongly indicates that individual investor choices, specifically the strategic decision to use
limit orders, rather than broker defaults, drive retail trade execution performance.
4.3
Trader Types and Persistence in Trading Costs
Our results so far suggest that retail investors achieve low trading costs by supplying liquidity.
A potential concern with using trader-level data from a trading journal is selection bias:
perhaps our sample is overrepresented by sophisticated power users who utilize limit orders,
while the average retail investor relies on market orders. To address this, we examine how
the gap between true and conventional effective spreads varies across distinct trader profiles.
We categorize investors using three variables that proxy for trading style to address the
concern that our results are driven solely by hyper-active, professionalized retail traders glued
to their screens. To ensure our findings apply to the broader retail population, we verify that
we have robust sample sizes for typical investors. First, we distinguish “Fast traders” from
those with longer horizons; notably, traders with a median holding period of more than one
day contribute 96,474 trades, confirming our sample is not limited to day-traders. Second,
we stratify by investor sophistication proxied by average trade size, isolating “Low stakes”
traders (median trade size < $200). This group accounts for 100,507 trades, allowing us to
23

examine behavior of smaller Robinhood-like traders and contrast it with the “High stake”
(> $5, 000) group. Third, we proxy for experience using trade frequency, identifying a “Few
trades” group (< 10 per month) that includes 31,973 trades and a “Many trades” group
(> 30 per month) that includes 278,763 trades. This granular decomposition allows us to
rule out the hypothesis that low trading costs are a privilege reserved only for the most
active and sophisticated segment of the retail market.
Table 6 reports the true and conventional effective spreads for these groups. Two critical
insights emerge regarding the universality of limit order usage. First, the cost savings from
limit orders are ubiquitous, extending well beyond professionalized traders. Across every
trader type, regardless of size, frequency, or holding period, the true effective spread is less
than half the conventional effective spread.
The ratio of conventional to true spread is
never lower than 2.31. This is particularly revealing for “Low stakes” traders, who serve
as our proxy for the typical, less sophisticated retail investor. Their conventional effective
spread is 3.17%, yet their true effective spread is only 1.37%. If these smaller investors relied
exclusively on market orders, these two measures would be nearly identical. The substantial
gap confirms that less sophisticated retail investor frequently employs limit orders to reduce
trading costs.
Second, while limit order usage is widespread, sophistication still generates superior execution. We observe a clear gradient based on capital commitment: “High stakes” traders
achieve a true effective spread of just 0.58%, significantly lower than the 1.37% paid by Low
stakes” traders. Furthermore, the wedge between conventional and true costs is widest for
these sophisticated traders (a ratio of 3.97 compared to 2.32 for low-stakes traders). This
suggests that while less sophisticated investors are capable of using limit orders to avoid
paying the full spread, high-capital investors are more skillful in managing their orders to
capture the spread.
We next test whether this variation in execution quality reflects persistent skill or random luck. If execution depends on individual order placement strategy, performance should
24

persist. We split each trader’s history into two halves. We sort traders into quintiles based
on their E/Q ratio in the first half and measure their performance in the second half.
Table 7 documents strong persistence in execution skill. Panel A shows that performance
in the second half aligns monotonically with performance in the first half. Traders in the
best execution quintile (Quintile 1) start with an average E/Q ratio of −0.10 and maintain
a low ratio of 0.13 in the second half. In contrast, traders in the worst quintile (Quintile 5)
start at 0.88 and remain high at 0.60. This persistence is not random. If execution quality
were a random walk, the probability of a trader remaining in their initial quintile would
be 20%. Instead, we observe a distinct ∪-shaped pattern. The best traders have a 51%
probability of remaining in the top quintile, and the worst traders have a 52% probability
of remaining in the bottom quintile. This ∪-shape implies two distinct equilibria: “good”
traders consistently utilize limit orders effectively to capture spreads, while “bad” traders
consistently demand immediacy via market orders.
Importantly, this persistence is driven by the trader, not the broker. Panels B and C
replicate the analysis for trades executed through TD Ameritrade (the most represented
broker in our dataset) and non-TD brokers, respectively. The ∪-shaped persistence pattern
remains identical across broker subsamples. For example, bad traders on non-TD platforms
have a 54% chance of remaining in the bottom quintile. This rules out the hypothesis that
our results are driven by differences in routing algorithms between brokers. Instead, the data
confirms that individual trader choices (specifically the strategic decision to provide rather
than take liquidity) determine retail execution costs.
4.4
Order Type Choice: Market vs Limit
In this section, we examine the mechanism behind retail cost savings by distinguishing
between market and limit orders. This validates the hypothesis that retail investors are not
merely passive price-takers, but active liquidity providers who strategically manage execution
risk to capture the spread.
25

Since our dataset reports executed trades but not explicit order types, we infer the order
type by comparing the trade price to the pre-trade quote midpoint. We classify a buy (sell)
trade executed below (above) the midpoint as a limit order, and a buy (sell) trade executed
above (below) as a market order. While this classification imperfectly separates aggressive
limit orders from market orders, it captures the fundamental distinction between providing
and taking liquidity. Importantly, our main findings in previous sections do not depend on
this limit-vs-market classification. The calculation of true effective spreads (1.07%) relies
solely on verified trade direction and the execution price relative to the pre-trade midquote.
Thus, our headline estimates of retail trading costs are robust to any potential misclassification in this section. The purpose of this decomposition is solely to shed light on the drivers
of those costs.
Figure 7 validates our classification of order types by plotting the distribution of execution
prices relative to the quoted spread. We normalize prices such that 0 represents the best bid
and 1 represents the best ask. The resulting distribution is strictly bimodal, with volume
concentrated almost exclusively at the NBBO bounds. Because very few trades execute near
the quote midpoint, the mechanical cutoff we use to distinguish market from limit orders is
highly robust. The sharp separation between trades at the bid and trades at the ask implies
that ambiguity in assigning liquidity provision versus liquidity taking is relatively small.
Table 8 decomposes retail trading costs by this inferred order type. Panel A restates
our full-sample estimate of a 1.07% true effective spread. Panels B and C reveal the stark
heterogeneity underlying this average. Market orders (Panel B) pay a substantial cost, with
an average effective spread of 2.44%. This figure is close to the conventional effective spread
of 2.61%, confirming that when retail investors demand immediacy, they pay nearly the full
quoted half-spread.
In contrast, limit orders (Panel C) effectively earn the spread. The average true effective
spread for limit orders is −1.01%. The negative sign indicates that, on average, retail limit
orders execute at prices better than the pre-trade midquote. Note that they do not capture
26

the full half-spread (which would imply a cost of roughly −1.8%) because many retail limit
orders are aggressively priced to ensure execution, often setting the NBBO themselves. This
result highlights the trade-off retail investors face: they can pay about 2.4% for certainty via
market orders or earn about 1.0% by bearing execution risk via limit orders.
We next examine the determinants of this choice. In Table 9, we regress an indicator
variable for limit order usage on market conditions. This analysis has two limitations. First,
our sample includes only executed orders and thus misses the unfilled limit orders. Second,
causality may run in both directions; for instance, high volatility might induce limit order
submission, or a lack of market orders might cause volatility. Nevertheless, we aim to assess
whether retail order choice aligns with standard theoretical predictions.
The results provide support for standard microstructure theories. First, market depth is
negatively associated with limit order usage (t-statistic of −4.56). Consistent with Parlour
(1998), when the book is thick (high depth), the probability of a new limit order reaching
the front of the queue diminishes. Retail investors appear to internalize this execution risk,
switching to market orders when depth is high to ensure their trades are filled.
Second, volatility is positively associated with limit order usage (t-statistic of 3.40). This
aligns with Foucault (1999), who argues that the option value of providing liquidity increases
with volatility. In volatile markets, the spread widens and the probability of a limit order
being picked off profitably increases, incentivizing patient traders to post limit orders rather
than trade at unfavorable market quotes.
We also document a significant asymmetry in trade direction. Buy trades are approximately 5.8% more likely to be executed via limit orders than sell trades (t-statistic of 6.14).
This suggests that retail investors exhibit different patience levels depending on their trading
motive. They are willing to wait for a favorable price when entering a position (buying), but
prioritize execution certainty when exiting (selling).

5
Conclusion
This paper challenges conventional wisdom about retail trading behavior by providing a
comprehensive assessment of execution costs in the options market. Leveraging Form 606
reports, we document that retail investors extensively use limit orders in both stock and
options markets. Non-marketable limit orders account for approximately 17% of stock retail
volume and more than 33.5% of option volume. This finding contrasts with the traditional
view that retail investors are predominantly liquidity takers. Instead, the wide spreads in
options markets drive retail traders to often supply liquidity rather than pay the spread.
Consequently, conventional spread measures substantially overstate actual retail trading
costs. While quoted spreads average 3.67% and conventional effective spreads average 2.67%
in our sample, the “true” effective spread is only 1.07% on average. This 60% reduction in
estimated costs implies that retail investors are far more price-sensitive and strategic than
previously documented.
Our analysis relies on a novel synthesis of data sources that balances precision with representativeness. We combine granular trader-level data, which provide depth on realized
costs and persistence, with Form 606 reports that offer breadth across the entire retail universe . This approach confirms that widespread limit order usage is a fundamental market
characteristic rather than a sample artifact. Methodologically, we refine the measurement
of execution quality by adjusting spreads for aggressive limit orders that set the pre-trade
NBBO. Without this correction, conventional metrics erroneously classify these orders as
earning the spread.
Finally, we show that execution quality is driven by individual trader choice rather than
broker routing defaults. Trader fixed effects explain the vast majority of the variation in
execution performance, whereas broker fixed effects explain less than 1%.
Furthermore,
execution skill persists: traders who demonstrate superior execution ability early in the
sample tend to maintain this advantage.
These findings suggest that policy efforts should pivot from equity market structure to
28

the options market, where the primary frictions for household finance now reside. Moreover,
because individual order choice drives execution quality, future regulation might focus more
on investor education regarding order types rather than solely on broker-dealer conflicts.

Figure 2. Example of Adjusting NBBO for Aggressive Limit Orders.
This figure illustrates how we compute the “true” bid-ask spread by adjusting the midpoint for aggressive
limit orders. The unadjusted midpoint ($2.575) is based on the prevailing NBBO, while the adjusted midpoint
($2.50) replaces the aggressive quote with the next best quote on that side of the book. For example, a limit
buy that establishes the new best bid ($2.55) is treated as aggressive because it executes at or within the
NBBO and the submitting exchange is the only one quoting at that price. In such cases, the midpoint is
mechanically adjusted downward, yielding a tighter estimate of the “true” trading spread. This correction
removes transient quote distortions caused by order submission and reflects the midquote that would prevail
immediately after the trade.

Figure 5. Effective and Quoted Spreads of Retail Option Trades
This figure compares alternative measures of trading costs for retail option trades. The true effective spread
is true trade direction times twice the percentage difference between the retail execution price and the pretrade midquote, adjusted for the aggressive limit orders. Thus, the spread estimates round-trip costs and is
usually positive but can be negative when retail investors supply liquidity. The conventional effective spread
is twice the absolute difference between the trade price and the midpoint of the national best bid and offer
at the time of the trade, divided by that midpoint. The conventional quoted spread is the difference between
the national best ask and bid prices at the time of the trade, divided by their midpoint. Thus, conventional
spreads are always positive and assume a trade takes liquidity. All spreads are expressed as percentages of
the midpoint price at the time of the trade.
