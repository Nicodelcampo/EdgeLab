LLM-Driven Market Microstructure Representation Learning and
Game-Theoretic Reinforcement Dynamics
Yifan Guo

Abstract
This paper develops a unified framework that combines market microstructure, large language model (LLM) representations, and game-theoretic reinforcement learning. High-frequency
markets are modeled as strategic and adversarial systems in which heterogeneous agents interact
through an LLM-induced representation of the limit order book. We formalize a representationdriven stochastic game, establish equilibrium existence and stability, and analyze how LLMinduced geometry shapes strategic incentives, volatility dynamics, and liquidity formation. We
then outline an empirical framework based on message-level data and RL-based agents interacting with liquidity providers and takers in a realistic microstructure environment.
1
Introduction
Market microstructure studies the mechanisms by which orders are submitted, executed, cancelled,
and strategically arranged by market participants. At high frequencies, the price formation process
is inherently game-theoretic: each agent’s decision depends on expectations about other agents’
behaviour, the evolving state of the limit order book (LOB), and latent informational signals that
emerge at sub-second horizons. As a result, market dynamics are not purely statistical; they also
reflect strategic, adaptive, and often adversarial interactions among heterogeneous traders.
Classical microstructure models—such as the Glosten–Milgrom model, sequential trade models, and the Almgren–Chriss execution framework—capture important stylised features of trading
behaviour. However, these models impose rigid parametric structures and rely on low-dimensional
state descriptions. They are not designed to accommodate the high-dimensional, non-linear, and
rapidly evolving nature of modern electronic markets, in which heterogeneous agents interact at
millisecond frequencies, respond to rich streams of order flow information, and adapt their strategies
to changing liquidity conditions.
Recent advances in large-scale representation learning and reinforcement learning (RL) offer
new tools for modelling such environments. When trained or adapted to microstructure data, large
language models (LLMs) can be viewed as generic sequence encoders that produce embeddings
encoding latent liquidity regimes, directional order-flow pressure, and asymmetric informational
patterns. Formally, these models induce a mapping
Zt = Φ(Xt−k+1, . . . , Xt),
where Xt denotes the microstructure state and Φ an encoder acting on a window of past order book
states. The resulting representation process (Zt)t is low-dimensional, informationally rich, and,
under suitable training objectives, regular enough to support downstream control and optimisation.
1

At the same time, the financial system is not passive: agents respond both to the observed
state and to each other’s anticipated strategies.
This motivates the integration of LLM-based
representations with game-theoretic reasoning and RL-based decision frameworks. An RL agent
interacting with a financial environment must (i) anticipate the strategic responses of other agents,
(ii) assess the informational content of the LOB and its representation, and (iii) optimise execution
or market-making policies under dynamic uncertainty and inventory constraints.
Using LLMbased embeddings as state variables provides a concise summary of liquidity conditions, order-flow
imbalances, and price impact risk that can be exploited in such strategic models.
This paper proposes a unified framework that combines (i) LLM-based representation learning,
(ii) game-theoretic microstructure modelling, and (iii) reinforcement learning for dynamic trading
strategies. The contribution of the study is threefold.
1. We construct a representation-theoretic game model for high-frequency trading.
Agents observe an LLM-derived embedding Zt and choose controls as functions of a shared
representation of market conditions. We show how this representation simplifies the description
of multi-agent interactions and induces a tractable equilibrium structure.
2. We develop an RL-based method for synthesising equilibrium strategies. Rather
than imposing parametric policy forms, we learn best-response strategies in a representationdriven stochastic game and show how the geometry of the LLM embedding stabilises the
optimisation landscape.
3. We provide empirical and theoretical evidence that LLM-enhanced signal extraction improves strategic performance. In particular, we document that RL policies built
directly on raw LOB features can exhibit instability and regime sensitivity, whereas policies
based on LLM embeddings display smoother value-function gradients and more favourable
reward-to-risk trade-offs.
Figures illustrating the microstructure flow, the LLM encoder structure, and the RL interaction
loop are provided in Section 4. We employ placeholders such as microstructure_diagram.png
and rl_architecture.png for these images.
Overall, the proposed framework links modern representation learning with the fundamental
principles of market microstructure. It contributes to a growing literature that treats electronic
markets as strategic, adaptive, and adversarial systems rather than as passive data-generating
processes. By combining LLM-based state construction with equilibrium reasoning and RL, the
framework offers a methodological basis for forecasting, execution design, and liquidity provision
in contemporary high-frequency markets.

Extended Discussion and Future Directions
The results presented in this paper demonstrate that LLM-based representations offer a powerful
and theoretically tractable mechanism for organizing the strategic complexity of modern electronic
markets. By embedding raw message-level microstructure data into a low-dimensional geometric
space, the encoder induces both a state representation and a strategic manifold on which equilibrium reasoning becomes feasible. The analytical results show that when the encoder possesses
Lipschitz continuity and stability properties, the entire chain of strategic interactions — from order
placement to liquidity formation and volatility generation — becomes well behaved and robust.
The empirical analysis further reinforces the theoretical insights by validating that representationinduced equilibria and RL-driven reactions manifest in real-world limit order book data.
Despite these strengths, several conceptual and methodological challenges remain. Electronic
markets are inherently multi-layered systems in which algorithmic strategies, institutional rules,
cross-asset arbitrage, and heterogeneous latency regimes interact simultaneously.
The present
framework captures one layer of this system — the strategic use of microstructure and LLMbased encodings — but an integrated treatment of broader market architecture remains an open
problem.
The findings also raise deeper questions regarding the evolution of market microstructure under
increasing automation. As representation learning becomes endogenous to market participants, the
16

geometry of state spaces may itself co-evolve with trading strategies, giving rise to novel forms of
coordination, competition, and instability. Such phenomena suggest a need for theoretical models
that allow the encoder to be an equilibrium object rather than an exogenous component.
7.1
Limitations
Several simplifying assumptions, necessary for analytical tractability, limit the scope of the current
framework:
1. Exogenous Representation Learning.
The encoder is treated as fixed after training,
whereas in practice algorithmic traders continuously update representations as market conditions evolve.
2. Single-Asset Setting. Cross-asset flows, correlated order splitting, and multi-venue execution are excluded, though they are central to institutional trading.
3. Local Stability Analysis. Our theoretical results rely on local Lipschitz and contraction
properties. Global nonlinear phenomena — liquidity crises, flash crashes, and regime shifts —
require additional tools.
4. Simplified Agent Heterogeneity. Agents differ in order type and objectives, but latency,
regulatory constraints, and real-world routing complexities are abstracted away.
These limitations are not weaknesses of the model per se but reflect the challenge of balancing
analytical tractability with realism in fast, high-dimensional strategic environments.
7.2
Future Research
Building upon the theoretical and empirical findings of this work, several avenues for future research
appear particularly promising:
1.
Endogenous Representation Learning.
Agents could jointly learn representations and
strategies, giving rise to a fixed point in both the space of encoders and the space of policies. This
requires extending game theory to settings in which information structures co-evolve with strategic
behavior.
2.
Multi-Asset, Multi-Venue Extensions.
In fragmented markets, arbitrage relationships
couple microstructure states across venues. A representation-based equilibrium defined on such
networks could capture latency races, order routing, and cross-exchange predation.
3. Regulation as Geometry Design.
The results suggest that market rules implicitly shape the
geometry of the representation space. Exchanges or regulators could in principle design mechanisms
that alter this geometry to promote stability, fairness, or liquidity — a novel perspective on market
design.
4.
Global Dynamics and Rare Events.
Studying systemic phenomena such as liquidity
vacuums or endogenous volatility spikes requires moving from local spectral analysis to global
nonlinear models. Interacting RL agents under adversarial market stress provide a natural setting
for such analysis.
17

5. Interpretability and Explainability.
Understanding how an LLM representation partitions
strategic regions remains an open challenge. Developing geometric or information-theoretic tools
to interpret Zt would deepen the connection between machine learning and financial economics.
7.3
Broader Implications
The framework developed in this paper highlights an emerging paradigm in which representations —
rather than raw data or handcrafted features — serve as the foundational object for understanding
price formation. In market environments dominated by machine-driven execution and predictive
algorithms, such representations act as a “strategic coordinate system” for agents. This viewpoint
suggests several broader implications:
• Markets may self-organize into representation-defined regimes, observable only through highcapacity encoders.
• Liquidity provision and withdrawal may increasingly depend on the geometry of learned embeddings, rather than traditional indicators such as spreads or depths.
• Competition across algorithmic traders may shift toward representation learning capabilities,
potentially creating new inequalities in informational advantage.
• Price impact and volatility could become more predictable within embedding space, but simultaneously more nonlinear in raw features.
7.4
Reproducibility and Computational Considerations
Given the complexity of the LLM–microstructure environment, reproducibility is a critical concern.
We outline key components:
1. Data Handling. We provide detailed cleaning and normalization procedures in Appendix B,
ensuring that message-level reconstruction can be replicated across assets.
2. Model Architecture. Hyperparameters for the encoder Φ, including depth, attention heads,
and optimization schedule, are listed in Appendix D.
3. RL Training Protocol. We adopt fixed seeds and standardized episode windows to mitigate
variance in learning.
4. Computational Resources. Experiments were conducted on GPU clusters comparable to
modern institutional infrastructures, ensuring realistic execution speeds for high-frequency
simulations.
These considerations support the transparency and extensibility of the experimental framework
for future studies.
7.5
Concluding Remarks
The synthesis of large language models, market microstructure theory, and game-theoretic reinforcement learning provides a unified perspective on the structure and dynamics of electronic markets.
The central insight is that machine-learned representations can serve as the state space on which
both strategic reasoning and statistical forecasting are jointly constructed. This opens the door to

a new class of microstructure models in which algorithms do not merely react to market states but
define the representations through which the market perceives itself.
The framework developed here constitutes a step toward such models, offering both theoretical
guarantees and empirical validation. As algorithmic trading continues to evolve, representationdriven approaches may become essential for understanding and shaping the behavior of modern
financial systems.
