Statistical Arbitrage via Single-view and Multi-view Spectral
Clustering on Mixed Frequency Data
Raymond C. W. Leung
September 2024
Abstract
Systematically identifying clusters of similar assets is a critical step in statistical arbitrage strategies.
This paper makes two key contributions: (i) In addition to assets’ daily closing prices, we incorporate
realized estimators (i.e. realized volatility, realized beta, and others) derived from high-frequency intraday
data into the daily feature set; and (ii) We examine the effectiveness of single-view and multi-view spectral
clustering algorithms on these feature sets for identifying similar assets. We use these clustered assets to
construct a variety of signals and trading rules, including the distance method, cointegration method,
copula method, and an optimal entry-exit rule based on Ornstein-Uhlenbeck spread dynamics. We
evaluate our methodology on the S&P 500 equities, futures contracts, exchange traded funds, and foreign
exchange asset classes. Our findings show that trading profitability is influenced more by the selection of
feature sets and clustering methods than by the choice of signals or trading rules. Specifically, strategies
employing multi-view clustering algorithms, which integrate various realized estimators, consistently
deliver superior risk-adjusted returns and reduced drawdowns compared to those based on the distance
method and single-view clustering that rely solely on daily closing price data.
Keywords: statistical arbitrage; pairs trading; high-frequency data; realized estimators; machine learning
algorithms; spectral clustering

Introduction
Broadly speaking, statistical arbitrage has two distinct steps: (1) Identify clusters of similar risky assets; and
(2) Monitor these clustered assets for any substantial price deviations amongst them for potential long-short
trading. There is extensive literature (see Section 2 for a review) on executing step (2), applying methods from
cointegration, copulas, to machine learning for quantifying trade opening and closing conditions. However,
the vast majority of the literature focuses on daily or monthly prices (or simple transformations thereof, like
returns) as the sole characterizing feature of assets for executing step (1). Our paper’s key finding is that
using intraday data and clustering algorithms can improve the quality of step (1), and hence enhance the
risk-adjusted performance of statistical arbitrage strategies.
In classical works like [Gatev et al., 2006] and many others, the daily closing price Pt,i of a risky asset
i is the sole characterizing feature of a risky asset. Normalizing the prices to some initial date t = 1, one
would then compare the normalized price index Pt,i := Pt,i/P1,i of asset i versus asset j’s index Pt,j over
a formation period. If the two assets’ price indices are similar Pt,i ≈Pt,j, then we would regard these two
assets as similar to each other and hence belong the same cluster. This is [Gatev et al., 2006]’s classical
distance method. Subsequently in the trading period, if the normalized prices of asset i and j substantially
deviate from each other, and since they were deemed to be in the same cluster, this would present a statistical
arbitrage opportunity to long the underpriced asset and short the overpriced asset and take a profit when the
price gap closes.
However, a significant risk in applying a completely data driven method to identify similar assets is
spurious clustering. Assets that are well clustered together should have fairly similar statistical properties.
So when a large price gap between the assets opens, the statistical arbitrageur is confident that this deviation
should be a statistical anomaly and should eventually reverse itself by market equilibrium. But if dissimilar
assets are erroneously clustered together in the formation period, then any perceived price gaps over the
trading period of these assets are spurious, the gaps will not close or even widen, and the statistical arbitrageur
will almost surely lose on the trade. We hypothesize including — above and beyond just the assets’ prices —
additional features motivated by the continuous-time literature should improve the likelihood of identifying
similar assets and thereby prevent spurious clustering.

identify clusters of similar assets. This paper focuses on two main classes of clustering algorithms: single-view

Suppose we are given a collection of features (e.g. closing price, realized variance, and others) for a risky
asset. The single-view paradigm concatenates all these features into a long vector for a particular asset, and
then stacks to a matrix for all assets. In all, the input to single-view clustering algorithms is one big features
matrix. However, the concatenation step inevitably causes a loss of the spatial and temporal information
between the features. Multi-view clustering algorithms aim to preserve these spatial and temporal information
by forgoing the concatenation step. Indeed, the input to multi-view clustering algorithms is a collection of
features matrices.
Let’s turn to the trading strategies after having identified the clusters of risky assets. In this paper we
study the classical two standard deviation rule ([Gatev et al., 2006]) and slightly extend it to consider other
parameterized probability distributions, the bivariate cointegration method ([Vidyamurthy, 2004], [Chiu and
Wong, 2011], [Huck and Afawubo, 2015]), the copula method ([Xie et al., 2016], [Rad et al., 2016]) and
optimal entry and exit rules based on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck framework ([Bertram, 2010]).
Our main text focuses on the S&P 500 equities over the data sample of 2000–2022. We also apply our
methodologies to selected exchange-traded funds (ETFs) over 2012–2022, continuous futures contracts over
2009–2022, and foreign exchange (forex) pairs over 2010–2022. But for brevity the results on ETFs, futures
and forex are only briefly discussed in this main text, and we delegate their details to the Supplemental
Materials [Leung, 2024].
As documented by [Avellaneda and Lee, 2010], [Rad et al., 2016] and others 2 the profitability of
conventional statistical arbitrage strategies has declined substantially over time. Indeed, one could argue that
the distance method of [Gatev et al., 2006] (although the ideas has its origins due to Morgan Stanley from the
mid-1980s; see [Vidyamurthy, 2004] for an account) is the classical benchmark clustering and trading strategy.
We confirm these findings: applying the distance method solely on the prices of risky assets, and even if we
explore a variety of trading methods, we do not find any statistically significant profitability once we account
for transactions costs. Concretely, the distance clustering method with the two standard deviation trading
rule yields an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.571 but collapses to 0.338 once transactions costs are accounted
for.
In sharp contrast, we robustly find meaningful statistical arbitrage profitability once we adopt a combination
of the multi-view clustering algorithms applied on various realized estimates based on intraday data, along
with more sophisticated trading rules beyond that of the two standard deviation rule. In this paper, the
best strategy on the S&P 500 applies the multi-view clustering algorithm on a plethora of realized quantities
along with the closing prices of risky assets, along with an optimal entry and exit rules based on an OrnsteinUhlenbeck framework; this strategy achieves an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.166 without transactions costs,
and it is 0.830 once we account for transactions costs. Most importantly, we show that much of the first
order profitability enhancements come from the choice of the clustering algorithm, above and beyond the
specific choice of the trading strategy: after accounting for transactions costs and utilizing all the features
studied in this paper, the best of the distance clustering method yields an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.187;
the best of the single-view clustering method yields 0.495; and the best the multi-view clustering method
yields 0.830. Downside risks are an omnipresent threat to statistical arbitrage strategies (e.g. see [Khandani
and Lo, 2008] for an account of statistical arbitrage strategies during the 2007–2008 financial crisis). The
benchmark distance method suffers a maximum drawdown of 18.8% over its daily excess return series. In
contrast, the aforementioned best of our multi-view clustering strategy suffers a maximum drawdown of 4.6%
over its daily excess return series. Moreover, we find striking robustness in our multi-view clustering based
strategies: as compared to features set of the best strategy, slight perturbations to the intraday features
set do not materially alter the Sharpe ratios, max drawdowns and other performance measures. We arrive
at the same qualitative conclusions for the ETFs, futures and forex asset classes. In all, our results show
that multi-view clustering algorithms applied on a plethora of realized estimates based on intraday data can

Literature review
Key studies of statistical arbitrage and pairs trading include [Vidyamurthy, 2004], [Gatev et al., 2006],
[Avellaneda and Lee, 2010], [Do and Faff, 2010], [Jacobs and Weber, 2015], [Rad et al., 2016], [Stübinger et al.,
2018], and many others. [Krauss, 2017] is a recent comprehensive survey on the statistical arbitrage literature.
[Brunetti and De Luca, 2023] comparatively studies how different metrics for measuring the similarity of
between pairs of stocks affect the performance of a cointegration strategy. [Eroğlu et al., 2023] is a recent
study that computes the cointegration relationship between the wavelet transform of pairs of assets, and the
authors find that the wavelet transform removes common noises between the assets and thereby the obtained
cointegration relationship is more stable and hence profitable in opening trading pairs. [Krauss et al., 2017] is
an interesting recent study that directly applies neural networks and other machine learning methods for
statistical arbitrage; in particular, unlike the prevailing statistical paradigm of first identifying clusters and
then monitoring for price deviations, their machine learning approach to statistical arbitrage effectively rolls
both of these steps into one.
Clustering is the backbone to our statistical arbitrage methodology. A detailed review of these clustering
algorithms will be given in Section 3. There are some contemporary papers that apply clustering methods to
find pairs of assets for statistical arbitrage. [Han et al., 2023] uses unsupervised machine learning algorithms
along with the low-frequency characteristics of stocks from [Green et al., 2017] for generating clusters of similar
assets. [Jin et al., 2023] is a recent study that finds clusters using the correlations of CAPM idiosyncratic
risks of various stocks by applying the method of [Cucuringu et al., 2019]. Both of these papers are similar in
spirit to this paper in that they are also using clustering methods to find pairs. This paper differs from these
papers in significant ways. [Han et al., 2023] focuses on a monthly rebalancing strategy. [Jin et al., 2023]
focuses on a fixed three-day rebalancing frequency but their trade opening and closing criterion is only based
on the under- or over-performance of stock returns compared to its mean return over the past five days. Our
paper explores a plethora of opening and closing conditions, and also our trading conditions are completely
endogenous and are not based on a predetermined rebalancing period. Most importantly, we introduce and
study whether intraday realized estimators could be useful in finding good clusters of assets. Finally, we also
study the novel multi-view aspect of our clustering methodology, while these two papers focus exclusively on
the single-view clustering paradigm.
We extensively use realized estimators constructed out of intraday data. These estimators are rooted in
the literature of high-frequency financial econometrics, and key references here include [Andersen et al., 2001],
[Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard, 2002], [Andersen et al., 2003], and many others. Comprehensive surveys
of this literature include [McAleer and Medeiros, 2008] and [Bauwens et al., 2012]. It should be noted that
while we do use intraday to construct these realized estimators, our paper does not study intraday statistical
arbitrage like in [Liu et al., 2017], [Stübinger and Bredthauer, 2017], or [Stübinger, 2019]. We use intraday
data to construct quantities that are then observable at a daily frequency. To the best of our knowledge, our
paper is the first to introduce realized estimation concepts from the financial econometrics literature into the
statistical arbitrage literature.

Methodology
There are two preparatory steps and three core steps to our methodology.
As is common in the statistical arbitrage literature, Section 3.1 shows our first preparatory step whereby
we break the data into formation and trading periods. Our second preparatory step in Section 3.2 defines
4

and computes the features of risky assets over the formation period; this section will motivate and introduce
various realized estimators that use high-frequency intraday data.
Thanks to the two preparatory steps, we have a set of features for each risky asset at the end of the
formation period. Section 3.3 is our first core step where we group assets into distinct clusters based
on the similarity of their features; this section will revisit the classical distance clustering method, and
introduce several single-view and multi-view clustering algorithms. After grouping assets into distinct clusters,
Section 3.4 is our second core step where we aggregate the information of these clusters into various signals.
The third and final core step is Section 3.5 where we apply these signals to determine our trade opening and
trade closing conditions. See Table 1 for an overall methodology summary. In all, a strategy in this paper is
the combination of four elements: (a) choice of the features set; (b) choice of the clustering algorithm; (c)
choice of the signal; and (d) choice of the trading rule.
The remaining subsections discuss various details to our methodology. Section 3.6 shows how we aggregate
our individual trades into an overall portfolio. Finally, Section 3.7 discusses our software tools and data
sources.
3.1
Formation and trading periods
Suppose there are N risky assets. We use letters i, j to identify assets. We use the formation period to both
cluster groups of similar assets together and construct signals for potential trading. From the clusters of
assets identified in the formation period, we use the subsequent trading period to open and close any potential
trades. Each formation period is 12 × 21 = 252 business days (i.e. assuming there are 21 business days each
month). The trading period is the 6 × 21 = 126 business days following the formation period. See Figure 1
for an illustration of the timeline of our formation and trading periods. The set of days in a given formation
period is denoted by Tform and its subsequent set of days in the trading period is denoted by Ttrade.

3.3.4
Cluster sizes
This paper only focuses on cluster sizes of K = 10 and K = 25. A critical hyperparameter in clustering
algorithms is the number of clusters. While there are some methods to determine the optimal number of
clusters in a single-view spectral clustering context (i.e. [Zelnik-Manor and Perona, 2004]), to the best of
our knowledge we do not know of any methods to determine the optimal number of clusters in a multi-view
context. The most informative view in this paper is always the normalized prices of assets. While it is feasible
to determine the number of optimal clusters based on the view of normalized prices alone, it is much less
clear whether this is the same number of optimal clusters when other views are fused together. We do not
take a stance on the optimality of the number of clusters in this paper, but rather view the two choices of
K ∈{10, 25} as a parameterization sensitivity check.
3.3.5
Distance method screening
Unlike the bivariate clusters from the distance clustering method of Section 3.3.1, a distinct characteristic of
multivariate clustering methods is that we can have clusters containing more than two assets. It is useful
distill down these multivariate clusters in order to apply (bivariate) pairs trading rules.
We apply the following procedure to distill multivariate clusters to bivariate clusters. Suppose after
single-view or multi-view clustering, we have clusters C1, . . . , CK of assets, where |C|k ≥2 for all k = 1, . . . , K.
For each cluster k and for all assets i, j ∈Ck, i̸ = j, compute their MSEi,j based on their normalized prices
again over the formation period. To maintain comparability with the bivariate distance clustering method
of Section 3.3.1, we wish to ultimately output 50 pairs. These 50 pairs should be chosen widely across the
clusters and not be chosen exclusively from a few select clusters. For any a ∈R, denote ⌈a⌉as the smallest
integer greater than or equal to a. Our rules are: (1) Within each cluster k, select ⌈50/K⌉number of pairs
that achieve the lowest MSE’s within its own cluster; and (2) Select 50 pairs of the lowest MSE’s from these
K × ⌈50/K⌉number of pairs. In all, this procedure distills the K number of clusters, each with varying
number of assets, to just 50 bivariate pairs. Most importantly, this procedure ensures that pairs are drawn
from each of the K clusters. See Figure 3 for an illustration.
[Figure 3 about here.]
Remark 1 (Number of pairs). Recall from Section 3.3.1 we choose to work with 50 pairs rather than 20 pairs
as is commonplace in the pairs trading literature. Our distance method screening procedure here makes clear
why choosing 50 pairs is a sensible choice. As we only work with cluster sizes of K ∈{10, 25}, choosing 50
pairs results in roughly ⌈50/10⌉= 5 pairs per cluster when K = 10 and roughly ⌈50/25⌉= 2 pairs per cluster
when K = 25. If we had chosen 20 pairs then we’d effectively be just choosing the lowest ⌈20/25⌉= 1 MSE
pair within each cluster when K = 25. Indeed, working with more pairs most likely biases down our overall
results as we then inevitably choose lower quality pairs in terms of their MSE fits.
3.4
Signals
With a cluster of assets, we now need to aggregate them to construct useful subsequent potential trading
signals.
3.4.1
Signals based on bivariate assets
The distance clustering method, the single-view clustering method with distance method screening, and the
multi-view clustering method with distance screening will result in 50 clusters and each cluster will have
exactly two assets. In this section we will simplify notation and label any two different assets as asset 1 and
asset 2.
10

Simple long-short
The simple long-short signal is effectively the classical method as advocated by [Gatev
et al., 2006]. We construct their undirected spread as spreadt = Pt,1 −Pt,2. We say spreadt as written is
“undirected” because we had arbitrarily chosen asset 1 to be in the “long position” and asset 2 to be in the
“short position”. We will fix the direction of trade after we construct an opening rule based on this undirected
spread signal.
Bivariate cointegration
[Vidyamurthy, 2004] provides a framework to apply cointegration to statistical
arbitrage pairs trading. Specifically, here we follow the recipe as provided by [Rad et al., 2016, Section 4.2]
and follow the [Engle and Granger, 1987] two-step procedure. Using the normalized prices in the formation
period, we run the linear regressions of Pt,1 on Pt,2, and Pt,2 on Pt,1 via ordinary least squares (OLS). We
compute the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test statistic of the two regressions, and pick the regression
that generates the lowest ADF test statistic. Suppose it is the first regression that generates the lowest ADF
test statistic. Then we construct the spread process as spreadt = Pt,1 −ˆβPt,2, where ˆβ is the OLS coefficient
estimate. Observe the ADF test statistic here is used as a mere heuristic to fix whether it is asset 1 or asset 2
that should have 1 or ˆβ loading onto its normalized price to construct spreadt; we do not use the ADF test
statistic itself to ascertain whether a cointegrating relationship exists between the two assets.
Bivariate copula
We follow a bivariate copula method studied by [Rad et al., 2016, Section 4.3]. First
obtain the daily returns of assets i = 1, 2 in the formation period. For each asset i, we apply maximum
likelihood to fit the returns individually over univariate distributions (i.e. Gaussian, logistic, generalized
logistic, and generalized extreme value). For a pair of assets and for a pair of their associated fitted marginal
distributions, we fit them through each of the copulas C (i.e. Gaussian, student-t, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank,
and Joe). We choose the best fitting marginal of asset 1, marginal of asset 2, and the copula ( ˆF1, ˆF2, ˆC) for
the two assets as the triple that achieves the highest AIC out of all the possible combinations. See [Nelsen,
2006] for an overview of copula theory. See the Supplemental Materials [Leung, 2024] for detailed descriptions
of the marginal distributions and the copulas.
3.4.2
Signals based on multivariate assets
Once again, a distinct feature of our single-view and multi-view clustering algorithms is that each cluster
would naturally have (weakly) more than two assets. Hence it is natural to consider trading strategies that
trade strictly more than a pair of assets.
Multivariate cointegration
A natural multivariate trading strategy is the multivariate extension to the
bivariate cointegration method. Standard references on vector cointegration include [Engle and Granger,
1987], [Engle and Granger, 1991] and [Maddala and Kim, 1998]. Fix assets i = 1, 2, . . . , n := |Ck| in a
cluster Ck. Obtain the daily normalized prices of these assets over the formation period and stack them as
P t := [Pt,1, . . . , Pt,n]⊤.
Following the standard estimation procedures of [Johansen, 1988, Johansen, 1991] estimation method,
we estimate the associated vector error correction model (VECM) with one lag, no trend and no constant.
Following [Aznar and Salvador, 2002], [Wang and Bessler, 2005] and others, we use the AIC to select the

entries of ˆβl would imply excessively large short or levered positions in the trade. To this end, we choose to
normalize the cointegrating vector by its Euclidean norm except for entry with the value 1. 6 Furthermore, it
is inconvenient for the subsequent trading step to work with r different possible βl’s (although statistically

4.1
Overall portfolio excess returns performance
Let’s begin with a simple benchmark. Table 5 shows the employed capital portfolio excess returns performance
of the distance clustering method applied on just the normalized price index P feature, but is studied across
all signals, trading methods and transactions costs assumptions. Just by the P feature alone, it is already
evident that our portfolio performance results will be influenced by the signal choice and the trading rule.
For instance, using the simple long-short signal with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck thresholds trading we can achieve
an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.851 without transactions costs (but drops to 0.536 with transactions costs).
Yet if we keep the long-short signal but change to the classical two standard deviation trading rule of [Gatev
et al., 2006], the portfolio Sharpe ratio drops to 0.571 without transactions costs (and even further to 0.338
with transactions costs). This will be a recurrent theme throughout the remainder of our results discussions:
it is not surprising that the inclusion of transactions costs will lower portfolio performance; however, the
choice of the features, signal and the trading rule will make substantial impacts on portfolio performances, on
top of the choice of the clustering method.
[Table 5 about here.]
Our 0.571 annualized Sharpe ratio value, using the [Gatev et al., 2006] distance method and applied on
the S&P 500 equities universe, is lower than what has been reported on the literature. However, this could
largely be attributed to differences in the universe choice, the time sample under study, and our choice of
studying 50 pairs. Most notably, [Rad et al., 2016] (see their Figure 8) shows the mean excess returns of the
distance method with 20 pairs over the CRSP universe peaked in the early 1970s (about 1.80% per month
or 21.6% per annum before transactions costs) and has dropped dramatically by the 2010’s (about 0.2%
per month or 2.40% per annum, which is in line with our Table 5). Also we should keep in mind that our
universe of S&P 500 equities is substantially more liquid than the average stock in the CRSP universe and
hence our results are unlikely to be driven by any liquidity premia. It should also be noted that [Rad et al.,
2016] also does not find their results to be driven by the liquidity premia either.
Let’s investigate the rest of the strategies. Firstly, in the Supplemental Results [Leung, 2024], we show
that the multivariate cointegration signal contributes to the most extreme adverse results in our paper. We
find: (i) naively applying the distance method to multivariate clusters do not seem to generate any positive
results (and indeed, seems to generate some of the worst performances); and (ii) although the introduction of
multivariate clustering methods seems to invite the enticing possibility of constructing multivariate signals
and hence exploiting statistical arbitrage opportunities involving multivariate assets, this alluring conjecture
16

(let alone transactions costs) does not seem to hold true. In light of these discussion and for brevity, for the
remainder of this section we will drop the multivariate cointegration signal unless specified otherwise. In
particular, this means all subsequent discussions will focus exclusively on pairs trading on two assets, as we
will no longer discuss trading on multivariate assets.

Table 6 is a key summarizing result of this paper. This table shows although there might be some
heterogeneity in portfolio performance when taking into account the exact features, signal and trading method
choices, it is quite clear that multi-view clustering methods rank the highest in terms of delivering portfolio
performance results. Moreover, compared to the baseline results distance method on just the normalized
price index feature from Table 5, our multi-view clustering methods prevent large drawdowns. For instance,
after transactions costs, the best performing strategy from Table 5 is to apply the simple long-short signal
and trade with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck thresholds, which yields an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.536 but suffers
a maximum drawdown of 13.7% at the daily frequency. In comparison, the best performing strategy from

We should also note the remarkable robustness of multi-view clustering to perturbations of the features
set. From Table 6, we clearly see the best Sharpe ratio strategies are associated with the bivariate long-short
signal along with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck trading rule. Again, the best strategy deploys the MV-CRSp(25)
algorithm on the All features to achieve a Sharpe ratio of 0.830. Yet the other top strategies are also all
based on multi-view clustering applied on various features subsets, and they also achieve Sharpe ratios in the
0.70–0.80 range. Although this paper does not aim to identify the “best” combination of features, we see the
choice of the clustering algorithm has a dominant effect on the strategy performance more than the specific
choices of signals or trading rules.
We have discussed at length the Sharpe ratios and the maximum drawdowns of various strategies. However,
as known in the literature, different metrics exhibit different measures of risks and it is prudent to further
investigate these metrics on our strategies. In the Supplemental Materials [Leung, 2024], we see the top ten
strategies sorted by their Sharpe ratios in general exhibit fairly similar quantities across the various risk
metrics (e.g. Omega, Sortino, VaR and others). Likewise and unsurprisingly, the bottom ten strategies sorted
by their Sharpe ratios exhibit fairly unfavorable risk metric quantities.

On the “All” features
Let’s focus on just the “All” features (recall Table 2) so that we can specifically discuss the effects of the

the choice of features as fixed. There is a clear ranking: the distance method is subpar to the single-view
clustering method, which is then subpar to the multi-view clustering method. Indeed, the best of the distance
method yields an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.187 (maximum drawdown of 14.3% over the daily excess returns

signals and trading methods are present in this table. It is quite evident the choice of the clustering method
has a bigger effect on the portfolio performance than either the signal choice or the trading method themselves.
For instance, across various signals and trading rules, the tenth best multi-view clustering case has a Sharpe
ratio of 0.611, which is still better than the best single-view clustering case that has a Sharpe ratio of 0.495.

5
Conclusion
We offer a revised answer to the question posed by [Do and Faff, 2010]: “Does Simple Pairs Trading Still
Work?” Our conclusion is negative. Since the 2000s, we have not observed statistical arbitrage profits across
S&P 500 equities, exchange-traded funds, futures contracts, and foreign exchange asset classes when using
19

the classical benchmark of [Gatev et al., 2006], which applies the distance clustering method combined with
the two-standard-deviation trading rule.
Instead, we positively answer the refined question: “Does Pairs Trading with Improved Assets Clustering
Methods Work?”. This paper makes two key contributions: (1) We incorporate realized estimators based
on high-frequency intraday data into the daily feature set that characterizes an asset; and (2) We examine
the effectiveness of applying single-view and multi-view clustering algorithms on this expanded feature
set to identify assets for potential statistical arbitrage trading. By utilizing multi-view clustering on both
closing prices and daily realized estimates, we uncover statistically and economically significant risk-adjusted
arbitrage profits across all major asset classes. In particular, by expanding the feature set and applying
clustering algorithms, we identify significant arbitrage opportunities where the benchmark “simple pairs
trading” method falls short. Indeed, since distance-based and single-view clustering methods inherently lose
spatial and temporal feature information, it is expected that multi-view clustering methods would produce
more effective clusters, leading to superior statistical arbitrage trading performance.
We believe there are several fruitful avenues for future research: (i) Consider more sophisticated multi-view
clustering algorithms. This paper has mostly focused on single-view and multi-view spectral clustering, which
are core staples in the clustering literature. Recent developments in multi-view deep clustering could be an
interesting direction (see [Ren et al., 2024]); (ii) Fine tune the number of clusters. It seems like a robust
method to find the optimal number of clusters in clustering algorithms remains challenging, although some
advances have been made on this important topic; see [Xu and Tian, 2015] and [Oyewole and Thopil, 2023]
for recent surveys. (iii) Expand the number of asset names, especially for equities. Due to our intraday data
availability, we have restricted our equities universe to just the S&P 500 constituents. It would be instructive
to extend our analysis to include smaller US equities; and (iv) Curate and expand the set of daily features.
To emphasize the realized estimators in this paper, we deliberately do not include low frequency fundamentals
into the features set. It would be interesting expand our work to include these slower moving fundamentals
(e.g. sector information for equities, and see the set of fundamentals by [Green et al., 2017]).

Table 10: Converged and unconverged trades for the selected strategies accounting for transactions costs of Table 8 over 2000–2022. “Conv”
classifies for both converged and unconverged trades (All), convergent only trades (C) or unconvergent trades (U). A trade is “convergent” if it
opens and closes endogenously by the prescribed trading rule, and it is “unconvergent” a trade is forcibly closed because the trade still remains
open by the end of the trading period. “%Trades” shows the proportion of trades that either of the convergent or unconvergent type. “Per-trade
average daily PnL” is the average of the PnL across all active trades for each day; we compute the time series mean and standard deviation (in
basis points), IR is the mean divided by the standard deviation (no annualization), and skew is the skewness over the time series. “Win” is the
percentage of trades that have a strictly positive cumulative PnL at the end of its trade length. “Trade length” is the number of business days
for a trade. “PnL per trade” is the cumulative PnL for each trade.
