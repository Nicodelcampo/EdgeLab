High-frequency trading and price efficiency:
a unified model
Abstract
I study the impact of high-frequency trading (HFT) on price efficiency, taking into account both
the asset demand from HFT itself and the change it induces in the demand from informed traders.
HFT trades against demand shocks when demand shocks typically originate from noise trading.
Conversely, HFT trades with demand shocks when demand shocks typically originate from a change
in fundamentals. This pattern enhances price efficiency on average, holding the behavior of informed
traders constant. However, HFT lowers the expected profit of informed traders, who may react either
by withdrawing from the market or by using order randomization to mislead HFT. Such responses
lower the weight of fundamentals in global price variance, which decreases price efficiency. Overall,
the impact of HFT on price efficiency is ambiguous. The model accommodates the bulk of the
findings of the now mature HFT literature, even those that appear contradictory at first glance.
Keywords:
High-frequency Trading, Price Efficiency, Randomization, Information treatment,
Information content.
JEL classification: G12, G14, G23.

1. Introduction
Consider a patient who suffers from insomnia, and is prescribed sleeping pills. Intuitively, the
pills should improve the sleep quality of the patient, as they typically end each insomnia episode.
Nevertheless, in the long-run, sleeping pills typically increase the number of insomnia episodes as
the patient’s body becomes less able to generate melatonin on its own. If this long-run reaction is
sufficiently strong, the costs of more insomnia episodes outweigh the benefits more sleep conditional
on an insomnia episode. Overall the impact of taking sleeping pills on sleep quality is unclear.
A sizable academic literature1 suggests that a similar trade-off arises in the relationship between high-frequency trading and price efficiency. On the one hand, HFT speeds up the process
of incorporation of relevant information into prices. On the other hand, HFT diverts profit away
from informed investors, which lowers their incentive to obtain and disseminate information. HFT
enhances price efficiency only if the benefits of a better treatment of existing information outweighs
the cost of a long-term decrease in the information content of the market.
Objective. This paper assesses the overall desirability of HFT for price efficiency, taking into
account both its impact on information treatment and its impact on information content. I provide
a model in which a representative high-frequency trader (HFTr) interacts with a representative
informed trader (ITr) and a representative liquidity trader (LTr), in a market for a single asset. HFTr
i) observes the order flow from LTr and ITr, ii) uses this information to estimate the fundamental
value of the asset, and iii) trades before other participants can trade again. I study the impact
of this trading rule on the level of price efficiency of the market, first holding the behavior of ITr
constant, and second allowing ITr to respond to HFTr through exit or order randomization.
Findings. The trading pattern of HFTr crucially depends on the ratio ρ of the variance of noise
trading, defined as the sum of the demand from LTr and a random component in the demand
from ITr, over the variance of fundamental trading, defined as the component in the demand from
ITr that reflects fundamental information. When ρ is above a threshold ρ∗, unexpected demand
shocks typically drive the price away from its fundamental value. In this market prices revert on
average at subsequent periods, and HFTr trades against any demand shock it observes to benefit
from this reversion, a strategy referred to as market making. By contrast, when ρ is below ρ∗,
unexpected demand shocks typically drive the price towards its fundamental value. In this market
the price is positively autocorrelated on average, and HFTr trades with any demand shocks it
observes to benefit from this movement, a strategy referred to as backrunning. Market making
leads HFTr to downplay fundamental shocks by trading against them, while backrunning leads
HFTr to exaggerate meaningless noise shocks by trading with them. However the HFTr trading
rule is correct on average, and consequently HFTr enhances the process of shock incorporation into
prices. This is the information treatment effect.
Maintaining constant the flow of new information being generated by ITr, better information
treatment makes HFTr desirable from a price efficiency perspective. However, because the increase in

price efficiency spurred by HFTr lowers the utility of ITr, ITr reacts to HFTr over certain parameter
sets. This reaction may take two forms. First, ITr can exit the market if the cost of acquiring
information exceeds the benefits of exploiting mispricings. Second, ITr can randomize its orders,
thereby reducing the expected utility of HFTr when HFTr backruns. For ITr, randomization entails
a utility cost stemming from the risk and transaction costs it involves, but ITr utility shoots up
if randomization successfully drives HFTr out of market. Both exit and randomization imply that
the share of fundamental trading over noise trading falls, decreasing price efficiency. This is the
information content effect.
For both ITr exit and randomization the information content effect
dominates the information treatment effect: the level of mispricing when HFTr threatens to enter
is higher than it would be if HFTr never existed. Nevertheless, randomization constitutes a second
best equilibrium in parameter sets for which ITr would have exited if randomization was not an
option.
Method. The model is built on two blocks. The first block solves for equilibrium prices and
utilities in the market in which both HFTr and ITr feature. This is done by using rational expectations to pinpoint how HFTr extract fundamental information from the order flow. The second
block considers the strategic game played by HFTr and ITr before trading starts. The game uses
a Stackelberg (1934) setup in which ITr moves first knowing the best response of HFTr. ITr must
decide between three strategies: participating without randomization, participating with randomization, and exiting. The model is solved recursively: the expected utilities in the market with both
HFTr and ITr, obtained from the first block, are fed into the game. The final impact of HFTr is
given by the equilibrium level of price efficiency defined by rational expectations, in the equilibrium
market structure defined by the strategic game.
Literature.
The model’s study of HFTr holding ITr behavior constant relates closely to an
extensive empirical literature that studies HFT within a given market whose characteristics remain
constant during the sample period. This literature has produced the following results. HFT may
trade with or against the order flow shocks it observes (van Kervel and Menkveld (2019), Korajczyk
and Murphy (2019)) depending on the structure of the market it operates in. This pattern generally
enhances price efficiency (Brogaard et al. (2014), Carrion (2013)), perhaps at the expense of some
excess volatility (Bershova and Rakhlin (2013), Boehmer et al. (2021)), and perhaps not (Hasbrouck
and Saar (2013), Chaboud et al. (2014)). HFT generally provides liquidity (Hendershott, et al.
(2011), Frino et al. (2014)) but sometimes switches to liquidity taking (Kervel (2015)). Finally,
HFT diverts profits away from informed investors (e.g., Hirschey (2021), Tong (2015)) and from the
market marker (e.g., van Kervel (2015), Biais et al. (2015)).
The model’s study of the response of ITr to HFTr in terms of participation relates to a broad body
of literature pioneered by Grossman and Stiglitz (1980), whose general message is that information
acquisition decreases when those who invest in information are not those rewarded for it. Specifically,
several recent empirical papers report a significantly negative link between HFT and information
acquisition (Lee and Watts (2021), Gider et al. (2021)), in line with the theoretical contributions
of Baldauf and Mollner (2020) and Weller (2018). Less work has been undertaken on the issue

of the strategies used by ITr to dissimulate information from HFTr. Van Kervel and Menkveld
(2019, vKM henceforth) and O’Hara et al. (2014) show that institutional investors seek to avoid
detection by HFT, by slowing down the pace of their position taking or by using odd lots. Hoffman
(2014) provides a theoretical model in which “slow traders” trade less in the presence of HFT. To
the best of my knowledge, only one paper (Yang and Zhu (2020), YZ henceforth) explicitly studies
randomization. In YZ, the decision to randomize orders by “fundamental investors” depends on the
number of high-frequency traders, as well as the quality of their order flow information.
Both vKM and YZ are the closest in terms of approach to this study. Similarity with vKM is
derived from following a similar methodological path: firstly, characterizing the demand of HFT,
secondly, examining the way this demand impacts informed traders, and finally investigating how
informed traders respond to HFT. In this way this paper can be seen as a theoretical basis for
vKM’s empirical evidence. In the case of YZ, similarity comes from the focus on randomization
as a response to HFT. The present study differs because it provides a general model in which
HFTr may trade either with or against ITr and in a full dynamic mode.2 Furthermore, YZ focus
primarily on the profits of fundamental traders relative to HFT, whereas I focus on the desirability
of randomization from a price efficiency perspective.
In terms of modeling, this study is linked to theoretical papers in which agents seek to extract
information from the order flow (see Kyle (1985), Glosten and Milgrom (1985), or Muth (1960)
in a more general setting). At the heart of these models is the statistical problem of estimating
the permanent component of a variable that is subjected to both permanent shocks and transitory
white noise shocks. In a linear setup the optimal rule can be derived analytically, which in my case
allows for the obtainment of closed forms on different indicators of market quality such as investor
utilities, market depth, or price efficiency. Several theoretical models have recently added HFT to
these classic models for different purposes (e.g., Foucault et al. (2016), Budish et al. (2015)).
Contributions. This paper makes two notable contributions. The first is to provide a theoretical
framework that accommodates the bulk of the findings in the HFT literature. In effect the paper
can be seen as a model-based literature review.
In particular, the results of the model on the
market impact of HFT when the flow of information is held constant match well the results of the
empirical literature on the market impact of HFT within a fixed market structure. Findings that
are consensual in this literature –such as the impact of HFT on the utility of informed traders
or price efficiency– correspond to the results that do not depend on the value of the parameter
set in the model. By contrast, issues over which this literature is divided – such as the short-term
impact of HFT on excess volatility or liquidity– correspond to results that depend on whether HFTr
acts as a backrunner or as a market maker, which itself depends on the ratio ρ of the variance of

The second contribution of this paper is to make a set of new predictions. Some of these are
concerned with the way HFTr treats information, such as the prediction that fundamental shocks
should be downplayed in markets dominated by noise trading.
However the most novel results
of the model relate to the responses of ITr to HFTr. They can be summarized as follows. Exit
and randomization both unambiguously reduce price efficiency relative to an absence of response
to HFTr by ITr. However, randomization is an attractive second-best if it prevents ITr exit. The
appearance of randomization depends on its efficiency in reducing HFTr utility, its cost for ITr,
and the utility gain for ITr of driving HFTr out. Driving HFTr out of the market is easier when
noise trading is high, because noise trading decreases the utility of backrunning. The region of the
parameter set over which randomization prevents ITr from exiting is smaller than the region over
which it prevents ITr from staying without randomizing.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 presents the framework. Section 3 characterizes the
inference problem and trading rule of HFTr.
Section 4 discusses the impact of HFTr on price
efficiency and ITr utility when ITr behavior is held constant. Section 5 studies the participation
and randomization response of ITr to HFTr, and what this response implies for price efficiency.
Section 6 concludes. A glossary of all the abbreviations and notation used in the paper follows the
conclusion.

3.2.2. Link to literature
Distinguishing strategies that trade against other investors (against the wind strategies) from
strategies that trade with other investors (with the wind strategies) has been an important topic
in the HFT literature. Against the wind trading is close to market-making, and has notably been
considered as such by Grossman and Miller (1988). With-the-wind trading can reflect different
strategies (Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2005)), but the way HFTr behaves in this model is particularly close to backrunning, which involves using publicly available data on past order flow from
informed traders to estimate fundamentals (YZ).
Several theoretical models consider both market making and backrunning by HFT (e.g., Foucault
et al. (2016), Baldauf and Mollner (2020)). However to my knowledge this paper is the first to
define the conditions under which HFT will switch between both rules. The role of ρ in the model is
consistent with several empirical contributions (e.g., Korajczyk and Murphy (2019) vKM). vKM in
particular demonstrates that high-frequency traders usually trade against the wind, but can trade
with the wind if “the information component dominates the transitory component in [investors]
order’s overall price impact”. vKM also report evidence that investors need some time (around
seven hour) to comprehend to true nature of a shock, which is conceptually close to the learning
process presented in section 3.1.6

3.3.2. Link to literature

part. Lemma 1 also relates to casual observation of the recent developments in the high-frequency
world. The efficiency of market makers increased in recent years in response to HFT (Pagnotta and
Philippon (2015)). Alternative trading venues such as dark pools developed. The fees for collocation
services rose. Finally, the profits of the HFT industry have been on a declining trend, particularly
those related to cross-venue arbitrage in which the race for stale orders occurs in its purest form
(Foucault et al. (2017)). This suggests that market makers were to an extent successful in hurting
high-frequency traders.

6. Conclusion
High-frequency trading is desirable from a price efficiency perspective only if ITr does not react
to it. If ITr randomizes its orders, the gains from HFTr are more than offset in equilibrium. If
ITr exits the market in response to HFT, information stops flowing in and the market fails. Which
option emerges in a given market crucially boils down to the characteristics of the market.

Preprint not peer reviewed

An implication of this paper is that regulation of HFT should be market and time dependent. If
high-frequency traders act primarily as market markers, and/or the cost of acquiring information is
low, the gains from HFT are likely to outweigh its costs. However, if high-frequency traders have a
tendency to backrun and the profits of informed traders are low, the regulator or the exchange may
consider implementing anti-HFT rules. This may include penalties on canceled orders, stochastic
execution time, or asymmetric speed bumps.
