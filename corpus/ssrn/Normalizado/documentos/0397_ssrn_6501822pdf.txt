ABSTRACT 
The relentless pursuit of alpha has driven the creation of several quantitative trading strategies, including pairs 
trading. The recent emergence of reinforcement learning frameworks, such as Q-learning, has led to the 
development of advanced statistical arbitrage models. This study presents an empirical investigation into the 
application of ensemble Q- learning, a reinforcement learning method, to pairs trading in the Indian equity 
market. Utilising hourly data from the NIFTY 50 index constituents, which covers over 128,400 data points, a 
novel composite scoring framework was devised to select optimal asset pairs, balancing both long-term 
cointegration and mean-reversion criteria. A custom trading environment was constructed using OpenAI’s gym 
library to realistically simulate capital constraints, transaction costs, and position management. The agent 
maintained multiple independently trained Q-tables, aggregating their estimates to reduce variance and improve 
stability in trading decisions. Hyperparameters for the agent were optimised via a Bayesian search, and strategy 
performance was evaluated on out-of-sample data across multiple independent runs. Results demonstrate that 
the ensemble Q-learning agent delivered an average six-month portfolio return of 105.2% with a Sharpe ratio of 
2.08, substantially outperforming both traditional pairs trading benchmarks and major global indices. These 
findings provide rigorous evidence that reinforcement learning, when coupled with well-designed pairs trading 
strategies, can drive superior alpha generation in financial markets. 
Keywords: Pairs Trading, Reinforcement Learning, Q-Learning, Ensemble Learning, Statistical Arbitrage, 
Quantitative Trading, Quantitative Research, Mean Reversion, Cointegration, NIFTY 50 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
Contemporary financial markets are characterised by intense competition, rapid technological innovation, and 
the constant pursuit of alpha using strategies that can yield consistent, risk-adjusted returns in the presence of 
uncertainty and noise. Within this context, statistical arbitrage has emerged as a cornerstone of quantitative 
finance, leveraging mathematical and statistical models to exploit temporary mispricing between related assets. 
Among the various statistical arbitrage processes, pairs trading stands out for violating the efficient market 
hypothesis through its market- neutral approach and empirical robustness [Gatev et al., 2006]. 
Statistical arbitrage, often abbreviated as ‘stat arb’, is predicated on the notion that financial markets, while 
generally efficient, are prone to transient deviations from equilibrium due to liquidity shocks, behavioural biases, 
information lags, or structural changes [Avellaneda & Lee, 2010]. Rather than seeking absolute price 
discrepancies, statistical arbitrageurs focus on the relative value between two or more assets, constructing 
market-neutral portfolios to hedge against broad market movements [Vidyamurthy, 2004]. The central aim is to 
identify and trade on mean-reverting relationships, instances where the price spread between two assets 
diverges from its long-term mean and is expected to revert, thereby generating profit when the convergence 
occurs. 
Pairs trading, first formalised in the 1980s by Nunzio Tartaglia’s team at Morgan Stanley and later popularised 
in academic literature [Gatev et al., 2006], is the predominant statistical arbitrage strategy. The approach 
involves selecting two securities, typically equities within the same industry or sector, that have exhibited strong 
historical co-movement. When the price relationship between these assets deviates significantly from its 
historical average, a pairs trader simultaneously takes a long position in the underperforming asset and a short 
position in the outperforming one, essentially betting that the spread will revert to its mean. The strategy is 
inherently market-neutral, as returns are generated from the relative movement between the two assets rather 
than from the direction of the overall market.

The theoretical foundation of pairs trading is the aforementioned principle of mean reversion. In contrast to the 
traditional random walk hypothesis, which posits that asset prices follow a memoryless stochastic process and 
that past movements have no predictive power, mean reversion suggests that prices or spreads between the 
prices of two assets tend to return to a historical average over time [Poterba & Summers, 1988]. Mathematically, 
if St denotes the spread between the prices of two securities at time t, mean reversion implies that 
𝐸[𝑆𝑡+𝑘 ∣ 𝑆𝑡] → 𝜇 𝑎𝑠 𝑘 → ∞ 
where 𝜇 is the historical mean of the spread. In pairs trading, this property is exploited by entering trades when 
the spread deviates by a statistically significant amount (often measured in standard deviations) from the mean, 
and exiting when it reverts. 
The process of implementing a pairs trading strategy can be delineated into several key steps: 
1. Pair Identification: The first and perhaps most critical step is to identify pairs of assets that exhibit a strong 
and stable statistical relationship. This relationship can be measured in several ways, including correlation, 
cointegration, and distance metrics. The selection process must account for the possibility of structural 
breaks, which are permanent changes in underlying relationships due to events such as mergers, 
bankruptcies, or regulatory shifts. The risk that a divergence is not temporary but structural is the principal 
risk in pairs trading, and underscores the importance of robust pair selection and the need to monitor these 
pairs. 
2. Spread Construction and Normalisation: Once a pair is selected, the spread is constructed, typically as a 
linear combination of the two price series. To facilitate signal generation, spreads are often normalised using 
a z- score transformation: 
𝑧𝑡 = (𝑥 − 𝜇) / 𝜎 
where St is the spread at time t, 𝜇 is the mean, and 𝜎 is the standard deviation of the spread over a rolling 
window. Traditionally, and even now, this normalisation allows the trader to interpret deviations in terms 
of standard deviations, providing a consistent basis for entry and exit signals [Gatev et al., 2006; 
Vidyamurthy, 2004]. 
3. Signal Generation: Trading signals are typically generated when the z-score of the spread crosses pre-defined 
thresholds. For example, a common rule is to enter a trade when the z-score exceeds ±2 (indicating a 
significant divergence) and to exit when it reverts back to 0 (the mean). Several frameworks and methods 
have been developed over time to optimise these thresholds, enabling the creation of a precise, rule-based 
pairs trading strategy. 
4. Risk Management: Effective risk management is essential not only in the long-term viability of pairs trading 
strategies, but in any investment strategy. This includes position sizing, stop-loss rules, maximum drawdown 
constraints, and explicit modelling of transaction costs. The goal is to ensure that the strategy remains 
efficient in the face of adverse market conditions and avoids catastrophic losses due to rare but extreme 
events. The risk-adjusted performance of a portfolio is often characterised by the Sharpe ratio, a metric

which calculates the excess return an investment provides for each unit of risk taken. A higher Sharpe ratio

The pair selection process is particularly nuanced and has been the subject of extensive research. Three principal 
approaches are commonly used: 
● Distance Approach: Popularised by Gatev et al. (2006), this method identifies pairs by minimising the 
sum of squared deviations between the spreads of normalised price series over a historical window. If 
PA(t) and PB(t) are the normalised prices of stocks A and B at time t, the distance is computed as: 
SSD = ∑ (from t=1 to t) (PA(t) − PB(t))² 
 
Pairs with the smallest SSD are selected for pairs trading, as they have the most similar historical price 
trajectories. 
● 
Correlation Approach: This approach selects pairs with the highest Pearson correlation coefficients over the 
formation period. Typically, a correlation coefficient of 0.80 or higher is considered a strong positive 
correlation, establishing a more definable statistical relationship. While easy to compute, correlation does 
not guarantee mean reversion, as it only measures linear co-movement and can be inflated by spurious 
relationships. 
● 
Cointegration Approach: Cointegration analysis, introduced by Engle and Granger, addresses the limitations 
of correlation by testing for the existence of a stationary linear combination between two non-stationary 
price series. If Xt and Yt are the log prices of two assets, they are cointegrated if there exists a coefficient β 
such that the residual Zt = Yt - βXt is stationary. It is essential to note that the price series of all equities are 
non-stationary (statistical properties such as mean and variance change over time). The Engle-Granger twostep method is an approach wherein, firstly, an OLS regression 
Yt = α + βXt+et 
is performed on the two securities, and then secondly, the residuals et are tested for stationarity using the 
Augmented Dicker Fuller (ADF) method. The ADF method tests for a unit root to evaluate the null hypothesis of 
non-stationarity [Engle & Granger, 1987]. Therefore, a lower p-value (the probability of obtaining results 
considering the null hypothesis is true), typically less than 0.05, makes a pair considered cointegrated and 
suitable for pairs trading. A lower p-value implies greater cointegration strength. The spread in cointegration- 
based pairs trading is often constructed as 
Spreadt = log(PA(t)) − βlog(PB(t)) 
Where β is estimated from the regression. The stationarity of the spread is crucial, as it highlights the mean- 
reverting property which the strategy exploits. 
Despite the rigour of these methods, traditional pairs trading strategies are typically rule-based, relying on fixed 
thresholds and static parameters. These approaches can be suboptimal in the face of the complex dynamics of 
financial markets. In response, recent years have witnessed the growing application of machine learning, 
particularly reinforcement learning, to the domain of statistical arbitrage [Deng et al., 2016; Yang & Malik, 2024; 
Carta et al., 2020]. 
Reinforcement learning is a paradigm wherein an agent learns to make sequential decisions by interacting with 
an environment, receiving feedback in the form of rewards or penalties. The agent’s objective is to learn a policy 
through mapping states and actions in order to maximise the cumulative expected reward over time [Sutton & 
Barto, 2018]. Reinforcement learning is formalised through the framework of a Markov Decision Process (MDP), 
defined by the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), where: 
● 
State (S): The representation of a given environment at a certain point in time. 
● 
Action (A): All possible decisions that the agent can take in any given state. 
● 
Transition Probability Function (P): 𝑃(𝑠′ ∣ 𝑠, 𝑎), or the probability of the agent moving to state 𝑠′ when action 
𝑎 has been taken. 
● 
Reward Function (R): 𝑅(𝑠, 𝑎, 𝑠′), or the feedback received upon transitioning from state 𝑠 to 𝑠′ due to action 
𝑎. It quantifies the benefit of the given action.

The Markov property stipulates that the next state depends only on the current state and action, and not on the

𝑃(𝑠𝑡 + 1 ∣ 𝑠𝑡, 𝑎𝑡, 𝑠𝑡 − 1, 𝑎𝑡 − 1, … ) = 𝑃(𝑠𝑡 + 1 ∣ 𝑠𝑡, 𝑎𝑡) 
In the context of pairs trading, the RL agent observes the current state (e.g. the z-score of the spread), selects an 
action (e.g. enter/exit/hold), receives a reward (e.g. profit/loss adjusted for transaction costs and risk), and 
transitions to a new state. Over time, the agent learns to optimise trading decisions in response to the evolving 
market environment. 
Recent studies have demonstrated that RL agents can adaptively learn optimal entry and exit points, 
outperforming traditional rule-based strategies in both equity and cryptocurrency markets [Yang & Malik, 2024; 
Liu et al., 2024; SSRN, 2023]. For example, Yang & Malik [2024] showed that RL-based pairs trading significantly 
outperformed both manual and traditional approaches in volatile cryptocurrency markets, achieving annualised 
profits as high as 31.53% compared to 8.33% for traditional methods. Similarly, deep reinforcement learning 
(DRL) techniques, such as DQN and hybrid RL networks, have been shown to yield superior performance and 
risk-adjusted returns in various stock and bond market settings [Liu et al., 2024; MDPI, 2022]. 
Q-learning is a widely used, model-free RL algorithm that estimates the optimal action-value function Q*(s, a) 
representing the maximum expected cumulative reward achievable from state s by taking action a and following 
the optimal policy thereafter [Watkins & Dayan, 1992]. The Q-table is updated iteratively using the Bellman 
equation: 
𝑄(𝑠𝑡, 𝑎𝑡) ← 𝑄(𝑠𝑡, 𝑎𝑡) + 𝛼[𝑟𝑡 + 𝛾𝑚𝑎𝑥𝑎′𝑄(𝑠𝑡 + 1, 𝑎′) − 𝑄(𝑠𝑡, 𝑎𝑡)] 
● 
rt is the immediate reward received 
● 
α ∈ (0,1] is the learning rate, which governs the weight of new information 
● 
γ ∈ [0,1] is the discount factor, which governs the weight of future rewards 
● 
𝑚𝑎𝑥𝑎′𝑄(𝑠𝑡 + 1, 𝑎′) represents the maximum expected future reward obtainable from the next state 
Through repeated interaction with the environment, the Q-table converges to the optimal value function, 
enabling the agent to select actions that maximise long-term returns. The epsilon-greedy policy is commonly 
used to balance exploration (random actions) and exploitation (informed, or greedy actions), with the 
exploration rate decaying over time to favour exploitation as learning progresses. The integration of Q-learning 
into pairs trading offers several advantages. The agent can adaptively learn optimal entry and exit points, 
dynamically adjust to changing market conditions, incorporate complex risk management objectives directly into 
the reward function, and more. 
Due to the inherent stochasticity of the learning process, single Q-learning agents often experience fluctuations 
in their learned policies and value estimates between runs, leading to unstable and inconsistent performance. 
Research has demonstrated that ensemble Q-learning, where multiple Q-networks or Q-tables are trained 
independently and their action-value estimates are aggregated, can enhance stability, reduce variance, and 
improve robustness in financial Reinforcement Learning (RL) applications. For instance, Averaged-DQN 
introduced by Anschel et al. (2017) and the Multi-DQN framework from Carta et al. (2021) both show that 
ensembling several Q-networks (typically ranging from four to sixteen) significantly reduces value estimation 
variance and stabilises learning, especially in noisy financial environments. In the context of a non-deterministic 
Q-learning model, ensemble learning is especially valuable for achieving stable results. 
The evolution of pairs trading from simple rule-based strategies to sophisticated, adaptive frameworks powered 
by reinforcement learning represents a significant advance in the field of quantitative finance.

a. Data Architecture and Preprocessing 
The empirical basis of this study is grounded in hourly frequency financial data from the constituents of the 
NIFTY 50 index, which offers a comprehensive representation of the Indian equity market by encompassing 
companies from key sectors with significant market capitalisation and liquidity. Hourly closing price data for all 
50 stocks were procured programmatically through the Yahoo Finance API, using the finance Python library. The 
dataset spans a 547-day window from 1 January 2024 to 1 July 2025, and includes over 128,400 data points. For 
modelling purposes, the dataset was divided into two distinct sets: a training window from 1 January 2024 to 1 
January 2025, and an out-of-sample evaluation window covering 1 January 2025 to 1 July 2025. Raw price data 
was rigorously preprocessed to ensure structural integrity. Missing values were addressed using a forward-fill 
followed by backward-fill to maintain continuity. All time series were aligned to a common hourly index, ensuring 
strict temporal synchronicity for spread and subsequent z-score calculations. 
a. Advanced Pair Selection Framework 
Given the pivotal impact of pair selection on the success of statistical arbitrage strategies, a robust and novel 
composite scoring approach was adopted to identify the most promising trading pairs from among the 1,225 
unique permutations available in the NIFTY 50 universe. The scoring mechanisms combine two metrics with 
equal weighting to capture both long-term cointegration and short-term mean reversion properties: 
1. Cointegration Strength (50% weight): For each asset pair, the Engle-Granger two-step methodology is 
applied on the raw price series to test for a statistically significant long-run equilibrium relationship. The 
first stage regresses one asset against the other; the second stage applies an Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) 
test on the residuals to determine stationarity. Implementation leverages the statsmodels Python library. 
2. Z-Score Mean-Crossing Frequency (50% weight): The raw price spread for each pair is calculated hourly, and 
its z-score normalisation is applied over the training window. The number of zero-crossings, which are 
instances when the z-score crosses the mean (0) is counted, with higher cross-frequency interpreted as 
stronger mean-reversion behaviour. 
Both cointegration and crossing metrics were individually normalised across all pairs and then averaged to form 
a composite pair score. The top five highest-scoring pairs were selected to comprise the trading portfolio used in 
subsequent model training and evaluation. The z-scores of the spreads for these prices were then computed and 
downloaded as a CSV file to be used for the state space of the agent.

c. Reinforcement Learning Agent Design 
A custom OpenAI Gym-compatible environment was designed to portray the dynamics of a realistic pairs trading 
framework. The environment encapsulates transaction costs, capital constraints, cooldown periods, forced entry 
and exit conditions, and penalises undesired behaviours such as overtrading and inactivity. 
In this framework, the state space is defined as a compact, information-rich encoding of recent market conditions 
and 
the agent’s current trading position: 
- 
Spread Z-Score History: The primary feature is a rolling window of the most recent three z-score values of 
the spread between each selected asset pair. 
- 
Discretisation: Each z-score is discretised into 15 bins, mapping the continuous range (empirically, [−5,5] to 
integer indices. 
- 
The agent’s trading position is encoded as one of three states. 
The full state is represented as a unique integer by combining the discretised z-score history and position 
encoding: 
state_index = ∑(from j = 0 to 2) [ bin(z_{t−2+j}) × 15^j ] × 3 + pos_idx 
Essentially, the system uses base 15 encoding (z-score multiplied by 15j) to provide a unique code for each zscore state. This scheme ensures that every possible combination of recent z-score history and trading position 
maps to a unique integer, which is necessary for indexing into the Q-table. This also makes sure the model is 
computationally efficient. Thus, the total number of states are 153 × 3 = 10,125. The agent operates within a 
discrete action space comprising six possible actions at each time step: 
1. Hold (do nothing) 
2. Enter Long-Short (long asset A, short asset B) 
3. Enter Short-Long (short asset A, long asset B) 
4. Enter Long-Short (alt) (alternative entry logic) 
5. Enter Short-Long (alt) 
6. Exit Position (close any open position) 
Positions may be opened only when the agent is not already engaged in a trade and the cooldown period (1 time 
step) since the last exit has elapsed. Trades are allowed under two conditions: (1) the magnitude of the current 
z-score exceeds a minimum threshold of 0.05 (to reduce unnecessary noise) or (2) the z-score crosses the mean, 
implying a transition through equilibrium. Stop-losses are loose, implemented as forced exits if the spread 
diverges excessively or if a trade is held for more than 60 steps without profit. Transaction costs are fixed at 
0.09% of capital per trade, set to reflect realistic conditions of the leading Indian brokerage platform Zerodha.

and penalise poor timing. At every environment step, the reward is constructed through multiple contextsensitive components. The full expression can be written as 
rt = -0.001 + rentry + rexit + rforced_exit + rgrowth 
where: 
● 
-0.001 is a constant time penalty applied at each step to discourage passive or inactive episodes. 
●

rentry = 0.5⋅∣ zt ∣ + 0.05

rgrowth = 0.05⋅((Ct - C0)/C0) where Ct is the current capital and C0 = 100,000 is the initial capital. 
d. Hyperparameter Optimisation via Bayesian Search 
Recognising the sensitivity of reinforcement learning performance to hyperparameter configurations, a Bayesian 
optimisation framework was employed using the optuna Python library. The search domain includes the learning 
rate (α), discount factor (γ), exploration rate (ϵ), and epsilon decay factor. A Gaussian Process serves as the 
surrogate model for the objective function, enabling informed sampling. The optimisation criterion was defined 
as the mean return minus 
two times the standard deviation to favour consistent performance over high volatility. The process ran for 60 
trials, with 10 runs per trial. The final values were slightly adjusted in accordance with current literature, 
resulting in: 
● 
Learning Rate (α): 0.015 
● 
Discount Factor (γ): 0.99

d. Out of Sample Evaluation 
Post-training, each learned agent is evaluated against out-of-sample z-scores spread series from 1 January 2025 
to 1 July 2025. For each pair, 10 independent training-evaluation cycles are run, each starting with fresh Q-table 
reinitialisation and re-training. During evaluation, the agent operates fully exploitatively, choosing greedily from 
the averaged Q-values of the ensemble. Performance metrics tracked included the total return, the Sharpe ratio 
adjusted for a risk-free rate of 6.79% and 853 trading periods, the total amount of trades with the model’s win 
rate (proportion of profitable trades), and the max drawdown value (largest peak-to-trough loss experienced). 
The capital series of each run is also recorded using the matplotlib Python library. Each metric is reported per 
run and aggregated across runs (mean ± standard deviation) for each pair. For visual clarity, capital over time is 
plotted with shaded bands denoting ±1 standard deviations across the runs. Additionally, capital trajectories 
across all pairs are aggregated into a composite portfolio-level performance plot. All metrics are logged and plots 
saved for reproducibility and granular post-analysis. 
III. 
MODELLING AND ANALYSIS 
The framework developed is a tabular ensemble multi-Q-learning agent trained via interaction with the custom 
environment. The agent leverages an ensemble approach to address the common issue of overestimation bias in 
conventional Q-learning. Specifically, an ensemble of n = 3 Q-tables, Q1, Q2, …, Qn is maintained. Increasing the 
value of n increases the stability and lowers the variance of the model, but also makes it computationally heavier. 
Each Q- table is instantiated with optimistic initialisation (value = 10.0) across all 10,125 observable states and 
six actions, encouraging early-stage exploration. During training, the agent selects actions via an epsilon-greedy 
policy. With probability epsilon, a random action is taken (exploration), and with probability 1 - epsilon, the 
action maximising the average Q-value across all ensemble members is selected (exploitation). Each table was 
independently trained for 50,000 episodes per pair using the respective training window data, in the same 
environment but with random seeds. At the start of each episode, the trading environment resets both capital 
and state variables. The agent alternates between exploration and exploitation based on the current epsilon 
value, which decays multiplicatively (according to the epsilon decay value) after each episode, systematically 
annealing exploration over time. After all Q-tables are trained, they are stacked into a NumPy array. The final, 
deployable Q-table used for policy decisions is obtained by computing the element-wise mean across all Q-tables 
in the ensemble.

It is essential to note that this study outperforms existing literature. Gatev et al. (2006) found average annualised 
excess returns of up to 11% for self-financing portfolios of U.S. equities using a distance-based pairs selection 
method over the period 1962–2002, after accounting for conservative transaction costs. Pizzutilo (2013) 
conducted an international study, constructing 10-pair portfolios that yielded a mean annual gross return of 
11.84%. Zhu (2024) replicated Gatev et al. (2006)’s methodology in modern markets, utilising a larger stock pool 
and accounting for changing risk factors, and noted that top-performing traditional strategies still yield a 
compounded annual excess return of 6.2%. Yang & Malik (2024) developed an RL-based pairs trading approach 
in cryptocurrency markets and reported annualised profits up to 31.53% for RL models. These results, while 
superior to many benchmarks, remain notably below the portfolio returns documented in the present study, 
which achieves a portfolio-level average return of 105.2% with an average Sharpe ratio of 2.08. This indicates a 
substantial excess return over global benchmarks, pointing towards the success of ensemble Q-learning in pairs 
trading, particularly in the Indian equity market. 
V. 
CONCLUSION 
In an era of unprecedented competition and technological advancements in strategies to apply in financial 
markets, this research demonstrates the empirical and practical advantages of applying ensemble Q-learning to 
pairs trading for statistical arbitrage methods. By combining rigorous data engineering, a novel composite metric 
for robust pair selection, and a carefully designed ensemble Q-learning framework, this research establishes a 
new benchmark for quantitative trading. It delivers a level of performance previously unseen in both Indian and 
international pairs trading research. Looking forward, this approach lays the groundwork for a new era of data- 
driven, reinforcement-learning-driven trading strategies, capable of thriving in the face of contemporary market 
dynamics.
